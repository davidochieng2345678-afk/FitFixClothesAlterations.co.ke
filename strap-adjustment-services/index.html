The invisible load

Why a strap is the most load-bearing thing on any garment or bag

Everything else on a garment is designed for comfort, drape or appearance. A strap is designed for one job: to carry weight without failing. A handbag strap takes the full weight of the bag and its contents. A pair of overalls is held up entirely by two straps over the shoulders. A dress hangs from its straps or falls to the floor. That means a strap alteration has a much higher bar than almost any other repair — because when a strap fails, whatever it was holding falls with it. Here's why that changes how the work should be done.

Straps fail at the attachment point — always

When a bag strap breaks, it doesn't break in the middle. It breaks where it joins the bag — the point where the strap is stitched, riveted or clipped to the D-ring or the loop. That's because the attachment point takes the entire concentrated load of whatever the strap is carrying, and it's the point with the least fabric or leather to distribute that load. On a genuine leather bag, the attachment tab is usually two layers of leather stitched together with a rivet through the centre. On a mass-produced bag, it's often a single layer of leather with glue and a stitch, which is why cheap bags fail at the attachment tab within a year. Every strap adjustment we do involves inspecting the attachment point first. If it's already compromised, we tell the client — because shortening the strap won't help if the attachment is the weak link.

The hardware determines what's actually possible

Most straps are adjustable — that's what the buckle, slider or D-ring is for. But the adjustable range has limits. A handbag strap with a slider gives you maybe 10 to 15 cm of adjustment. Shorten the strap beyond the slider's range and the strap stops being adjustable — it becomes a fixed-length strap. That's fine if the client wants a fixed strap. It's not fine if the client wants to keep the adjustability. This is the most common mistake we see in strap alterations done elsewhere: the tailor shortens the strap from the wrong end, passes the slider, and now the strap only fits one way. We always check the hardware first and shorten from the correct end to preserve the full adjustment range where the client wants it. On bags where the hardware is non-removable (riveted buckles, sealed D-rings), we sometimes have to work around the hardware entirely, which changes the technique and the price.

The strap has to match the item — width, colour, weave, and age

A strap is the most visible element on a bag or pair of overalls. It's the part everyone sees. If we're adding length — say, extending a bag strap that's too short — the new leather or fabric has to match the original in every way: width, colour, weave, grain, and age. Matching new leather to five-year-old leather is genuinely difficult, because the old leather has darkened and softened in a way the new leather hasn't. On a KSh 4,000 bag, this rarely matters — the client just wants the strap to work. On a KSh 40,000 designer bag, the match has to be invisible, and sometimes the honest answer is that we can't source a match and the strap should be replaced entirely with a new one made from scratch.

Shortening a strap is permanent — there's no un-shortening

You can lengthen a strap if it was cut with too much fabric left, but you can't un-shorten a strap after it's been cut. This means the measurement has to be right before the first cut. On a bag strap, the client should have the bag at the height they want it — either on their shoulder, in their hand, or across their body — and we measure from there. On overall straps, the client should have the overalls on and the straps adjusted to where they're comfortable. We never shorten from a photo alone, because the strap length depends on where the bag or garment actually sits on the body, and that varies enormously between clients of different heights. This is why our strap measurements take longer than the alteration itself.

Worn leather doesn't take a new stitch the way new leather does

Leather fatigues with age. Where the strap flexes — at the shoulder curve, at the buckle, at the attachment rivet — the leather dries out and softens. Once leather has reached that stage, pushing a new needle through it can cause the fibres to split along the grain, which means the new stitching line will fail within a few months. We check the flexibility of the leather before starting any strap repair. If the leather is tired, we may recommend hand-stitching rather than machine-stitching (which is gentler on aged leather), or we may recommend full strap replacement instead of a shortening job. It's the same principle as any other leather work — the older and drier the leather, the more careful the technique has to be.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 800 strap shortening on a KSh 15,000 leather bag saves the bag. A KSh 800 strap shortening on a KSh 1,500 nylon bag from a market stall usually doesn't — the bag will fail elsewhere within a year. We turn down strap jobs every month because the honest answer is that a new bag or a new overall makes more sense than the alteration. On the other hand, we've rescued bags that were worth ten times their original price to the person who owned them: a graduation gift, a family heirloom, a bag from a specific trip. Strap work is about preserving function, not about the price of the item. It's the same standard we hold on leather jacket alterations and fabric patching.

Not all straps are the same job

Three strap tiers — three completely different alterations

A nylon backpack strap and a genuine leather handbag strap require completely different handling. One uses webbing and plastic hardware; the other uses leather and metal fittings. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will fail. Here's how we categorise straps before deciding what technique to use.

Everyday tier

Nylon, webbing and synthetic straps

Backpack straps, gym bag straps, camera straps, adjustable webbing on bags and sports gear. Usually 2 to 4 cm wide, made from nylon webbing or polyester, with plastic hardware — sliders, buckles, adjusters. The straps that get shortened most often and the easiest to work with. Common on backpacks, sports bags, laptop bags and travel gear.

What we do differentlyWebbing is tough but it frays aggressively at a cut edge, so every cut is heat-sealed or overlocked before finishing. On backpack straps, we re-bar-tack the attachment point with a heavier stitch than the original, because a backpack carries more weight than a handbag and the strap has to hold. Plastic hardware is preserved unless it's failed — in which case we source a colour-matched replacement.

Everyday tier

Canvas, cotton and woven fabric straps

Tote bag handles, canvas overall straps, canvas camera straps, woven cotton bag handles. Usually 2.5 to 5 cm wide, made from cotton canvas, duck cloth or woven webbing. Attachment is usually a stitched fold at the bag or garment, sometimes reinforced with a rivet or a leather tab. Common on market bags, canvas totes, work overalls and gardening overalls.

What we do differentlyCanvas straps that have been worn heavily are often thin at the fold line, which is the first place they fail. When shortening a canvas strap, we strengthen the attachment fold with a second row of stitching — otherwise the strap will tear at the attachment point within a season. On overall straps, we also check the button or buckle hole for stretch before returning the item.

Premium tier

Leather and designer bag straps

Genuine leather bag straps, designer handbag straps, and leather overall straps. Usually 2 to 4 cm wide, made from full-grain or top-grain leather with metal hardware — buckles, D-rings, sliders, rivets. Attachment is often a fold of leather through a metal loop with a rivet and hand-stitching. Common on high-value bags, leather totes, designer crossbody bags, and heritage overalls.

What we do differentlyLeather strap work is done entirely by hand where possible — machine stitching on aged leather risks splitting the grain. We match the leather for colour, grain and thickness, and we hand-stitch with a saddle stitch that matches the original. On a designer strap with a specific colour of edge-paint, we re-paint the cut edge by hand after finishing. A careless alteration on a KSh 40,000 leather bag strap is a KSh 40,000 mistake.

If you're not sure which tier your strap falls into, send a close-up photo of the strap and one of the attachment point on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the material and the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five parts of a strap that affect the alteration

What a strap actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a strap as "the handle that goes over your shoulder." That description doesn't help anyone doing the work. Structurally, a strap has five parts, and each one changes how the alteration should be done. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a strap.

01

The strap body — the visible length

The strap body is the length of leather, webbing or fabric that runs from one end to the other. It's the part everyone sees and the part that most alterations shorten or lengthen. On a bag strap, the body is usually 60 to 130 cm long depending on style. On an overall strap, it's about 30 to 40 cm per side. On a dress strap, it can be anywhere from 5 cm (a short shoulder strap) to 40 cm (a halter strap). The strap body is the easy part to shorten. The hard part is what happens at the ends.

02

The attachment point — how it joins the item

The attachment point is where the strap joins the bag or garment. On a leather bag, it's usually a folded loop of leather passing through a D-ring or metal loop, stitched closed with a saddle stitch and often riveted. On an overall, it's a buttonhole stitched through the fabric or a metal button on the strap that slots into a buttonhole on the overall. On a dress, it's the point where the strap is stitched to the bodice. On a backpack, it's often a bar-tack reinforcement where the webbing loops through a plastic buckle. This is where straps fail first, and it's the part that most alterations don't touch — unless they should. When we lengthen a strap, the attachment point has to be re-anchored. When we shorten a strap, the attachment point often stays where it is.

03

The adjustable hardware — buckle, slider, D-ring

Most straps are adjustable through one of three mechanisms: a slider (a flat metal or plastic rectangle the strap threads through), a buckle (a frame with a prong that passes through a hole), or a D-ring (a loop the strap passes through and doubles back on itself). The hardware defines the adjustable range of the strap. If you shorten the strap beyond the end of that range, the strap stops being adjustable. If you lengthen the strap, the new fabric has to feed through the hardware the same way the original did. On designer bags where the hardware is a specific colour or finish, we preserve it and work around it — the hardware is often worth more than the strap itself.

04

The finished end — stitched, riveted or capped

The end of a strap is where the tailoring details matter. A leather strap end is usually finished three ways: cut straight and edge-painted, folded back and stitched, or capped with a metal tip. A webbing strap end is usually heat-sealed (to prevent fraying) or folded and bar-tacked. A cotton strap end is usually folded double and stitched through. When we shorten a strap, we always re-finish the cut end in the same style as the original — so a strap that had edge-painted leather ends still has edge-painted leather ends when we're done. Matching the finish is the difference between a strap that looks altered and one that looks factory-made.

05

The flex point — where the strap curves

Every strap has a flex point — the spot where it curves or bends every time the item is used. On a bag strap, that's the shoulder curve. On an overall strap, it's the point where it passes over the shoulder and folds down to the buttonhole. On a dress strap, it's the top of the shoulder. The flex point is where leather dries out and cracks, where webbing frays, and where fabric thins. When we alter a strap, we check the flex point first. If it's already compromised, a shortening job won't help — the strap needs to be replaced entirely, or the alteration has to move the flex point to a different part of the strap. This is the most commonly overlooked part of strap alteration, and it's usually why a strap alteration fails within a year.

If a tailor quotes a strap adjustment without asking about the hardware, the attachment point, or the flex point, they're about to shorten the wrong end of the strap. We'd rather ask three questions upfront than see you back in three months with a strap that's failed.

The material decides the technique

The six strap materials we work with most often — and how each one behaves

The material determines everything about a strap adjustment — the needle, the thread, the finish, the hardware compatibility, and how the strap will age after the alteration. Here's our honest breakdown by material, the same one we'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Nylon and polyester webbing

The toughest strap material there is, and the most common on bags that carry real weight — backpacks, gym bags, camera bags, laptop bags. Webbing is a woven material that resists abrasion well, but it frays at a cut edge if it isn't heat-sealed or overlocked. Every cut we make on webbing gets sealed with a hot blade or finished with an overlock stitch — otherwise the strap develops a fringe of loose threads at the cut, and eventually the weave starts to come apart. On webbing, we also re-bar-tack the attachment point more heavily than the factory did, because the client wouldn't be bringing it to us unless they wanted the strap to actually hold.

Genuine leather (full-grain and top-grain)

The material of every good handbag strap, and one of the trickiest materials to work with on a strap because it has to match perfectly. Leather straps are usually cut from a single continuous piece — no seams in the middle. That means a lengthening job requires adding a panel, which introduces a seam. Our matching process: colour (against the faded current tone, not the original), grain (smooth, pebbled, or natural markings), thickness, and flexibility. On aged leather, we hand-stitch with a saddle stitch instead of using the machine, because machine stitching on tired leather risks the grain splitting along the new stitch line. Then we hand-finish the cut edge with wax and heat so it looks like the original finish.

Canvas and cotton duck cloth

The classic material for tote bags, work overalls and casual bag straps. Canvas is strong and takes alterations well, but it thins over time at the flex point — usually the top of the shoulder curve. When we shorten a canvas strap, we add a second row of stitching at the attachment point as reinforcement, because the original attachment stitching on a canvas strap is almost always the first thing to fail. On overall straps, we also check the buttonhole for stretch — a stretched buttonhole means the strap slips out of the button, which is the second thing to fail on overalls.

Synthetic leather and PU

The plastic-coated fabric used on budget bags, faux leather totes and mid-tier designer alternatives. Synthetic leather is much harder to alter cleanly than genuine leather, because the surface is a plastic coating that doesn't recover from stitching the way real leather does. Push a needle through synthetic leather and it leaves a permanent hole. Press a hot iron on it and it bubbles. Where the strap has to be shortened, we overlock the cut edge and finish with a fold — no exposed cut edges, because synthetic leather frays visibly at the coating layer. On synthetic straps that are already peeling at the edges, we tell clients honestly that the strap should be replaced entirely rather than altered.

Woven cotton and silk (dress straps)

Dress straps are usually a smooth woven fabric — silk, satin, cotton poplin, or a matching fabric cut from the dress itself. They're much lighter than bag straps, but they're often sewn into a garment with almost no seam allowance to work with. When a dress strap has to be shortened, we open the strap where it joins the bodice, shorten from the inside, and re-stitch it invisibly from the inside of the dress. On delicate fabrics like silk and satin, we hand-stitch throughout and use the finest possible needle so the fabric doesn't mark. Where the strap is adjustable (common on bridal gowns and evening dresses), we preserve the original slider or buttonhole so the strap can still be adjusted.

Cotton braid and elastic (bra straps)

Bra straps are their own category — usually a woven cotton or nylon braid, sometimes with an elasticated section, always with plastic or metal sliders. Shortening a bra strap is deceptively simple: unpick the slider, cut the strap, re-thread the slider, stitch the end. Lengthening is harder, because you're adding a section that has to blend with the elasticated strap and still feed through the sliders. On bra straps where the elastic has gone (common on older bras), we usually advise the client that replacement is more sensible than alteration. On bra straps where the elastic is fine and only the length is wrong, the adjustment is quick and permanent.

What we won't adjust

We turn down strap alterations every month because the honest answer is that the strap has reached end of life. On leather straps where the grain has cracked at the flex point, a shortening job will move the crack, not eliminate it. On synthetic straps where the coating is peeling at the edges, the strap will continue to deteriorate no matter what we do. On webbing straps where the weave has frayed to the point that the width is no longer consistent, the strap will keep fraying after alteration. On cheap bags where the whole strap is a single piece of plastic-glued fabric, the honest answer is usually that the bag should be replaced. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money. It's the same standard we hold on darning and invisible mending.

A short note from the bench

Why straps always fail at the attachment point

Every strap failure I've ever seen follows the same pattern. The client brings a bag with a snapped strap and shows me the break. Nine times out of ten, the break is at the top — where the strap joins the bag. Not in the middle of the shoulder curve. Not at the buckle. At the attachment point. There's a reason for this, and understanding it changes how a strap should be altered.

Think about how a strap carries weight. When you sling a bag over your shoulder, the whole weight of the bag — plus everything in it — is transferred through the strap to that one point where the strap joins the bag. The strap itself is fine because it distributes the load along its length. But the attachment point is where that entire concentrated load hits a narrow tab of leather or a single line of stitching. That's why the attachment tab is usually the shortest, thickest, most heavily stitched part of the strap. It's engineered to take load. It's also the first thing to fail on any bag where the engineering was compromised to save money.

When I shorten a bag strap, I don't touch the attachment point unless it needs touching. The attachment tab has already been designed, tested and stitched by the maker to carry the load. If I open it to shorten the strap, I'm replacing their engineering with mine — and unless I'm very careful, the new stitch line won't distribute the load as well as the original did. So on a shortening job, the cut happens at the free end of the strap (the end that goes through the buckle), not at the attachment end. The attachment stays factory. The end gets re-finished to match the original. And the client gets a strap that's shorter but still has its original strength where it matters.

The exception is when the attachment point is already failing — which is exactly the case for the bags clients bring me most often. On those jobs, I open the attachment, remove the old stitch line and rivet, and rebuild it properly. On leather, that means a hand-stitched saddle seam with a new rivet through the centre. On canvas, it means two parallel rows of stitching with bar-tacks at each end, which distribute the load the way the original should have. On webbing, it means re-bar-tacking with heavier thread than the factory used. This adds about thirty minutes to the job and adds three to five years to the bag's life. Most Nairobi tailors skip the attachment because it's fiddly and it's hidden inside the bag. Skipping it is why so many bag repairs fail within a year.

One more thing about straps that clients don't always think about. If you're shortening a strap that has a slider or buckle, you have to shorten from the correct end — the end that doesn't go through the hardware. If you shorten from the wrong end, you cut off the free end of the strap and the hardware can no longer be adjusted because there's no length left to feed through it. The strap becomes a fixed length whether you want that or not. I've seen this on more bags than I can count, and it's the single biggest mistake in strap alterations. It's fixable — we re-open the strap at the attachment and start over — but it costs more than doing it right the first time.

Fourteen years of strap work has taught me that clients who bring a strap to me usually want one of two things. Either they want it shorter — because the bag sits too low, or the overalls slip, or the dress keeps falling off — or they want it repaired, because the attachment has failed or the leather has cracked. Both are solvable, and both are worth solving, because a strap is what makes the item useful. A beautiful handbag with a broken strap is a paperweight. A great pair of overalls with torn straps is a garment that sits in the wardrobe. That's the work — restoring the thing that makes the item work.

Make your straps last between replacements

Eight habits that keep straps out of the workshop

Straps take more abuse than any other part of a bag or garment. They carry the full weight, they flex at the same points every time, and they get caught on chairs, doors and car doors daily. Most of the damage we repair in the workshop was preventable. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect an adjusted or repaired strap.

1. Rotate where the strap flexes

Every strap has a flex point — usually where it passes over your shoulder. If you always carry a bag with the same strap on the same shoulder, that flex point takes every load and dries out first. Rotating the strap to the other shoulder every few wears, or occasionally carrying the bag by hand, moves the flex point around and roughly doubles the strap's working life. On bags with a double strap, we always shorten both to the same length, but rotating which side you wear on is a client habit — not something we can do for you. It's the single biggest habit for extending strap life.

2. Never hang a heavy bag by one strap

Hanging a heavy bag — say, a fully-loaded tote or a leather holdall — by one strap puts the entire load on one attachment point and one rivet. Over time that one point will stretch, crack, or fail before the other. On bags with a single strap, that's the design and there's nothing you can do about it. On bags with two straps, always hang the bag by both, or better, set the bag down flat rather than hanging it. Never hang a leather bag from a hook by one strap for hours — leather stretches under sustained load, and the attachment tab deforms permanently.

3. Condition leather straps twice a year

Leather dries out over time. When it dries, it loses flexibility, cracks at the flex point, and becomes more prone to tearing at the stitch line. A good leather conditioner — applied twice a year on clean leather, worked in with a soft cloth — restores the oils and keeps the strap supple. Never use a petroleum-based product (it dissolves the wax finish); use a purpose-made leather conditioner. Nairobi's dry season and the July/August dust are the two moments a leather strap needs it most.

4. Don't over-load a bag

Every bag has a load limit, and it's usually lower than the client thinks. A leather tote designed for a laptop and a notebook can carry those for years. Load the same bag with three hardback books, a full water bottle and gym clothes every day and the strap will fail at the attachment within eighteen months. Nylon webbing bags can take more load than leather, but the same principle applies — a strap that's over-loaded every day will fatigue faster than one that isn't. If you routinely carry heavy loads, use a bag with wide webbing straps and a reinforced attachment rather than a thin leather strap.

5. Keep the hardware clean and dry

Metal hardware — buckles, D-rings, sliders, rivets — corrodes when it gets wet and isn't dried. On a bag with brass or nickel hardware, this shows up as a dull green or grey film that eventually pits the metal and stiffens the strap where it passes through. Wipe hardware dry after the bag has been in the rain, and once a year, give buckles and sliders a light polish with a metal cloth to keep the surface clean. On plated hardware (common on mid-tier bags), never use an abrasive polish — it strips the plating. Just dry and buff.

6. Don't stretch a strap to make it fit

If a strap is too short, stretching it is the wrong answer. Leather that's been stretched under load will relax back to a slightly stretched state, but the leather has been weakened at the flex point — which is the exact point where it will eventually fail. The right answer is lengthening, not stretching. Same on webbing — pulling a webbing strap tight to get it to fit stresses the weave and the attachment, and it will fray faster. If the strap doesn't fit, message us — a KSh 1,000 lengthening job is cheaper than a strap that fails.

7. Fix small issues before they become large ones

A stitch starting to fray at the attachment tab is a KSh 400 reinforcement job today. Left for three months, it becomes a KSh 1,200 attachment rebuild. Left for a year, the whole strap needs replacing. Same for a small crack at the flex point: caught early, it can be conditioned and stabilised for KSh 400. Caught late, it's a full strap replacement at KSh 2,500. The clients who spend the least on strap repairs are the ones who message us the first time they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less.

8. Store bags flat, not hung by the strap

A leather bag stored hanging by its strap for months will eventually develop a permanent curve at the flex point and a stretched attachment tab. Store leather bags flat on a shelf, or stuffed with tissue paper and sitting upright — never hanging by the strap. Nylon bags can hang by their straps without much issue, but even then, use a wide hook rather than a narrow one, so the strap isn't compressed at a single point. If you're storing a bag for a season, loosen the strap slider all the way so the leather isn't compressed under the buckle.

Follow those eight habits and your straps will hold their shape and strength for years. And when they do eventually need work — a shortening, a lengthening, a strap replacement — we're one WhatsApp message away.
