Two garments, two crafts

Why a jogger set is two alterations, not one

Almost every jogger set sold in Nairobi comes from a single factory size. The trousers are cut to fit an average body — usually a body that doesn't exist in real life. The hoodie is cut to the same average, but with completely different stretch and drape. Because most tailors treat the set as one job, they charge one price and use one technique — which is why so many altered tracksuits end up looking wrong somewhere. Here's why we treat them separately, and why that changes the result.

Jogger trousers are constructed like knitwear

Jogger pants — whether they're cotton fleece, French terry, or a technical synthetic blend — are knit or knitted-blend garments. That means the fabric moves with the body, stretches at the knee, relaxes at the hem, and holds a specific drape that changes depending on how the trousers are worn. When we shorten a jogger, we can't simply cut and stitch — the elasticated cuff at the ankle has to be preserved, re-linked, and re-stitched at the correct tension. If the tension is wrong, the cuff grips the ankle too tightly (creating a permanent bulge above the shoe) or too loosely (creating a floppy hem that drags). This is why our jogger hemming starts at KSh 1,500 — not because we're expensive, but because there's a real technique behind every pair we touch.

The waistband elastic is the first thing to fail

Every pair of joggers gets a stretch waistband because that's what makes them comfortable. But the elastic inside that waistband is what fails first — usually within eighteen months of regular wear. It loses its spring, the waistband starts to sag, and by two years the joggers will not stay up without a belt. Most people respond by pulling the drawstring tighter, which stretches the eyelets and eventually tears them. The right fix is to open the waistband channel, extract the old elastic, insert new high-quality elastic, and re-stitch the channel with a reinforced seam. That's a KSh 1,200 job, and on a pair of joggers you actually like, it's cheaper than replacing them.

Hoodies are a completely different garment to alter

A hoodie is structured like a boxy knit jacket. It has a hood, a kangaroo pocket, ribbed cuffs, an elasticated hem, and often a zip. Every one of those elements behaves differently under alteration. If you slim a hoodie body without adjusting the hem rib, the ribbing flares. If you shorten sleeves without re-anchoring the cuff ribbing, the sleeve twists. If you replace a hoodie zip without opening the entire front placket, the zip sits at a different tension from the original and the front of the hoodie starts to buckle. Hoodie alteration is genuinely a different craft from trouser alteration — and it's why we quote it separately.

Why we don't charge one set price

A full jogger set alteration — trousers and hoodie together — involves at least four distinct operations: waistband, hem cuffs, hoodie sleeves, and hoodie body. Even if you only want the trousers hemmed and the hoodie sleeves shortened, that's still two separate jobs on two separate garments. Pricing them as one number means one job gets short-changed. We price trousers and hoodie separately so you can see exactly what each piece of work costs. If you want the full set done in one go, we offer a combined rate that saves roughly 10% versus pricing each job individually — but you still see the breakdown.

Sportswear fabric doesn't forgive sloppy work

Most jogger sets in Nairobi are made from cotton fleece, French terry, or a polyester-cotton blend with a small percentage of elastane. Each of those has different behaviour under a needle — fleece pills if you over-stitch it, terry pulls at the seam if the tension is wrong, and elastane-blend fabric buckles if you press it with too much heat. On a tracksuit, a mistake shows within the first wash. That's why we cut with a tolerance, stitch at lower speeds on technical fabric, and never press directly onto a coated surface. It's slow work — but the result is a jogger set that holds its shape through years of wear.

Why this matters to your wallet

We charge KSh 1,500 to hem jogger trousers. A general alterations shop might charge KSh 700 — and hem them wrong, so the elastic cuffs no longer sit correctly and the leg twists by the second wash. On a KSh 1,500 pair of market joggers that's annoying. On a KSh 9,000 Nike Tech Fleece set, it's a KSh 9,000 mistake. Doing it right the first time is cheaper than doing it twice — and it's the same standard we hold on track pant alterations and sweater and knitwear alterations.

Read the fabric before you book

Three fabric tiers — three completely different alterations

Cotton fleece, French terry, and technical performance fabric require different handling. If a tailor treats them the same, one of them will end up looking wrong. Here's how we categorise jogger sets before deciding what technique to use.

Everyday tier

Cotton fleece and budget sweatshirts

Mass-produced jogger sets from market stalls, mid-tier retailers and fast fashion. Fabric weight 250–300 gsm, usually plain cotton fleece or a poly-cotton blend. Elastic waistband with drawstring, simple ribbed cuffs at the ankles and hoodie cuffs. Colour tends to fade over the first year of washing. The most common jogger set in Nairobi.

What we do differentlyFleece pills if the needle is too heavy, so we use a fine-gauge needle and cut with a larger seam allowance. The elastic in these waistbands is usually thin (20 mm or less) — we replace it with 25 mm or 30 mm for a firmer grip that lasts longer than the original did. Same price, better result.

Mid-tier

French terry, premium sweat sets

Nike, Adidas, New Balance, mid-range Italian and premium local brands. Fabric weight 300–400 gsm, often French terry or a brushed-back fleece with a smoother surface and better retention. Wider waistbands (4–5 cm) with thicker elastic, sometimes with elastic panels at the sides. The jogger set of Nairobi professionals.

What we do differentlyThe terry backing on the inside of the fabric pulls at the seam if the tension isn't even, so we use a walking-foot machine for even feeding. On hoodies, the double-layered hood needs to be opened and re-stitched rather than cut through the outer layer — a technique many tailors don't bother with.

Performance tier

Technical sportswear and designer sets

Nike Tech Fleece, Adidas Z.N.E., Lululemon, Gymshark, and designer hoodie-and-jogger sets from Italian or Japanese labels. Fabric weight 200–350 gsm, often with bonded seams, stretch panels, laser-cut ventilation, or fused hems. Waistbands sometimes fused rather than stitched. Precise seam tolerances to within a millimetre.

What we do differentlyOn garments where the hem is bonded (thermally sealed rather than stitched), we can't re-bond on a workshop bench — we re-stitch with a hidden seam instead. On technical fabric, we use the finest available needle and dry-press only, because heat damages the coating. A careless alteration on a KSh 12,000 Tech Fleece set is a KSh 12,000 mistake.

If you're not sure which tier your set falls into, send a photo of the label and the inside of the hoodie on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The six parts of a jogger set that affect the alteration

What a jogger set actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a jogger set as "comfortable trousers and a hoodie." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a jogger set has six parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to either piece.

01

The waistband — elastic, drawstring, or both

Jogger waistbands are usually 3 to 5 cm wide, with internal elastic and, on most pairs, a drawstring running in a separate channel. The elastic is what holds the trousers up. When the elastic goes slack, the waistband folds in on itself and the joggers slide down. Replacing the elastic means opening the waistband, removing the old elastic, threading the new one through, and re-stitching the channel — usually from the same access point so nothing shows on the outside.

02

The ankle cuffs — ribbed elastic knit

Every jogger ends in a ribbed elastic cuff — a knit band that grips the ankle and keeps the trouser from dragging. Ribbing has different stretch from the main fabric, so shortening the leg requires removing the ribbing, shortening the leg fabric, and reattaching the ribbing at the correct tension. Getting the tension wrong is the most common mistake: the cuff either grips too tightly (creating a visible bulge above it) or flops loose (creating a sagging hem). On premium joggers with contrast or technical cuffs, we source a matching rib knit to replace the originals if they've gone.

03

The leg — taper and knee

Jogger legs taper from the thigh to the ankle, sometimes aggressively. When we slim a jogger, we follow the original taper and simply reduce the width — we never impose a different shape than the trouser was designed for, because that would make it look modified. The knee is usually the widest point of the leg — anything we do below the knee has to preserve the natural drape that starts there. Slimming both legs takes two to three days because the seams have to be re-stitched at the correct tension on knit fabric.

04

The hoodie body — length, width, hem

Hoodie bodies are cut boxy by design — that's part of the comfort. When we slim a hoodie, we take in from the side seams only, tapering from the armpit down to the hem. The kangaroo pocket has to be preserved at the correct position, and the elasticated hem band has to be re-tensioned so it doesn't flare. On hoodies with a zip, the front placket has to stay vertical. Slimming a hoodie is a KSh 2,500 job and takes two to three days — it's one of the more technical knitwear alterations we do.

05

The hoodie sleeves and cuffs

Hoodie sleeves are usually raglan or set-in. Shortening from the cuff is straightforward on standard cuffs — detach, shorten, re-attach at correct tension. On hoodies with contrast cuffs or long ribbed cuffs, we sometimes shorten from the shoulder to preserve the cuff construction. On zipped hoodies, sleeve shortening can be trickier because the sleeve has to be re-aligned to the shoulder zip if the hoodie is a full-zip style. Sleeve shortening is KSh 1,500; sleeve slimming is KSh 2,000.

06

The hood and its drawstring

The hood is usually a double layer of fabric with a drawstring running through a channel around the front edge. Hood size is determined by the cut of the fabric and the depth of the crown seam — we can reduce the depth slightly but we can't make it much bigger. On hoods that have lost shape, re-blocking works — the hood is dampened, re-shaped on a form, and dried. Hood drawstring replacement is a KSh 500 fix and one of the smallest jobs we do that makes the biggest difference.

If a tailor quotes you a jogger set alteration without asking about the ribbing, the elastic, or the fabric tier, they're guessing — and the result will show up in the first wash.

Every fabric demands its own approach

The five jogger set fabrics we work with most often — and how each one behaves

Jogger set fabric decides how the alteration behaves under a needle, how it presses, and how it holds shape after the first wash. Here's what 14 years of tailoring has taught us, fabric by fabric.

Cotton fleece (brushed-back)

The classic jogger set fabric — a knit cotton with a brushed inner surface that feels soft against the skin. Cotton fleece takes alteration well because the fibre has give, but it also pills if you over-stitch it. We use a fine-gauge needle and stitch slowly, keeping the tension even so the seams don't draw in. Fleece also shrinks on the first hot wash — usually by half a size — so we cut with a small allowance for future shrinkage. If you've already washed the joggers several times, tell us when you message — we adjust the measurements accordingly.

French terry

French terry has a flat outer face and a looped inner face, giving it a smoother look and better shape retention than brushed fleece. It's the fabric of most mid-tier jogger sets and of most hoodies sold at premium retailers. Terry responds beautifully to alteration — the looped inner surface hides minor stitch imperfections, and the flat outer face presses crisply. The only tricky part is the tension: terry pulls at the seam if the tension isn't even, so we use a walking-foot machine for even feeding. Slight premium in price over cotton fleece, but worth it for the finish.

Poly-cotton blends

Common on mid-tier and budget jogger sets. The polyester content gives the fabric better stretch and drape but also makes it more sensitive to heat — hot ironing will leave a permanent shine on the fabric surface. We press poly-cotton blends through a muslin cloth at low temperature and never use a direct high-heat setting. On poly-cotton jogger sets, we also avoid heavy stitching, because the fabric can pucker around dense seam lines. Since the fabric is durable and stretchy, poly-cotton is one of the easiest tiers to alter well.

Technical performance fabric

Nike Tech Fleece, Adidas Z.N.E., Gymshark and other performance jogger sets. The fabric is often a bonded knit — two layers stuck together rather than stitched — with laser-cut ventilation, stretch panels, and sometimes a water-repellent coating. Alterations on this tier require the finest needles available and dry pressing only. We never put a technical jogger set through a standard sewing machine — we use an industrial single-needle with micro-adjustable tension. The reward is a fit that feels genuinely made for you, on a garment you'd normally never consider altering.

Wool-blend and cashmere-blend luxury knit sets

A smaller category in Nairobi but one that shows up on our bench regularly — luxury jogger sets in wool-cashmere blends from Italian or Japanese labels. The fabric is delicate, the finish is hand-sewn, and every alteration has to respect the knit structure of the fabric itself. We hand-finish every seam on these sets and never press directly onto the fabric. A wool-blend jogger hem takes about ninety minutes to finish properly — but the result holds its shape for years and the alteration disappears into the knit structure.

What we don't alter

On jogger sets where the outer face of the fabric is bonded (heat-sealed, not stitched), we can't re-bond on a workshop bench — so we re-stitch with a hidden seam instead, and we tell you upfront that the finish will differ slightly from the factory. On heavily distressed sets where the elastic has gone brittle and the ribbing has turned crunchy, we may advise retiring the set rather than trying to alter it. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money. It's the same standard we hold on sweater and knitwear alterations.

Before you book — does your jogger set actually need altering?

Seven checkpoints that tell you if a tracksuit is worth altering

Most clients message saying "my jogger set doesn't fit right." That's not enough for us to quote accurately, and it's not enough for you to decide what to ask for. Here's the checklist we use on every jogger set in the workshop. Run through it with both pieces on. If three or more come back positive, an alteration is worth doing. If one or two, you may be able to live with it.

01

The waistband slides down even with the drawstring tied

Tie the drawstring snugly and walk ten steps. If the waistband still slips below your hip bone, the elastic has lost its spring — that's the number one issue we fix on joggers. Sometimes the drawstring channel has stretched as well, which is a slightly bigger job. Standard waistband elastic replacement is KSh 1,200. The drawstring itself is free to replace when done alongside a waistband replacement — KSh 500 as a standalone job.

02

The ankle cuffs feel loose or have lost their grip

Pull the cuff of each ankle over your shoe and let it snap back. It should feel firm against the ankle — not squeezing, but gripping. If the cuff hangs loose, the elastic in the ribbing has failed. Cuff replacement is KSh 1,200 per pair of cuffs, and we source matching ribbing in the same colour as your joggers. If the cuffs are still tight but the hem length is wrong, then it's just a hemming job — KSh 1,500 with the cuff preserved. Different problems, different prices — that's why we always check the cuffs before we quote the hem.

03

The legs billow past your knees

Stand side-on to the mirror. If the fabric behind your knee puffs out and holds, the jogger cut is too wide for your shape. Leg slimming on both legs is KSh 2,000. We taper from the knee down to the cuff, following the original shape of the jogger — never imposing a new shape. The ankle cuff stays intact and the pocket bags are re-anchored at the top corner (which most tailors skip, and which is why so many slimmed joggers tear at the pocket within a year).

04

The hoodie sleeves cover your hands

Raise your arms in front of the mirror. The sleeve should end at your wrist bone with the cuff ribbing still touching the back of your hand. If the cuff fabric covers your knuckles, the sleeves are too long. Shortening the sleeves on a hoodie is KSh 1,500 — we detach the cuff ribbing, shorten the sleeve body, and reattach the ribbing at the correct tension. If the sleeves are too short, we can usually let them down by 1.5 cm at the cuff; more than that and the sleeve fabric is usually gone.

05

The hoodie body is too long — it covers the kangaroo pocket awkwardly

If the hem of the hoodie sits more than 5 cm below your hip bone and it drapes over the front pocket, it's too long. Hem shortening on hoodies is KSh 1,500 — we shorten the body, preserve the elasticated ribbing at the hem, and re-attach at the correct tension. On hoodies where the original hem ribbing has failed, we source a matching rib knit and rebuild the whole band (KSh 1,800 because the rib has to be knitted and matched).

06

The hood sits too deep or flops over your eyes

Put the hoodie on and pull the hood up. It should sit around your head without falling forward over your eyes. If the hood depth is wrong, we reshape it — the hood is dampened, re-shaped on a form, and dried. Hood reshapes are KSh 1,500 and take one to two days because the hood has to dry slowly in the new shape. If the hood drawstring has lost its aglets or frayed, we replace it for KSh 500 — one of the smallest and most satisfying fixes we do.

07

The front zip drags, skips or has gone sticky

Zip the front up and down a few times. If the zip drags, skips teeth, or feels sticky, it's failed — and on a hoodie, a failed zip makes the garment unwearable. Front zip replacement is KSh 1,000 using a heavy-duty YKK metal zip. We open the full placket, remove the old zip, fit the new one, and re-align everything so the front sits straight again. Same-day is often possible if you message before midday. See our zipper replacement page for the wider service.

Run through all seven. If three or more come back positive, send us a photo of your joggers and hoodie on WhatsApp and we'll tell you honestly which alterations will make the biggest difference. You don't have to fix everything at once. Often the right answer is to fix one thing and live with the rest.

An honest decision — alter, or retire?

When a jogger set is worth altering, and when you should just buy new

We turn down jogger set jobs every month. Not because we can't do them — because the honest answer is that a KSh 2,000 alteration on a KSh 1,800 market tracksuit doesn't make sense. Here's the test we use before quoting. If your set fails more than two of these, buy new.

✓

Was the jogger set good quality to begin with?A KSh 1,800 market tracksuit has a short remaining life no matter how carefully you alter it — the fabric pills faster, the elastic goes slack sooner, and the seams pull earlier. On a KSh 8,000 mid-tier set or a KSh 12,000 Nike Tech Fleece, every alteration pays back because the fabric has years of life left. The value of the original garment is the first thing we check.

✓

Is the fabric still supple or has it gone stiff and pilled?Run the outside of the fabric between your thumb and fingers. Does it feel smooth and knit-like, or has it gone pilled, stiff and slightly scratchy? Fabric that has pilled badly will not hold a new seam evenly — the alteration will look right on collection and start to show wear at the new seam within a month. On heavily pilled sets, the honest answer is usually to retire rather than alter.

✓

Is the elastic and ribbing still workable?If the waistband elastic has gone slack and the cuffs have turned crunchy, replacing all three of those (waist, ankle cuffs, hoodie cuffs) can push the alteration past the value of the set. On a KSh 12,000 set, that's still worth it — you're saving KSh 10,000+ by comparison. On a KSh 2,000 set, three elastic replacements at KSh 1,200 each is already more than the set is worth. We tell you straight which side of the line you're on.

✓

Will the total cost be under 35% of a new set?On a KSh 5,000 mid-tier tracksuit, we keep the total alteration under roughly KSh 1,750 to be worth doing. On a KSh 12,000 set, we can go up to KSh 4,000 and it still makes sense. On budget sets below KSh 2,500, we usually cap total spending at KSh 800 — beyond that, replacing makes more sense. The percentage stays the same; the numbers scale with the garment.

✓

Do you actually wear the set often enough?There's no point altering a jogger set you wear twice a year. But if it's the set you wear every weekend, the one that travels with you, the one you'd otherwise have to replace — the numbers clearly favour alteration. If you wear it less than four times a month, we sometimes recommend letting it be, or donating it and buying something that actually fits. Honest advice.

✓

Does the set have meaning beyond the price tag?A set your team wore in a specific season, a tracksuit from a specific trip, a set from a family member — the calculation changes entirely. On sentimental garments, we do the alteration no matter what the numbers say, because the value isn't in the fabric. Tell us when you message, and the quote is built around the meaning, not the price tag.

You won't get a hard sell when you message us. If the honest answer is "buy new," we'll tell you that straight. Better that than charging you for an alteration that stops looking right in six months. Same standard we hold on fabric patching and elastic replacement.

A short note from the bench

Why ribbing tension is the whole job on a jogger alteration

When clients ask me what's the hardest part of altering a jogger set, I always give the same answer: ribbing tension. Not the fabric, not the length, not even the waistband. Every jogger and every hoodie has ribbed cuffs and hems, and how those ribs are reattached after an alteration is what separates a tracksuit that looks factory-finished from one that looks home-sewn. I want to explain the technique, because it's the answer to a question that clients ask me almost every week.

Ribbing is knit differently from the body of a jogger. The body is plain knit or fleece — loops running in horizontal rows. Ribbing is knit with alternating knit-and-purl columns, which creates vertical stretch while keeping horizontal stability. That's why a jogger cuff grips the ankle without strangling it, and why a hoodie hem stays flat across the hips instead of drooping. But that alternating structure also means ribbing has a specific stretch percentage — usually 40 to 60 percent more stretch than the body fabric. Every time we reattach a ribbed cuff after shortening a sleeve or a leg, we have to reproduce that stretch percentage exactly.

There are two ways to attach ribbing after shortening. The fast way is to cut the ribbing to size, overlap the ends, and stitch through. On cheap tracksuits this works fine for a while — then the cuff starts to twist because the tension isn't even, or the rib flares because the fabric has less give than the shortened sleeve body, or the seam shows as a visible bulge because the stitch density is wrong. The slow way — the way we work — is to un-link the ribbing from the sleeve body row by row, remove the surplus length, and re-link the ribbing at the exact tension the factory used. On a fine-gauge jogger cuff, re-linking a hem takes about forty-five minutes end to end. On a hoodie hem with wider ribbing, closer to an hour.

The difference between the two techniques shows within two washes. The fast-attached cuff starts to twist — the seam pulls to one side. The slow-attached cuff stays exactly where it should. On a KSh 12,000 Nike Tech Fleece set, that difference is visible from a metre away after the second wash. On a KSh 3,000 tracksuit, it matters less — but it still matters. We do the slow version on every jogger set, no matter what it cost originally, because that's the standard.

The same principle applies to the waistband. When we replace the elastic inside a jogger waistband, we match the original elasticity of the elastic — usually 100% stretch on a tech fleece, closer to 60% on cotton fleece. If we use elastic that's stronger than the original, the waistband will grip the waist more firmly than the wearer is used to; if we use weaker, the waistband sags. Getting this right requires a bench-side feel for what the wearer wants — is the joggers' fit supposed to be snug or relaxed? We ask when we can. When we can't, we match the original factory specification.

If you own a jogger set where the cuffs or hem ribbing has gone loose and you're not sure whether a re-link is worth it — send me a photo of the cuff close-up and the whole trouser. I'll tell you honestly whether the ribbing can be re-tensioned (KSh 1,200) or whether it needs to be replaced with new matching rib (KSh 1,200 plus material). Either way, you'll get a proper job, done at the correct tension. That's the answer you deserve.

Make your jogger set last between alterations

Eight habits that keep tracksuits out of the alteration pile

A jogger set takes more punishment than almost any other garment — worn to the gym, worn around the house, washed after every wear. That cycle is what ages them fastest. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect an altered jogger set. The same ones we follow with our own.

1. Wash cold — always cold

Heat is the biggest killer of elastic and ribbing. Hot water softens the rubber fibres inside the waistband and cuffs, and once softened they don't fully recover. Over twenty or thirty hot washes, the elastic gradually loses its spring — and that's the single biggest reason joggers end up in our workshop. Cold water — 30°C or below — preserves the elastic far longer. The rest of the garment doesn't care about temperature nearly as much as the elastic does.

2. Turn them inside out before washing

Joggers fade and pill from the outside in, so turning them inside out protects the visible surface. This is especially important on French terry and premium blends, where pilling on the outer surface ages the set fastest. Wash with similar colours and never overfill the drum. If your joggers have a drawstring, tie it loosely before washing so it doesn't tangle.

3. Air dry flat — never tumble

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shrink a jogger set and kill the elastic. The heat softens the rubber, the tumbling stresses the fibres, and the result is a waistband that goes slack within a few months. Air dry instead — laid flat on a rack, or hung over the back of a chair. If you must use a dryer, use the lowest heat setting and remove the pieces while still slightly damp. Never hang joggers by the drawstring — the whole weight of the wet garment on one cord stretches the eyelet holes and eventually tears them.

4. Depill with a proper fabric shaver

Pilling is normal on cotton fleece and terry — surface fibres tangle as they rub against each other. Remove pills with a fabric shaver or sweater comb designed for knitwear. Never use a disposable razor — it cuts the fabric itself, not just the pills, and creates thin patches that eventually open into holes. Depill gently, in one direction, with the garment laid flat on a table. Ten minutes of depilling every few months adds years to the life of a tracksuit.

5. Rotate between two or three sets

Knit fabric needs 24 to 48 hours to recover its shape between wears. Wear the same set every day and the elastic stays stretched, the cuffs stay compressed, and the set ages twice as fast as it should. If you own two or three jogger sets and rotate them, each one gets worn a third as often — which roughly triples its life. This single habit alone roughly doubles the working life of waistband elastic.

6. Don't over-fill the kangaroo pocket

The kangaroo pocket on a hoodie is designed for a phone, keys and a small wallet. If you carry heavy items in it daily — a full water bottle, tools, gym equipment — the pocket fabric stretches and the top corner of the pocket tears within a year. That's the number one reason hoodie pockets fail in our workshop, and it's entirely preventable. Use a small gym bag or a belt pouch for anything heavier than a phone.

7. Fix small issues before they grow

A drawstring starting to fray, a cuff slightly loose, a small pull at the kangaroo pocket corner — these are KSh 500 to KSh 1,200 fixes when caught early. Left alone, they become KSh 2,000+ repairs because the surrounding fabric fails alongside the original damage. The clients who spend the least on alterations are the ones who message us first when they notice something. Small fixes early always cost less.

8. Store folded, not hanging

Hanging a jogger set by the waistband puts the entire weight of the garment on the elastic and the drawstring eyelets — which stretches them and eventually pulls the eyelet holes out. Fold joggers and hoodies flat in a drawer or on a shelf. Stack them loosely rather than tightly, so the knees and cuffs don't take on permanent creases. If you're storing a set long-term, use a breathable cotton garment bag rather than plastic.

Follow those eight habits and your jogger set will hold its shape and elastic for years. And when it does eventually need an alteration, we're one WhatsApp message away. See our elastic replacement page if the care has slipped and repair is needed.
