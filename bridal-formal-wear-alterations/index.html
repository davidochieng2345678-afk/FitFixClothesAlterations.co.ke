The questions I actually get asked, answered honestly

Fourteen years of bridal tailoring, and the same ten or so questions come up every week. I've put my honest answers below. If yours isn't here, WhatsApp me — I reply personally, not a receptionist.

Cost

"How much does it cost to alter a wedding dress in Nairobi?"

It depends on the fabric and the amount of work. Hemming on a simple satin gown starts at KSh 3,500. If the gown has lace overlay, beading, or a train, the hem alone can take four hours. Full body slimming — taking in the bodice and reshaping the side seams — starts at KSh 4,500. Bustle and train lifting, KSh 2,500. Strap adjustments from KSh 1,500. I'll give you a firm number on WhatsApp once I see a photo, and I won't move it after.

Timing

"How long before the wedding should I get the dress altered?"

Three to four months if you can. That gives room for a first fitting, a second fitting, and a final check three weeks out. But I know life doesn't always work that way. If you're two weeks out, message me — I've finished full bridal gown resizes in ten days. If you're three days out, message me anyway. I'll tell you honestly whether I can help.

Fabric

"My dress is silk. Can you alter it without ruining it?"

Yes — but slowly. Silk marks permanently if the needle or the iron is too aggressive. I hand-stitch silk hems and every seam gets pressed through a muslin cloth with low heat. Same for velvet and any fabric with a nap. If I look at a dress and think an alteration carries real risk, I'll say so upfront instead of charging you and hoping for the best.

Beading

"Can you alter a dress that has beading or lace on it?"

This is my speciality. Beaded and lace gowns are the ones most tailors refuse, because you can't just run them under a machine. I remove the beads along the seam by hand, alter the fabric underneath, then reattach every bead individually. It's slow work. On a heavily beaded bodice, reattaching can take a full day. But it is the only way to get the fit right without destroying the design.

Urgent

"My wedding is in four days — is that too late?"

Not always. I keep two slots a week open for exactly this. If the dress just needs a hem and a strap adjustment, I can do it in a day. If it needs a full bodice rebuild, four days is tight but not impossible. WhatsApp me the photo and the deadline. I'll give you a straight yes or no in five minutes — no dragging it out.

Fit

"My dress is too small. Can you make it bigger?"

Sometimes. Most quality gowns have 1 to 2 centimetres of seam allowance built in, which lets me let out the sides a bit. If there's no allowance left, the answer depends on the design — I can sometimes add a matching panel of lace or fabric that blends into the existing pattern. If I can't do it well, I'll tell you. I won't take a job that I know will look wrong when it's finished.

Groom

"Can you tailor the groom's suit and groomsmen's suits too?"

Yes, and I'd rather do it than have you take them somewhere else. Matching suits altered at the same time end up looking more consistent. I collect the whole set from one address, alter them together, and deliver them back together. Groom suit tailoring starts at KSh 3,500; groomsmen suits are the same rate, and I discount the group if there are five or more.

Pickup

"Do you come to my house, or do I have to bring the dress in?"

I come to you. Free, anywhere in Nairobi — Runda, Kilimani, Karen, Kasarani, Ongata Rongai, Kitengela, Utawala, all of it. You send me a photo first, I quote you, then I send somebody to collect. When the dress is done I bring it back and you try it on in your own home. If anything needs a final tweak, I take it back with me and return it. No charge for any of that.

The fabric matters more than the fit

What I can and can't do, by fabric type

Most people don't realise that bridal alterations aren't just about measuring. They're about knowing how each fabric behaves under a needle. Here's my honest rundown — the same one I give brides on WhatsApp before I quote them.

Silk & Satin

Silk is unforgiving. Every needle leaves a permanent mark, and every wrinkle in the iron shows up like a scar. I hand-stitch silk hems and press them through muslin at low heat. Satin is slightly more forgiving but has the same problem when it comes to needle marks. Hemming a satin gown takes roughly two hours. A body slimming on a satin bodice takes most of a day.

Lace & Tulle

Lace is beautiful to alter because the pattern hides the seam. Tulle is the opposite — anything heavy tears it. I use French seams on lace and a rolled hem on tulle, and I never machine-stitch either. If your gown has a lace overlay with a tulle underskirt, the hem is usually two separate operations: shorten the tulle first, then reapply the lace by hand. That's why lace hems cost more than plain ones.

Beaded Fabrics

Beading is what makes a gown expensive and what makes a tailor nervous. I remove beads along the seam by hand, alter the fabric underneath, then reattach every bead individually. A heavily beaded bodice can take a full day just to re-bead. It's slow, it's tedious, and it's the only way to get the fit right without destroying the design.

Crepe & Chiffon

Both are drapey, both are unforgiving on the fit, and both are used constantly in bridesmaid dresses. Crepe doesn't hide a bad seam, so every line has to be perfect. Chiffon is a nightmare to press — it singes before it smooths — so I do most of the work with steam and my hands rather than the iron. If a bridesmaid dress is chiffon, expect the hem to take a full hour.

Velvet

Velvet has a nap — a directional pile — and if you cut or press it the wrong way, the whole panel shows a permanent light-versus-dark patch. I lay the pattern pieces flat before I cut, mark the direction with chalk, and only work on velvet in daylight. Velvet gowns are usually worn for winter or evening weddings. If yours is velvet, come in for a fitting — I won't take a velvet job without seeing it in person.

Wool, Tweed & Suiting

For groom suits and groomsmen, this is the easy category. Wool takes a press beautifully, holds a crease, and forgives most adjustments. Tweed needs a lighter hand because the weave is loose and can tear if you rush the needle. Nothing here is difficult — but I still measure twice. A groom's suit that's half a centimetre off in the shoulders looks wrong in every photograph.

If you don't know what fabric your dress is, send a close-up photo on WhatsApp. I'll tell you what it is and what's possible. No guesswork, no vague answers.

Still unsure?

The rest of the questions I get asked

Can you replace a broken zipper on a wedding dress?

Yes, and it's one of the most common emergency jobs I take. A wedding dress zipper is usually an invisible zipper, which means it sits inside the seam and can't be seen from the outside. Replacing one properly takes two to three hours. I keep a range of invisible zippers in stock because brides often call me the week of the wedding. Same-day turnaround is possible if you message me early in the day. Cost starts at KSh 1,500.

What is a bustle, and does my wedding dress need one?

A bustle is a set of concealed hooks, loops, or ribbons under the back of the dress that lift the train up off the floor after the ceremony — so you can walk, dance, and go to the bathroom without dragging the train behind you. If your train is longer than about 30 centimetres, you need one. If you have a short sweep train, you can probably leave it. The most common bustle I install is a three-point bustle, which lifts the train up in three places and keeps it looking even. Cost is KSh 2,500.

Can you make my wedding dress bigger if I've gained weight?

Sometimes, depending on the seam allowance. Quality gowns are usually cut with 1–2 centimetres of hidden allowance in the side seams — that's the tailor's margin, and it lets me let the dress out by roughly one size. If the dress was made cheaply or was already let out once, there may be no allowance left. In that case, I look at whether I can add a matching panel of lace or fabric in a way that blends with the original design. I'll tell you honestly what's possible before you commit.

Do you charge extra for rush alterations?

Sometimes, and I'll always tell you upfront. A normal bridal alteration takes three to five days. If you need it back in two days, it's a moderate rush fee. If you need it back in 24 hours, it's a bigger rush fee — and I'll only accept it if I'm confident I can finish it properly. I'd rather turn down a rush job than deliver something rushed. Wedding photos last forty years.

Can I bring my dress to your workshop instead of you collecting it?

Absolutely. My workshop is in Industrial Area, Nairobi — a short drive from the CBD, and there's parking. Some brides prefer to bring the dress in person so they can see the workspace. I'm happy either way. Message me on WhatsApp and I'll send you the exact location pin.

Do you do fittings at my house, or do I come in for fittings?

Both. Home fittings are my preferred option for brides — you're already in the dress, in your own light, with your own shoes and underwear. It's easier to see what needs adjusting. But if you'd rather come into the workshop, I have a private fitting room and plenty of mirrors. Fittings are always free, whether at home or here.

Do you alter dresses from other designers or boutiques?

Yes. I alter gowns from every major bridal boutique in Nairobi — designer gowns, imported gowns, vintage finds, and locally made dresses. I don't care where the dress came from. I care about getting the fit right without damaging the garment. If your gown is a designer piece that needs careful handling, tell me the label when you message — some designers use construction methods that I know to approach differently.

What payment methods do you accept?

M-Pesa, cash, or bank transfer. For bridal work I ask for a 50% deposit once the quote is confirmed and the dress is collected, with the remaining 50% on final delivery. If you'd rather pay it all at the end, that's fine — just tell me upfront. I don't take cards.
