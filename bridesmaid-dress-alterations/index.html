The hardest alteration in womenswear

Why a bridesmaid dress alteration is harder than any other dress job, and why most Nairobi tailors quietly avoid wedding parties

A single bridesmaid dress is a delicate alteration. A wedding party of six bridesmaid dresses is a coordination problem that most tailors turn down. The dresses are often ordered from a designer or a bridal shop in the same size or the closest size available, which means every dress needs work, every dress needs to look identical from the front of the church, and every bridesmaid has a different body that the original pattern was not cut for. Add in a deadline that cannot move because the wedding day does not move, and you understand why most Nairobi tailors say no. Here is what we actually check when a bridesmaid dress, or six of them, land on the bench.

The dress has to fit the bridesmaid, and still match the row

The hardest part of a bridesmaid dress alteration is not the fit. It is the coordination. If six bridesmaids wear the same dress in the same colour, they have to read as one continuous line from the front of the church. That means the hem length has to be identical across all six dresses, measured from the same point, on six women who are different heights. It means the bodice has to be taken in at the waist on each dress without changing the shape of the neckline, because the neckline is what reads on camera. It means the straps sit at the same height on six different shoulder shapes. Every one of those details is measured against the group, not just against the individual. Book bridesmaid dress alterations with free door to door pickup in Nairobi Kenya today and we will coordinate the whole party, not just each dress on its own.

Bridal fabrics are the most delicate we work with

Bridesmaid dresses are cut from chiffon, satin, crepe, tulle, silk, georgette, lace overlay and chiffon-lined satin, sometimes all in the same dress. Every one of those fabrics behaves differently under the needle. Chiffon slips. Satin shows every pin mark. Crepe stretches in one direction and not the other. Tulle tears if the needle is too thick. Lace overlay has to be lifted and re-anchored after any cut underneath, because cutting through lace destroys the pattern. Silk shows every needle mark and cannot be pressed with a hot iron at any point. Every bridesmaid dress we alter is worked at low tension with fine needles and pressed through muslin or steamed. On silk and chiffon, we also rest the dress for 24 hours before the final check, because those fabrics move when they relax. Schedule bridesmaid dress fitting with free pickup across all Nairobi estates today and we will quote each fabric on its own terms.

Beadwork, lace and appliqué have to be preserved

Many bridesmaid dresses have beaded straps, beaded necklines, lace overlay, appliqué panels or embroidered bodices. Any alteration that touches one of those areas has to preserve the beading or the lace exactly as it was. Cutting through a beaded bodice means removing the beads first, re-cutting the fabric underneath, then re-attaching every bead by hand at the original spacing. Cutting through lace overlay means lifting the lace off the lining, altering the lining, then re-anchoring the lace so the pattern still reads continuous. This is slow, careful work, and it is the reason a bridesmaid dress alteration on a beaded silk gown can cost three times what the same alteration costs on a plain crepe dress. We always tell the client upfront which parts of the dress are delicate and why the price reflects that. The deadline cannot move, and that changes everything

A wedding day is the only deadline in tailoring that never moves. That means the timeline matters more than the price. A bridesmaid dress that takes four days is not a problem. Six bridesmaid dresses that need to be finished, checked, and returned together two weeks before the wedding is a planning problem. We recommend starting the bridesmaid dress alterations at least six weeks before the wedding date. If the wedding is closer than six weeks, we can still take the job but we tell the bride upfront what the realistic timeline is, and we do not take on a wedding party we cannot finish. Wedding parties are almost never made up of women with the same body type. One bridesmaid is taller, one is shorter, one has a longer torso, one has a fuller bust, one has wider shoulders, one has a smaller waist. The dresses come in the same size or the closest size available, which means each dress fits one bridesmaid well and the rest need work. Not every bridesmaid dress is the same job

Three bridesmaid dress tiers, three completely different alterations

A KSh 5,000 market bridesmaid dress and a KSh 45,000 designer silk gown look similar in a photo but are not the same garment. One is a simple two-layer polyester dress with a machine-stitched hem and no beading. The other is a hand-finished silk dress with a lace overlay, beaded straps, and a bodice built on a hidden corset. Treating them the same way is how a good dress gets ruined. Here is how we categorise bridesmaid dresses before deciding which technique to use.

Polyester, market and budget bridesmaid dresses

Mass-produced bridesmaid dresses from market stalls, budget retail, and fast-fashion wedding shops. Simple two-layer construction, machine-stitched hem, factory-closed bodice, and no beading, lace or appliqué. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 3,000 and KSh 10,000.

What we do differentlyOn these we work pragmatically because the goal is to make the dress fit, not to restore a construction that was never there. Mid tier

Better retail, satin, crepe and chiffon bridesmaid dresses

Bridesmaid dresses from better retail and mid-tier bridal shops. Fabric weight 200 to 300 gsm, in satin, crepe, chiffon, or a chiffon-over-satin combination. Keep the bridesmaid dress perfect from delivery to the wedding aisle

Eight habits that keep an altered bridesmaid dress looking new until the wedding day and beyond

A bridesmaid dress has a short but intense life. It is altered two to six weeks before a wedding, worn once for a full day of standing, sitting, dancing, hugging and photographs, and then either kept as a memory or worn to another formal occasion. The gap between delivery and the wedding day is where most dresses pick up their problems: a crease that sets overnight, a bead that comes loose, a hem that catches on a chair. Every habit below is something we teach every bridesmaid when she collects her dress, and none of them takes more than five minutes.

1. Hang on a padded or shaped hanger, never wire

A bridesmaid dress should be hung on a padded or shaped hanger that supports the shoulder line. On a thin wire hanger, the weight of the skirt pulls the shoulder seams out of shape and the bodice slowly distorts. This is worse on a beaded or lined dress because the weight is greater. A padded hanger costs about KSh 500 and protects a dress that cost ten to forty times that. When we deliver an altered bridesmaid dress, we bring it on a proper hanger, and we ask the bridesmaid to transfer it to her own padded hanger before she does anything else. Never hang a bridesmaid dress over the back of a chair, on a doorknob, or on a radiator. The weight of the skirt will pull the whole dress out of shape within a week.

2. Store in a breathable garment bag, never plastic

Between delivery and the wedding day, a bridesmaid dress should be stored in a cotton or canvas garment bag, never plastic. Plastic traps moisture against the fabric, which creates the environment mildew loves, and mildew on satin, chiffon or silk shows up as small dark spots that cannot be removed without damaging the fabric. A cotton garment bag costs KSh 1,500 and protects a dress worth twenty to forty times that. For long-term storage after the wedding, add a cedar block or lavender sachet to each pocket of the bag. On a beaded dress, use a garment bag with a padded shoulder so the beads do not press into the fabric of the bag.

3. Steam the dress, never press it directly

The single biggest mistake a bridesmaid can make between delivery and the wedding is pressing the dress at home with a hot iron. A hot iron flattens the fabric, shines the surface, and on satin or silk leaves a permanent mark. If the dress needs refreshing before the wedding, use a steamer held six to eight inches away from the fabric. Steam lifts the surface wrinkles without touching the weave. If you must press, use a clean cotton cloth between the iron and the fabric at low to medium heat, and never press beaded, lace-covered or silk areas. On chiffon, never press at all, only steam, because chiffon loses its natural movement under a direct press and the hem will go stiff.

4. Do not try the dress on with self-tanner, perfume or deodorant on

Satin, chiffon and silk absorb anything they touch. Self-tanner, perfume and deodorant spray leave invisible marks on the dress that become visible under the strong lights at the wedding. If a bridesmaid tries the dress on between delivery and the wedding, she should do it with clean skin, no perfume, and no deodorant. Apply perfume and deodorant after the dress is on, and let them dry completely before the ceremony. The same rule applies on the wedding day itself. This is the single most common cause of visible marks on bridesmaid dresses we hear about after the wedding.

5. Check the beadwork and lace weekly between delivery and the wedding

On a beaded or lace-covered bridesmaid dress, run your hand gently along the beaded areas and the lace edges once a week between delivery and the wedding. If a bead is starting to come loose, we can re-stitch it in a five-minute visit before the wedding. If a lace edge is starting to lift, we can re-anchor it in the same visit. Do not pull on a loose bead or thread. Just note where it is and message us on WhatsApp. A five-minute re-stitch today prevents a bead falling off on the wedding day, and on a dress with a beaded hem a single bead falling on a smooth floor is a slip risk for the bride.

6. Carry a small bridesmaid emergency kit in your handbag

Every bridesmaid should carry a small emergency kit in her handbag on the wedding day. The kit should include: a small sewing needle threaded with the same colour as the dress, a safety pin in the same colour, a spare invisible zip slider, a small clear nail varnish for stopping a run in chiffon or tights, and a stain remover pen in a matched colour. This is not about expecting the dress to fail. It is about knowing that if a bead comes off, a strap pulls, or a small mark appears, the bridesmaid can handle it in thirty seconds without anyone knowing. Most of the small dress emergencies we hear about are a KSh 200 fix in the moment and a KSh 2,000 repair after the wedding.

7. Loosen any adjustments before sitting and dancing

If a bridesmaid dress has a corset back, a laced panel or a tight waist seam, loosen the lacing slightly before sitting for a long dinner, and before dancing. Sitting in a tightly laced bridesmaid dress stresses the waist seam more than standing, and on a dress that has been taken in at the waist, that stress is the most common cause of a seam failing during the reception. Loosening by half a centimetre is enough. Re-tighten after the meal if the look matters for the photographs, and loosen again before dancing. Small adjustment, big difference to how the dress holds on the day.

8. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose bead is a KSh 500 re-stitch today. Left for two weeks, the bead comes off entirely and the thread pulls, and the job becomes a KSh 3,000 strap or hem re-bead. A strap that has slipped a millimetre is a KSh 1,500 adjustment today. Left alone, the strap stretches and starts to show, and the job becomes a KSh 3,000 strap replacement. A small mark from perfume or deodorant is a KSh 2,000 spot-clean today. Left for a week, the mark sets and the job becomes a KSh 5,500 lining replacement on the affected panel. One more habit that is not about the wedding day itself but about the years after. The cloth decides the technique

Every bridesmaid dress fabric behaves differently under the needle, and the alteration changes with the fabric

Most tailoring advice treats bridesmaid dresses as one category of garment. They are not. A polyester satin bridesmaid dress and a silk chiffon bridesmaid dress with a lace overlay are not the same job, even when they are cut to the same pattern. The needle, the tension, the press, the thread, the amount of hand-finishing, and even the way we collect and deliver the dress change with the cloth. Here is how we handle every bridesmaid dress fabric we work with, and why each one needs a different approach. Polyester satin bridesmaid dresses, the everyday standard

Polyester satin is the fabric most budget and mid-tier bridesmaid dresses are cut from. It is a smooth, tightly woven fabric with a distinct sheen on one side and a matte finish on the other. It takes a press well at medium heat through a muslin cloth, and it behaves predictably under a standard needle. The two things we watch on polyester satin are pin marks, because satin holds a pin mark longer than almost any other fabric, and sheen, because a hot iron on the shiny side creates a permanent bright patch. Every polyester satin bridesmaid dress we alter is pinned with silk pins rather than standard pins, and pressed through muslin at medium heat with the iron on the matte side only. Hem shortening on polyester satin starts at KSh 1,500. Bodice take-in starts at KSh 2,500. The same technique we use on this fabric carries into our bridal and formal wear alterations work.

Chiffon bridesmaid dresses, the fabric that slips

Chiffon is the fabric that gives bridesmaid dresses their movement, and it is also the fabric that slips the most under the needle. Chiffon is a lightweight, plain-weave fabric, usually in polyester, silk or a blend, and it is almost always cut as an overlay over a lining. The hem of a chiffon dress is not cut and machine-stitched, because a machine stitch stiffens the hem and destroys the movement. Chiffon hems are rolled by hand, a technique called a rolled hem, where the edge is folded over twice at about two millimetres and stitched by hand with a fine needle. This is slow work and it is why chiffon hem shortening starts at KSh 2,500 rather than KSh 1,500. On a chiffon dress with a beaded hem, the beads are removed first, the hem is rolled by hand, and the beads are re-attached at the same spacing on the new hem line. Silk and silk crepe bridesmaid dresses, the most delicate cloth in the workshop

Silk is the most delicate cloth we work with on bridesmaid dresses. It is a natural protein fibre with a smooth hand and a natural sheen that no synthetic fabric can replicate. The two things silk hates are needle marks and heat. A standard needle leaves a visible hole in silk that does not close, and a standard iron leaves a permanent shine or a burn. Every silk bridesmaid dress we alter is worked at low tension with the finest needles we stock, and the dress is steamed rather than pressed. Silk hems are hand-rolled and hand-finished, and every seam that was hand-finished at the factory is re-set by hand. Silk crepe behaves slightly differently from silk satin, because crepe has a matte finish and a slightly pebbled surface, and crepe stretches more in the cross direction than in the length direction, which means the pattern has to account for direction when we cut. Crepe and georgette bridesmaid dresses, structured with movement

Crepe and georgette are mid-weight fabrics that sit between satin and chiffon in both weight and behaviour. Crepe has a slightly matte surface and a firm hand, and it drapes with a structured fall that reads formal on camera. Georgette is lighter than crepe, more fluid, and often used for the skirt or the sleeves of a bridesmaid dress while the bodice is cut from a heavier fabric. Tulle bridesmaid dresses, the fabric that tears

Tulle is the netting fabric used in bridesmaid skirts, underskirts, sleeves and overlays. It is lightweight, transparent, and structurally delicate. Tulle tears if the needle is too thick or the tension is too high, and it does not self-heal, which means a wrong stitch leaves a permanent hole. Every tulle panel we alter is worked with a fine ballpoint needle at low tension, and every torn point is reinforced before any alteration begins.
