The difference between a patch and a proper repair

Why most patching fails within a year — and how to do it so it doesn't

There are two ways to patch a garment. The first is to cut a square of fabric, lay it over the hole, and run a zigzag stitch around the edge. That's what most alterations shops do, and it takes about ten minutes. The second is to think about the fabric, the direction of the weave, the tension of the surrounding cloth, and the way the garment moves when worn — then plan a patch that disappears into that structure. The first way fails within a year. The second way holds for the life of the garment. Here's what the difference actually looks like.

Fabric has a direction — ignore it and the patch shows

Every woven fabric has a warp (the threads running vertically) and a weft (the threads running horizontally). Every knit fabric has a wale (the vertical columns of loops) and a course (the horizontal rows). When you patch a garment, the patch fabric has to be oriented to match the original direction — warp to warp, weft to weft, wale to wale. If the patch goes on the bias (diagonal) relative to the surrounding fabric, it will catch the light differently, feel different to the touch, and age differently. Within three months, the patch looks like a patch. Within a year, the surrounding fabric has pulled against it and the seam has started to fray.

The reason we don't just zigzag around the edge

A zigzag stitch around a patch is fast, strong, and above all obvious. The zigzag transfers all the stress of the repair to two points — the top and bottom of the patch — and within a year those points start to tear. On top of that, the zigzag is what makes a patch look like a patch. On a pair of work jeans worn in a garage, that's fine. On a favourite wool jacket, it's a scar. We use a variety of stitching methods depending on the fabric and the location — hand-darning, close-set straight stitching, overlock reinforcement, or invisible mending — none of which produce the tell-tale zigzag pattern.

The edge of the patch matters more than the patch itself

Most patching failures start at the edge of the patch, not the centre. If the edge is left raw, it frays — slowly on cotton, faster on wool, and quickly on linen or silk. We finish every edge on every patch — with an overlock, a bound edge, or a folded-under finish depending on the fabric and the location. It's a two-minute extra step per patch. It's the reason our patches last three times as long as the ones we see done elsewhere.

Invisible patching is a different craft from visible patching

There's a place for visible, honest patches — workwear, denim, and garments where the patch becomes part of the design. But when a client wants an invisible patch on the visible surface of a good jacket, the technique is completely different. Instead of laying a patch on top, we open the seam, insert a patch underneath the torn area, and re-weave the surface threads from the surrounding fabric across the top. The patch itself becomes structural support; the surface stays visually intact. This takes 3 to 5 times as long as a standard patch. It's also the only way to repair a suit jacket or a good blazer without it looking like a repair.

The fabric you supply is often the wrong one

Clients sometimes bring their own patch fabric — usually a piece of similar-looking cotton from a market stall. Sometimes that works. Often it doesn't, because the weight, weave or stretch percentage is different from the garment. If you bring your own patch fabric, that's fine — but we'll check it against the garment before we cut anything. If it won't hold, we'll tell you and offer to source a matching piece from our own stock. We keep denim, canvas, wool, tweed, cotton and lining fabric on hand for exactly this reason.

Why this matters to your wallet

A rushed patch is KSh 400. A properly done patch is KSh 500 to KSh 1,200 depending on size and location. The rushed patch fails within a year — sometimes within six months — and you either pay for it again or you stop wearing the garment. The properly done patch holds for the life of the garment. On most garments, doing it right the first time is cheaper than doing it twice, and it's the same standard we hold on darning and zipper work.

Read the damage before you book

Three tiers of patching — three different jobs, three different prices

A small moth hole on a cashmere sweater and a blown-out knee on a pair of work trousers are both "patching" in theory — but they require completely different techniques. If a tailor treats both the same way, one of them is going to look wrong within months. Here's how I categorise patching jobs before I decide what technique to use.

Standard patch

Small holes, tears and splits

Small holes up to about 2 cm across, short splits along a seam, snags on thin fabric, cigarette burns on non-delicate cloth. The kind of damage that happens in daily wear — a hook catching a jacket, a snag on a shirt, a small tear in a trouser leg. Handled with a targeted patch that blends or blends-with-character depending on the garment.

What we do differentlyEven on a small patch, we match the weave direction, finish every edge, and use the correct thread weight for the fabric. A 2 cm hole on a cotton shirt takes about twenty minutes. Same hole on a wool jacket takes forty because the fabric is denser and the finish matters more.

Reinforced patch

Worn-through knees, elbows and seats

Fabric that has thinned and opened from repeated stress — knees on work trousers, elbows on jackets and shirts, seats on any trouser, cuffs on sleeves. Damage is not from a single incident but from sustained wear, and the surrounding fabric is usually also compromised. Common on workwear, denim, school uniforms and well-worn office trousers.

What we do differentlyWorn-through areas need reinforcement before patching — an internal layer of matching fabric stitched behind the weak area so the patch has something to hold onto. Without that internal layer, the patch holds for a few months and then the surrounding fabric fails next to it. We add the internal reinforcement as standard on every worn-through job.

Invisible patch

Visible-surface damage on quality garments

Holes, tears or burns on the visible surface of a good jacket, blazer, coat, suit trouser or dress — where a visible patch would be unacceptable. Includes moth damage on wool suits, cigarette burns on blazers, small tears on the front of a coat, and splits on the front panel of a dress. Also includes restoration of vintage and heirloom garments where the fabric itself is delicate.

What we do differentlyOn a visible-surface repair, we do not lay a patch over the damage. Instead we open the seam or lining, insert a matching support layer underneath, and re-weave the surface threads across the damage by hand. The patch itself is hidden — the surface looks intact. This takes three to five times as long as a standard patch. It's the only technique that works for a suit jacket.

The five parts of a properly done patch

What a patch actually is — piece by piece

Most people think of a patch as "a piece of fabric on top of a hole." That description doesn't tell you anything about how the patch is built or why some patches hold and some don't. Structurally, a good patch has five layers and details, and each one changes how the repair behaves over time. Here's what I look at before I take scissors to a piece of matching fabric.

01

The patch fabric itself — matched or contrasting, on-grain or on-bias

The patch fabric must match the garment in weight and weave direction. If you use a heavier patch on a lighter garment, the patch pulls the surrounding fabric out of shape. If you use a lighter patch on a heavier garment, it wears through faster than the surrounding fabric. And critically, the patch must be cut on-grain (aligned with the original warp and weft or wale and course) — otherwise it catches the light at a different angle and looks wrong from two metres away.

02

The internal reinforcement layer (where needed)

On worn-through areas — knees, elbows and seats — the surface fabric is compromised. A patch stitched on top of failing fabric is a patch that will fail. We add an internal reinforcement layer behind the weak area first, stitched to the seams of the garment so it supports the repair without being visible. This layer is what makes the difference between a patch that holds for a year and a patch that holds for the life of the garment. It's standard on every worn-through job we do.

03

The edge finish — raw, overlocked, folded or bound

Every patch has four edges, and every edge has to be finished. A raw edge frays. An overlocked edge is durable but visible. A folded edge is tidy but adds bulk. A bound edge is the strongest finish but the slowest to do. The finish we choose depends on the fabric and the location — on an internal reinforcement layer, overlock; on a visible patch on workwear, a folded edge; on a decorative patch, a bound edge. The wrong finish shows within months. The right finish is invisible from the outside.

04

The stitching method — machine, hand-darned or re-woven

Standard patches are stitched by machine — a dense running stitch around the perimeter and sometimes a diagonal pattern across the middle to hold the patch flat against the garment. Invisible patches are stitched by hand — the surface threads are re-woven one by one across the damage, and the support layer behind is stitched by hand so no machine marks show through. Re-woven patches on premium garments take 3 to 5 times as long as a machine patch, which is why the pricing reflects that.

05

The press — how the patch is finished and set

After stitching, the patch is pressed — and how it's pressed matters as much as how it's stitched. On cotton and wool, we press through a muslin cloth at controlled heat so the patch settles into the surrounding fabric. On synthetic and coated fabrics, we press at low temperature or not at all, because heat damages the coating. On leather and suede, we never press — the material would be permanently marked. Pressing is the final step that makes the patch look like part of the garment rather than an addition to it.

If a tailor quotes you a patch without asking about the fabric weight, the grain direction or the finish, they're guessing — and the patch will look like a patch within a year.

Every fabric demands its own patch technique

The five fabrics I patch most often — and how each one behaves

Patching is where fabric knowledge shows most. Each material patches differently — some need reinforcement, some need heat control, some can be re-woven, and some can't be patched at all without showing. Here's what I've learned across fourteen years, fabric by fabric.

Denim and heavy cotton twill

Denim is the most forgiving fabric to patch because it's built to take punishment and it looks better with age. A patch on denim doesn't need to be invisible — it needs to be strong. We use 12 to 14 oz denim as patch fabric, on-grain, with a folded edge. On knees and seats, we reinforce internally first, then lay the patch and stitch it with a dense running stitch in a thread colour that matches the original stitching. If the client wants a visible reinforcement, we sometimes use a contrasting thread for a modern look. Denim patches are one of the strongest repairs we do and often outlive the original garment.

Wool, tweed and worsted suiting

Wool suiting is where the invisible patch technique really matters. A visible patch on a wool suit jacket is not an option — clients wear these to work and to events, and a patch on the front panel would be a permanent embarrassment. We open the lining or seam, insert a support layer of matching wool underneath, and re-weave the surface threads by hand across the damage. The re-weaving follows the original weave direction exactly and uses thread drawn from a hidden seam inside the garment so the colour is a perfect match. This is slow work, and it costs more than any other patching we do — but the result on a KSh 45,000 suit jacket is a jacket that looks untouched.

Cotton shirting and poplin

Cotton shirting patches are usually straightforward because the fabric is thin and holds a small patch well. The tricky part is matching the weave — fine poplin has a tighter weave than oxford cloth, and the patch fabric has to match the original density or the patched area will pucker. On visible surfaces, we do invisible patching — the damage is re-woven from behind with matching cotton thread. On lower-visibility areas like the tail of a shirt or the inside of a cuff, a simpler patch works. Cotton patches age gracefully if the weave is matched; if it isn't, the patch draws attention to itself within months.

Leather and suede

Leather patching is a completely different craft. You cannot stitch a patch onto leather in the normal way — every needle hole is permanent, and if you have to reposition a stitch you've already ruined the patch. Instead, leather repairs are done with a combination of hand-stitching through existing holes, adhesive bonding under the surface, and — for larger tears — a patch set behind the leather and stitched through in a way that mimics the original construction. Suede is even more difficult because the surface finish is delicate and cannot be matched. We handle leather and suede patching personally, and we'll tell you honestly if a repair is not advisable — because on leather, a bad repair is worse than the original damage.

Knitwear, jersey and fine knits

Knitwear doesn't take a patch the way woven fabric does — the loops behave differently. On a jumper or a knitted top, the right technique is re-knitting rather than patching: the loops around the hole are recreated one by one in the original yarn direction, in the original gauge. This is the same technique we use for sweater and knitwear alterations. If the hole is too large to re-knit, we sometimes add a support layer behind the knit and re-knit the surface loops on top — invisible from the outside, structurally sound underneath. On jersey fabrics (t-shirts, polo shirts), the same principle applies but the loops are smaller, so the repair is faster.

Silk, satin and delicate blends

Silk is the most delicate fabric we patch. Every stitch leaves a mark, every press leaves a shine, and the fabric tears if you handle it too quickly. Silk repairs are done by hand on a daylight bench, using fine needles and matching silk thread drawn from a hidden seam. On visible surfaces, the technique is re-weaving — support layer behind, surface threads rebuilt across the damage. On light silk, the repair will still show a faint shadow if you look closely — that's the nature of the material, and we tell clients upfront rather than pretending otherwise. On heavily patterned silk, the patch is sometimes cut from a matching section of the garment itself (an inside seam, an underarm panel) so the pattern continues across the repair.

If you don't know what your garment is made of, send a close-up photo of the fabric on WhatsApp. I'll identify the weave and tell you honestly what's possible and what isn't.

Before you book — does it actually need patching?

Seven checkpoints that tell you whether a garment is worth repairing

Most clients message saying "there's a hole, can you fix it?" That's not enough for me to quote accurately, and it's not enough for you to decide whether to spend the money. Here's the checklist I use on every damaged garment in the workshop. Run through it with the garment in front of you. If three or more of these come back negative, the honest answer is usually to retire the garment rather than patch it.

01

Is the damage isolated or is the surrounding fabric failing?

Run your thumb gently around the edges of the hole or tear. Does the fabric feel solid, or does it feel thin and soft at the edges — as if the next pull will enlarge the hole? If the surrounding fabric is still strong, patching is straightforward and the repair will hold. If the surrounding fabric has gone thin and dusty, patching just moves the damage — the hole will open again next to the patch within months. On failing fabric, we sometimes recommend reinforcing the whole area first — from KSh 1,000 — rather than doing a standard patch.

02

Is the damage in a stress area or a quiet area?

Damage at the knee, elbow, seat, underarm or crotch is in a stress area — fabric that moves and stretches every time you wear the garment. Damage in a quiet area (the back of a shirt, the inside of a sleeve, the tail of a coat) is much easier to repair and will last much longer. Stress-area patches need reinforcement behind the patch; quiet-area patches don't. Telling me where the damage is when you message is the fastest way to an accurate quote.

03

Is the damage on a visible surface or a hidden one?

A hole on the front chest of a good jacket needs an invisible patch — the slow, hand-worked technique that takes three to five times as long as a standard patch. A hole on the inner thigh of a trouser, or on the back of a jacket, can take a standard patch and the client will never see it. When you message, tell me where the damage is on the garment — that single piece of information changes the price and the technique more than the size of the hole itself.

04

Is the fabric lightweight or heavy?

Lightweight fabrics — poplin, voile, light cotton, silk, fine wool — need careful handling and often invisible re-weaving rather than patches, because the weave is fine and a stitched patch would show through the fabric from behind. Heavy fabrics — denim, canvas, wool, tweed, leather — take patches well and hold up to reinforced stitching. The lighter the fabric, the slower the repair, and the higher the cost per square centimetre. Fine silk can cost more to patch than heavy canvas.

05

Will the repair cost less than 40% of a new garment?

A KSh 5,000 pair of jeans with a blown-out knee at KSh 700 is a clear yes. A KSh 1,500 cotton shirt with a small tear at KSh 600 is borderline — the repair is 40% of the shirt's value. A KSh 3,000 jacket with a visible-surface hole needing an invisible patch at KSh 1,200 is still worth doing if the jacket fits well and the fabric is good. On the other hand, an invisible patch on a KSh 2,000 suit that has already been altered twice is where the numbers turn. We tell you straight which side of the line your garment falls on.

06

Do you want an invisible patch or a visible one?

Invisible patching costs more but makes the repair disappear. Visible patching costs less and — on workwear, denim and casual garments — can actually add character. Some clients specifically want a visible, honest repair — a leather patch on a denim jacket, a fabric elbow patch on a tweed blazer, a contrast knee reinforcement on work trousers. Tell me your preference when you message. It changes both the technique and the price.

07

Does the garment fit you and do you actually wear it?

There's no point patching a garment that doesn't fit or that you haven't worn in two years. A patch is a small investment in a garment's future — but only if the garment has a future. If the fit is also wrong, message me anyway and tell me what the fit issue is. Sometimes we combine a patch with an alteration on the same visit — a knee reinforcement alongside a hem shortening, or a patch alongside a waist take-in. Two repairs at once often costs less than two separate pickups.

Run through all seven. If three or more come back negative, send me a photo anyway — I'll give you an honest assessment rather than just taking the job. And if the honest answer is "don't repair it," I'll tell you that too. That's the same standard we hold on darning and invisible mending.

An honest decision — patch, or retire?

When a garment is worth patching, and when you should just let it go

I turn down patching jobs every week. Not because I can't do them — because the honest answer is that a KSh 700 patch on a garment that will fail elsewhere in six months doesn't make sense. Here's the test I use before I quote you. If your garment fails more than two of these, the honest advice is to let it retire.

✓

Is there just one damaged area, or several?One hole, one tear, one worn patch — that's a repair. Four or five damaged areas on the same garment usually means the fabric is at the end of its life and every repair just moves the problem. If we patch one hole today and you're back with another in two months, we've both wasted time and money. On heavily damaged garments, we tell you straight that the fabric is failing — and the honest advice is to retire it rather than spend KSh 2,000 on patches that won't hold.

✓

Was the garment good quality to begin with?A KSh 1,500 market jacket that's been worn daily for three years is often past the point of patching being worthwhile. The same damage on a KSh 8,000 jacket or an Italian coat is almost always worth repairing. The value of the original garment is the first thing we check — because a patch on a cheap garment rarely pays back.

✓

Is the fabric still supple, or has it gone stiff and brittle?Run the fabric between your fingers. Supple fabric takes a patch well. Stiff, brittle fabric tears along the seam line as soon as you stitch — the patch itself becomes the failure point. On garments where the fabric has gone brittle (usually from over-washing or from long-term storage in a hot environment), a patch is not advisable. We'll tell you honestly if that's the case.

✓

Is the repair cost under 40% of a new garment?If a mid-tier jacket costs KSh 6,000 new, a KSh 2,400 patch is at the upper end of what makes sense. Anything above that tips toward buying new — unless the garment has sentimental value, which changes the calculation entirely. On higher-end garments (Italian wool, designer denim, good leather), the calculation shifts because the replacement cost is much higher.

✓

Is the damage in a spot that will fail again?A hole in the seat of a work trouser that was caused by sliding on benches will fail again in the same spot if the repair isn't reinforced. A tear in the underarm of a shirt that was caused by repeated stretching will tear again next to the repair. On these "cause-specific" damages, the fix is always a reinforced patch that addresses the underlying stress point — not just a cover over the hole. We tell you when this is the case.

✓

Does the garment have meaning beyond its price tag?A jacket your father wore, a shirt from a specific trip, a piece of workwear with a story — the calculation changes entirely. On sentimental garments, we do the repair no matter what the numbers say, because the value isn't in the fabric. Tell us when you message, and the quote is built around the meaning, not the price tag. On heirloom garments, we also use a different technique — hand-worked, matching thread drawn from inside the garment, slower finish — because a machine repair on an heirloom never feels right.

You won't get a hard sell when you message me. If the honest answer is "retire it," I'll tell you that straight. Better that than charging you for a patch that starts failing in six months — and it's the same standard we hold on zipper replacement and elastic replacement.

A short note from the bench

How to make a patch disappear — the invisible patch, explained

When clients ask me whether a hole in a suit jacket can be repaired, they usually expect the answer to be no. A visible patch on the front chest of a jacket is worse than the hole — everyone would ask what happened. So most people assume the jacket is done. It isn't. The invisible patch technique exists, and it produces a result that most people — even other tailors — cannot find afterwards. I want to explain how it works, because it's the answer to a question that clients ask me almost every week.

The first step is access. We open the garment — usually at the lining seam on the inside, or at an inconspicuous construction seam on the outside — so we can reach the back of the damaged fabric without cutting into the visible surface. On a suit jacket, this means opening the lining along the inside of the front panel. On a coat, we open the inside hem. On a shirt, we open the side seam or the inside placket. This access step is invisible from the outside — nothing the client sees is disturbed.

The second step is the support layer. Behind the damage, we insert a piece of matching fabric — sometimes drawn from a hidden seam inside the garment itself so the colour is a perfect match — and stitch it in place from behind. This support layer becomes the structural foundation for the repair. It's the layer that stops the damage from opening again next to the patch, and it's the reason invisible repairs last.

The third step is the surface rebuild. This is the slow part. Working from the front, we take a fine needle and matching thread — drawn from the same hidden seam as the support fabric — and re-weave the surface threads across the damage. On a wool jacket, this means recreating the warp and weft in the correct directions and at the correct spacing. On a fine cotton shirt, the threads are thinner and the weaving is finer. On knitwear, the technique is entirely different — we recreate loops rather than weave threads — which is why knit repairs are usually done as re-knitting rather than weaving.

The fourth step is the press and the closure. We press the repair through a muslin cloth at controlled heat — never direct — so the re-woven threads settle into the surrounding fabric. Then we close the access opening we made at the start, using the same stitch pattern and thread colour as the original seam. When it's finished, the repair is not visible from any angle in normal light. Under a magnifying glass, an experienced eye can usually find the repaired area — but nobody wears a suit jacket under a magnifying glass.

The whole job takes me between three and five hours on a wool jacket, and up to two days on more complex damage. It starts at KSh 1,200 for a small hole and goes up to KSh 3,500 for larger areas or burns. I do these repairs myself — not because my team can't, but because a bad invisible patch is worse than no patch at all, and the standard I hold for that work is what our reputation rests on. If you have a garment with visible-surface damage and you're not sure whether it's worth repairing — send me a photo. I'll tell you honestly whether the invisible patch is the right answer, or whether the honest advice is to leave the garment alone.

Make the patch last

Eight habits that keep patched garments looking good for years

A good patch holds for the life of the garment — but only if the surrounding fabric stays healthy and the repair is treated the same way as the rest of the garment. Here are the eight habits I teach every client when they collect a patched garment. The same ones I follow with my own.

1. Wash cold, wash inside out

Patches are stitched onto fabric, and every stitch has a small amount of tension. Hot water relaxes the surrounding fabric and can pull the patch out of alignment — usually by just a millimetre, but that's enough to make the repair visible after a few washes. Turn the garment inside out, wash cold, and let the machine agitate gently. If the garment has a visible patch on an exterior surface, wash it inside out to protect the patch face from friction against the drum.

2. Air dry flat — never tumble

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shorten the life of a patch, for the same reason it shortens the life of elastic: heat and friction. The stitched edges of a patch are the most vulnerable part, and tumble drying tugs at them from every direction. Air dry instead — on a line, on a rack, or laid flat. If the garment is a jacket or coat with a visible patch on the front, dry it flat so the patch doesn't hang under its own weight.

3. Iron from the inside, never directly on the patch

Ironing directly over a patch is one of the fastest ways to ruin it. The press can flatten the patch face, cause the stitches to show through the surface, or — on leather or suede patches — leave permanent shiny marks. Always iron patched garments from the inside, or through a thin muslin cloth if the patch is on the outside. On suede and leather patches, do not iron at all — steam from the reverse if needed.

4. Rotate the garment, don't wear it every day

A patched garment that's worn daily has no recovery time. The surrounding fabric stays stretched, the patch seam stays under tension, and the whole repair ages twice as fast as it should. If you have a favourite pair of patched trousers or a jacket, own a second pair or jacket and rotate them. Even one day of rest between wears makes a measurable difference to how long the patch holds.

5. Fix small issues before they become new holes

Watch for new wear around the edges of an existing patch — a small fray, a loose stitch, a slight thinning of the surrounding fabric. If you spot one, message us. A quick stitch at the edge is a KSh 300 job. Left for six months, it becomes a new hole that connects to the original one, and you're looking at a KSh 800 repair on top of the KSh 700 you already paid. Small fixes early always cost less.

6. Don't over-strain the patched area

If the patch is on a knee, seat or elbow, that area is already under stress by design — you're kneeling, sitting, or bending there constantly. Do what you can to avoid the specific motion that caused the original damage. If the knee blew out from kneeling on rough concrete, put a mat down next time. If the seat blew out from sliding on a bench, stop sliding. The repair is strong, but the surrounding fabric is only as strong as it was before.

7. Store the garment the way it wants to be stored

Patched trousers fold flat. Patched jackets hang on a wide-shouldered hanger so the repair doesn't crease. Patched knitwear folds flat rather than hanging. If you have a patched garment that gets stored long-term, put it in a breathable cotton bag — never plastic — and add cedar blocks or lavender sachets to deter moths. A moth hole next to a patch is a much more expensive repair than the original patch was.

8. Tell us when you notice something wrong

Patches are one of the few repairs where the client usually notices the early signs of a problem before we do. If the patch looks different after a wash, if the stitching feels different under your fingers, if the surrounding fabric starts to fray — message us. Almost every patch problem is fixable in the early stages, and almost every one becomes expensive in the late stages. Client feedback is what keeps our repairs holding for years, and we take it seriously.

Follow those eight habits and your patched garments will hold for years. And when the next repair is needed, we're one WhatsApp message away.
