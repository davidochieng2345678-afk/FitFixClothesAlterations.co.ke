1. The History & Heritage of the Trench Coat

To understand why a well-made trench coat is such a powerful garment, you need to understand where it came from. The trench coat was not designed as a fashion item. It was designed as a weapon of survival.

During the First World War, soldiers fighting in the muddy trenches of France and Belgium needed protection from the rain, wind, and cold—while still being able to move freely. The traditional heavy wool greatcoat soaked through and weighed the soldier down. A lighter, weatherproof alternative was needed.

Two British brands—Burberry and Aquascutum—are famously credited with developing the first trench coats. They replaced the heavy wool with tightly woven cotton gabardine, chemically treated to repel water. They added epaulettes to hold the straps of binoculars and other gear, D-rings on the belt for grenades, storm flaps to protect against rain running down the shoulders, and a long belt that could be used to carry wounded comrades.

Every single detail served a purpose. The "gun flap" on the right shoulder was designed to shed water away from the rifle. The wrist straps were tightened to keep water out of the sleeves. Even the belt buckle had a functional role, allowing the coat to be cinched tight against wind and rain.

From the Trenches to the Boardroom

After the war, soldiers returned home and continued to wear their trench coats—not because they needed to, but because they had come to appreciate the utility, the elegance, and the sense of quiet authority the garment conveyed. Hollywood took notice. In the 1940s and 1950s, film stars like Humphrey Bogart, Cary Grant, and Audrey Hepburn cemented the trench coat's place as a symbol of sophistication and mystery.

By the 1960s, the trench coat had become a staple of the business wardrobe. It was the natural companion to the suit, offering protection from the elements while maintaining a sharp, polished silhouette. Presidents wore them. Executives wore them. Anyone who wanted to project calm competence in the face of the storm wore them.

Today, the trench coat remains one of the few garments that is simultaneously practical and elegant. It is at home in a monsoon, in an airport, in a boardroom, and at an evening dinner. It is a garment that has earned its place in the modern wardrobe.

Why Most Modern Trench Coats Are a Betrayal of That Heritage

Unfortunately, the trench coat's legacy has been cheapened. The vast majority of trench coats sold today are pale imitations of the original. They are made from thin synthetic fabrics that bear no resemblance to the tightly woven gabardine of the 1910s. They feature decorative flaps that serve no purpose. Their buckles are plastic. Their belts are glued rather than stitched. And their fit is drafted for an imaginary "average" body that does not exist.

When you put on one of these modern imitations, you do not feel like a man walking into a storm. You feel like a man wearing a costume. The fabric feels cheap. The silhouette looks wrong. The coat does not protect you, and it does not flatter you.

A bespoke trench coat from FitFix is a return to the original ethos. It is a functional garment built for the real world. It is a piece of engineering that protects you from the elements, flatters your body, and communicates an unmistakable sense of confidence.

2. The Anatomy of a True Bespoke Trench Coat

What separates a genuine bespoke trench coat from a cheap imitation? It is not just the fabric. It is the way the garment is constructed, the details that are included, and the engineering that goes into every seam.

The Canvas: The Invisible Skeleton

Most trench coats on the market today are fused—meaning the outer fabric is glued to a synthetic interlining. This is fast and cheap, but it destroys the natural drape of the garment and causes bubbling after a few months of wear.

Our bespoke trench coats feature a full floating horsehair canvas in the chest and lapels. This is a layer of natural horsehair, wool, and camel hair that is hand-stitched into the chest of the coat. It gives the garment its structure and shape. Over time, the canvas molds itself to your body, creating a fit that becomes more personal with every wear.

This canvas construction is one of the most significant differences between a genuine bespoke garment and a mass-produced one. It is expensive, time-consuming, and entirely invisible from the outside—but you can feel it the moment you put the coat on.

The Storm Flaps

A true trench coat has functional storm flaps. There is a large "gun flap" on the right shoulder, designed to shed water away from the body. There is a smaller storm flap on the left chest, protecting the chest from rain. Some designs also include a "storm shield" on the back, protecting the upper back from water running down the coat.

On a cheap trench coat, these flaps are decorative—purely cosmetic details sewn flat against the fabric. On a bespoke FitFix trench coat, they are functional engineering features designed to keep you dry in a real storm.

The Belt and Buckle

The belt is one of the most iconic features of the trench coat. It allows the wearer to cinch the coat tightly against wind and rain, transforming the silhouette from a loose overcoat to a sharp, tailored piece. On a high-quality trench coat, the belt is cut from the same fabric as the coat and finished with a solid brass or metal buckle.

On a cheap trench coat, the belt is often made from a cheaper fabric, and the buckle is plastic. It will lose its shape, crack, or discolor within a season.

Our bespoke trench coats use only solid brass hardware—the kind that will last for decades. The buckle is heavy, tactile, and satisfying to use. The belt loops are reinforced with bar tacks to prevent tearing.

The D-Rings

Originally designed to carry grenades on the battlefield, the D-rings on the trench coat belt have become a signature design detail. On a genuine trench coat, they are solid metal rings attached to the belt, allowing the wearer to hang small items—keys, a hat, gloves, or, in modern iterations, a small bag.

On a cheap trench coat, the D-rings are often plastic or thin metal, incapable of supporting any weight. On our bespoke trench coats, they are forged from solid brass and hand-stitched into the belt with reinforced leather tabs.

The Sleeve Straps

Another functional detail from the trenches: the sleeve straps. These are adjustable straps on the wrists that allow the wearer to tighten the sleeves against wind and rain. They are not just decorative. They keep water from running down your arms.

On cheap trench coats, the sleeve straps are often fake—just a decorative piece of fabric with no real function. On our bespoke trench coats, they are fully adjustable and functional, allowing you to cinch the sleeve tightly around your wrist or wear it loose.

The Collar

The collar of a trench coat is one of the most important design elements. It should be cut to sit smoothly against your neck, with the ability to be turned up to protect your face from wind and rain. The collar should be large enough to make a statement, but not so large that it overwhelms your proportions.

Our bespoke trench coats feature a hand-rolled collar with a wide facing, allowing it to stand up sharply when popped and lie flat when worn down. This is a detail that takes hours of handwork—and it is the first thing people notice about the coat.

The Back Vent

A true trench coat has a single or double back vent, allowing the coat to move naturally as you walk. Without a vent, the coat would bunch up awkwardly and restrict your stride. On a cheap trench coat, the vent is often sewn shut or cut too short, making it non-functional.

Our bespoke trench coats are drafted with a proper working vent, allowing you to walk, sit, and drive without restriction.

3. Fabric Selection: Ventile, Gabardine & Beyond

The fabric is the soul of a trench coat. It determines how the coat looks, how it feels, how it performs in the rain, and how long it lasts. At FitFix, we offer a curated selection of heritage fabrics—each with its own character, weight, and performance characteristics.

Ventile: The Aviator's Choice

Ventile is, without question, the most impressive fabric in our trench coat collection. It was developed during the Second World War for British fighter pilots, who needed a fabric that could keep them dry while flying at high altitudes over the North Atlantic.

Ventile is made from long-staple cotton, tightly woven at high density. The result is a fabric that behaves like no other. When dry, it is soft and breathable, allowing air to circulate freely. But when it comes into contact with water, the cotton fibers swell and close the tiny gaps between threads, creating a completely waterproof barrier.

This means Ventile is not chemically treated with waxes or synthetic coatings. It does not rely on chemicals to repel water. It uses the natural properties of densely woven cotton. The result is a fabric that breathes exceptionally well, repels water completely, and lasts for decades.

The downside of Ventile is cost. It is expensive to produce, and the resulting fabric is more costly than most other trench coat fabrics. But for a garment designed to last a lifetime, it is worth every shilling.

High-Twist Gabardine: The Classic

Gabardine is the original trench coat fabric. It was patented by Burberry in 1879 and was the material used in the very first trench coats. High-twist gabardine is made from tightly twisted cotton yarns woven in a diagonal pattern, creating a dense fabric that naturally repels water.

Our gabardine trench coats feature a water-repellent finish that enhances the natural weather resistance of the fabric. They are slightly lighter than Ventile and have a subtle sheen, giving them a sharp, polished appearance.

Gabardine is the classic choice for a formal trench coat. It works beautifully over a suit and pairs elegantly with dress shoes. It is also easier to care for than Ventile, as it can be dry-cleaned and pressed without affecting its weather resistance.

Coated Cotton: Modern Performance

For clients who want maximum weather protection with a modern aesthetic, we offer coated cotton trench coats. These feature a lightweight polyurethane coating applied to the inside of the fabric, creating a fully waterproof barrier.

Coated cotton is heavier and stiffer than Ventile or gabardine, but it offers complete protection against heavy rain. It is the ideal choice for clients who commute by motorbike or spend significant time outdoors in Nairobi's rainy seasons.

Lining: The Internal Layer

The lining of a trench coat is just as important as the outer fabric. It determines how the coat feels against your suit, how warm it is, and how well it breathes.

Our standard lining is Bemberg cupro—a breathable, anti-static material made from cotton linters. It glides smoothly over your suit, preventing friction, and helps the coat breathe. For warmer months, we offer a partial lining or a removable liner. For colder months, we offer a full wool lining for additional warmth.

Every lining is hand-stitched into the coat, with generous seam allowances to prevent tearing. The interior of the coat is finished with the same care as the exterior—because a bespoke garment should be beautiful from every angle.

4. Customization Options & Design Details

One of the great pleasures of commissioning a bespoke trench coat is the level of customization available. Every detail can be specified to your exact preferences, ensuring the finished garment is uniquely yours.

Length

Traditional trench coats come in three lengths: short (ending at mid-thigh), standard (ending just above the knee), and long (ending at or below the knee). The right length depends on your height, your style, and how you intend to wear the coat.

For taller clients, a longer coat creates a dramatic, elegant silhouette. For shorter clients, a standard or short length is more flattering. Our master tailor will help you choose the right length for your body during the consultation.

Lapel Style

The lapels of a trench coat are a defining feature. We offer several styles:

  • Notch Lapel: The most versatile and traditional. Works well for both business and casual wear.
  • Peak Lapel: More formal and dramatic. Ideal for a trench coat intended to be worn over a suit.
  • Ulster Collar: A wide, dramatic collar that can be turned up for maximum protection. The classic choice for a true trench coat.
  • Stand-and-Fall Collar: A modern interpretation, with a smaller standing collar and a softer fall. Cleaner and less formal.

Color

The classic trench coat colors are khaki, beige, and camel. These are timeless choices that work with any wardrobe. But we also offer a range of other colors, including navy, charcoal, olive, and deep burgundy—giving you the opportunity to create something more personal.

Buttons and Hardware

The buttons and hardware of a trench coat are the finishing details that set it apart. We offer a selection of solid brass, dark horn, and mother-of-pearl buttons, alongside a range of metal finishes for the D-rings and buckles (brass, gunmetal, or antique silver).

Monogram

For a truly personal touch, we offer a discreet monogram embroidered on the inside lining of the coat. Your initials, or the date of a special occasion, discreetly stitched into the garment—a private signature that only you know is there.

Pockets

The trench coat traditionally features a variety of pockets: two large front pockets, two side-entry pockets for your hands, and often a hidden interior pocket for valuables. We can customize the pocket layout to suit your needs—adding an interior pocket for a phone, a welt pocket for a wallet, or a special pocket for a passport.

5. How We Fit a Trench Coat Over a Suit

Fitting a trench coat is one of the most complex challenges in menswear. Unlike a suit jacket—which is worn directly over a shirt—a trench coat must accommodate a full suit jacket underneath, while still maintaining a sharp, flattering silhouette.

Most trench coats fail at this task. They are either too tight (making the suit underneath bulge and pull) or too loose (making the wearer look boxy and shapeless). A true bespoke trench coat solves this problem through careful drafting and multiple fittings.

The "Drop Shoulder" Adjustment

When a trench coat is worn over a suit, the extra bulk of the jacket's shoulders and sleeves pushes against the coat's shoulders, causing the coat to ride up and creating an awkward silhouette. To solve this, we draft the coat with a slightly wider shoulder and a more generous armhole, allowing the jacket to sit comfortably underneath without distortion.

The Waist Suppression

Even with extra room through the chest and shoulders, a trench coat should taper sharply at the waist. This is what gives the coat its elegant, sculpted silhouette. We achieve this by carefully drafting the coat with a nipped-in waist—but only after accounting for the bulk of the suit jacket underneath.

The Sleeve Pitch

The sleeves of a trench coat must hang naturally over the suit sleeves, with no twisting or bunching. We adjust the sleeve pitch during the basted fitting, ensuring that the sleeves drape smoothly and that the cuff shows just the right amount of shirt and jacket sleeve beneath.

The Length

The length of a trench coat must be carefully balanced. It should be long enough to cover the jacket, but not so long that it looks like a cape. For most clients, we recommend a length that ends just below the knee—allowing the jacket to be visible at the hem while preserving the dramatic sweep of the coat.

The Vent

Because a trench coat is worn over a suit, the vent must be properly cut to allow for comfortable walking, sitting, and driving. A poorly cut vent will cause the coat to pull and bunch when you move—an unmissable sign of a cheap garment.

7. How to Wear & Care for Your Trench Coat

A bespoke trench coat is an investment. With proper care, it will last for decades, aging gracefully and becoming more personal with every wear. Here is how to wear and care for your coat so it stays in pristine condition.

Wearing Your Trench Coat

The trench coat is one of the most versatile garments in menswear. It can be worn open, tied loosely at the waist, or belted tightly. For the sharpest look, we recommend the following:

  • Over a suit: Wear the coat open, with the belt tied in a simple knot at the waist. This creates a clean, elegant silhouette that shows off the suit underneath.
  • With a knit: Wear the coat belted for warmth and shape. The cinched waist contrasts beautifully with the bulk of the knit underneath.
  • Casual: Wear it open, with the collar popped and the sleeves pushed up slightly. This is the relaxed, effortless look that made the trench coat iconic.

Caring for Your Trench Coat

Ventile and gabardine are durable fabrics that can last for decades with proper care. To keep your coat in top condition:

  • Brush regularly: Use a soft clothes brush to remove dust and dirt after each wear.
  • Hang properly: Always hang your coat on a wide wooden hanger—never a wire one—to maintain the shape of the shoulders.
  • Spot-clean: For small stains, use a damp cloth with a mild soap solution. Blot—do not rub—and allow to air dry.
  • Dry-clean sparingly: Dry cleaning is safe for Ventile and gabardine, but it should be done sparingly—no more than once a year. Too much dry cleaning can strip the natural oils from the fibers.
  • Avoid heat: Never put your trench coat in a dryer, and avoid ironing directly onto the fabric. If pressing is needed, use a steam iron on low heat with a pressing cloth.
