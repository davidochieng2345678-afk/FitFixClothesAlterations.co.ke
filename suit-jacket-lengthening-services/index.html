The alteration with a hard physical limit

Why jacket lengthening is the alteration most tailors quietly refuse

Shortening a jacket is common work. Lengthening is not. The reason is simple: you can always remove fabric, but you can't add fabric that doesn't exist. If the original tailor left a hem allowance inside the jacket — extra cloth folded up into the hem when the jacket was made — we can let it down. If the allowance is gone, the only honest answer is to source matching cloth and add a visible extension that will never be truly invisible, or to tell the client the jacket cannot be lengthened. Most Nairobi tailors quote the job, take the money, and hand back a jacket with a seam running across the hem. That's not lengthening. That's damaging the jacket. We don't work that way.

The hem allowance — how much is really there

When a jacket is made, the tailor cuts the front and back panels slightly longer than the finished jacket, folds the excess up inside the hem, and stitches it in place. That folded fabric is the hem allowance. On a well-made suit, the allowance is usually 2 to 5 centimetres — enough to let the jacket down by up to one size if the wearer grows or the jacket is bought second-hand. On cheaply made jackets, the allowance is often 0 to 1.5 centimetres — barely anything, because the manufacturer was saving cloth. On vintage jackets, it's sometimes surprisingly generous because the original tailor expected the jacket to be let down as the wearer aged. We measure the allowance before we quote, always. If we can't see the inside hem, we ask the client for a photo of it before we agree to take the job. It's the single most important number on the whole quote.

What happens when the allowance is gone

If the allowance is gone, the only way to lengthen the jacket is to add a strip of matching cloth at the hem. This introduces a horizontal seam across the bottom of the jacket — sometimes visible, sometimes hidden by a flap, sometimes disguised as a design feature. On some jackets this works surprisingly well. On others it looks like what it is: an alteration. We tell clients honestly when a lengthening job is going to leave a visible seam and we tell them upfront, before taking the work. On plain wool or tweed, a matching extension can look close to invisible. On a jacket with a bold check, a pinstripe or an unusual weave, the extension will almost always show — and on those jackets the honest answer is often "this jacket shouldn't be lengthened, buy a new one."

Pattern matching — the invisible detail that decides the outcome

On any patterned cloth — a Prince-of-Wales check, a chalk stripe, a windowpane plaid — the extension has to align with the pattern of the jacket around it. If the pattern doesn't line up, the extension reads as a stripe or a block of different colour across the hem, even if the cloth is identical. Matching a check across a 3-centimetre extension is a slow, careful job that involves cutting the extension from a specific position in a specific piece of matching cloth. On a solid-colour jacket, this step doesn't apply and the job is quicker. On a patterned jacket, it's the single most time-consuming part of the work, and it's the reason we ask clients to send a close-up photo of the cloth before we quote.

Double-breasted jackets — the hardest of all

On a double-breasted jacket, the button stance sits at a specific height relative to the hem — usually about 8 to 12 centimetres above the bottom edge. If you lengthen the jacket without adjusting the button stance, the buttons end up sitting too high, which reads wrong on the wearer. If you move the buttons down to compensate, the buttonholes have to be reworked — which is hand-finishing work on a canvased jacket. On a fused double-breasted jacket, this can be done on a machine; on a canvased one, every buttonhole is hand-sewn. This is why double-breasted jacket lengthening costs more than a standard lengthening. It's also why we're cautious about taking the job on a jacket whose button stance doesn't allow the change — sometimes the honest answer is that the jacket is a wash.

Vent and lining — the two things that must be adjusted together

When you lengthen a jacket, the vent (the slit in the back that lets the jacket move) has to be lengthened too — otherwise the vent stops short of the new hem and the jacket looks wrong from behind. And the lining has to be adjusted — pulled down or extended so it doesn't show below the new hem line, and so the lining hem swing (the gap between the lining hem and the outer hem) stays at the correct 2 to 4 centimetres. On a fully lined jacket, this means opening the lining at the hem and re-anchoring it. On a half-lined jacket, the back seams need to be re-checked. Both jobs have to be done at the same time as the outer hem, in the correct order, or the jacket looks altered from every angle.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 3,500 lengthening job on a jacket with a healthy allowance is a rescue — the jacket fits, it looks factory, it goes back into rotation. The same KSh 3,500 on a jacket with no allowance and a bold check is money wasted, because the extension will be visible and the client won't wear the jacket. The worst outcome is not a refused job — it's a job that got taken because the client insisted, and a jacket that comes back looking altered. We turn down lengthening jobs every month. Not because the work is too hard, but because the honest answer is that the jacket can't be lengthened cleanly and we'd rather tell the client that than take their money for a result they won't like. It's the same standard we hold on suit alterations and blazer alterations.

Not all lengthening jobs are the same

Three lengthening tiers — three completely different techniques

A 2-centimetre let-down on a jacket with a healthy hem allowance and a 5-centimetre extension on a jacket with no allowance require completely different handling. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be ruined. Here's how we categorise lengthening jobs before deciding what technique to use.

Everyday tier

Small let-down — 1 to 3 cm from the allowance

A modest lengthening job where the original hem allowance is enough to cover the full extension. The jacket is opened at the hem, the fold is released, the new hem line is pressed in, and the lining is re-anchored. The outer fabric is untouched — no new cloth is added, and no seam appears at the hem. This is the cleanest, cheapest and most common lengthening job we do. Most clients who bring us a jacket "one size too short" fall into this tier.

What we do differentlyEven when the allowance is healthy, we don't just unfold it and stitch. The old press line has to be steamed out completely before the new hem is set — if the previous fold is still visible, it reads as a shadow across the jacket. We steam-press over a ham and let the cloth settle for 24 hours before finishing. This adds a day to the job and makes the difference between a hem that looks original and one that looks let down.

Everyday tier

Medium let-down plus partial fabric extension — 3 to 5 cm

The hem allowance covers part of the extension — say, 2 cm of the 4 cm needed — and the remaining 2 cm has to be made up with matching cloth. This job involves opening the hem, releasing the allowance, cutting a narrow strip of matching cloth, and setting it in as an extension at the very bottom of the hem. On a solid-colour jacket, this can be almost invisible if the cloth match is good. On a patterned jacket, the pattern has to be aligned across the extension, which is slower. Common on jackets where the client has grown slightly taller, or where a jacket was cut for a shorter body originally.

What we do differentlyWhen we source matching cloth for this tier, we source it against the jacket's current colour, not the original colour on the label. Wool fades. A jacket that was charcoal four years ago is now a slightly lighter charcoal, and new cloth of the original colour will be visibly darker. We may need to source from a small number of specialist cloth merchants, or — on cloth no longer in production — we take a hidden panel from inside the jacket (the inside hem of the vent, usually) and use that as the extension, hiding the missing panel where it doesn't show.

Premium tier

Full fabric extension — 5 cm+ with no allowance left

The hem allowance is gone — either the jacket was made with a minimal allowance or a previous tailor has already used it. Lengthening has to happen entirely through added cloth. This is the hardest and most expensive tier, because matching cloth and matching the pattern across a wide extension is genuinely difficult — and on a boldly patterned jacket, the honest answer is often that the job shouldn't be done at all. Common on vintage jackets with no allowance, or on jackets bought second-hand where the previous owner let them down before re-selling.

What we do differentlyOn this tier, we hand-finish everything. The extension is cut on the correct grain, matched against the existing pattern, and stitched with a hand-finished seam that mimics the original factory construction. We also hand-rework the vent, the lining hem swing and (on a double-breasted jacket) the button stance. This takes three to four days and, on a patterned jacket, may not achieve a truly invisible result — which we tell clients upfront, before starting.

If you're not sure which tier your jacket falls into, send a close-up photo of the inside hem (the bottom of the jacket where the lining meets the outer cloth) on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the allowance how much we can add and what technique applies — before you commit to anything.

The five parts of a jacket that affect the lengthening

What a lengthening job actually involves — piece by piece

Most people think of lengthening as "let the hem down." That description misses the four other parts of the jacket that change when the hem changes. Structurally, a lengthening job involves five parts of the jacket, and each one has to be adjusted correctly. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a jacket.

01

The hem allowance — how much cloth is actually available

The hem allowance is the folded cloth inside the hem of the jacket — the extra length the original tailor left for future adjustment. On a good suit, it's 2 to 5 centimetres. On cheaper jackets, it's often 0 to 1.5 centimetres. On vintage jackets, sometimes more. The allowance determines everything about the lengthening — the technique, the price and the outcome. We measure it before quoting and we ask the client to send a photo of the inside hem if they can't tell. There is no way around this step. A tailor who quotes a lengthening without seeing the allowance is guessing.

02

The outer hem seam — where the new bottom edge will sit

The outer hem seam is the visible bottom edge of the jacket. When you lengthen, the outer hem seam moves down — either by releasing the allowance or by adding an extension below it. On a solid-colour jacket, the new hem seam reads as the natural bottom of the jacket. On a patterned jacket, the new hem seam has to align with the pattern, which sometimes means the extension has to be positioned precisely to land on a specific point in the print. This is a slow measurement step and it's why patterned jackets cost more to lengthen than solid ones.

03

The vent — the back slit that has to move with the hem

Almost every suit jacket has a vent — a single centre vent or a pair of side vents — that lets the jacket move with the wearer when they sit and walk. The vent opening starts a specific distance above the hem and runs down to the hem. When you lengthen the jacket, the vent has to lengthen too, otherwise the vent stops short and the jacket looks wrong from behind. Vent adjustment is part of every lengthening job and can be fiddly on jackets with a deep vent or a functional button at the top of the vent.

04

The lining hem swing — the invisible detail nobody notices until it's wrong

The lining of a jacket stops 2 to 4 centimetres above the outer hem — that gap is called the "hem swing." It exists so the lining doesn't show below the jacket when you walk or sit. When you lengthen the outer hem, the lining also has to be adjusted — either re-anchored at the new height, or extended to match the new hem swing. If the lining isn't adjusted, it either drops below the outer hem (and shows) or stops too short inside the jacket (and pulls). This step is invisible when done right and immediately visible when done wrong — which is why we always open the lining at the hem during a lengthening job.

05

The button stance — the detail that decides double-breasted jackets

On a single-breasted jacket, the button stance rarely affects the lengthening — the buttons sit near the waist and moving them isn't usually necessary. On a double-breasted jacket, the button stance sits a specific distance above the hem — usually 8 to 12 centimetres — and lengthening the jacket without adjusting the stance leaves the buttons sitting too high. On a fused double-breasted jacket, the button stance can usually be shifted with machine-stitched buttonholes. On a canvased double-breasted jacket, the buttonholes have to be hand-reworked, which is a full afternoon of work on its own. This is why we sometimes advise against lengthening a double-breasted jacket even when the allowance is available.

If a tailor quotes a jacket lengthening without asking about the hem allowance, the vent or the lining hem swing, they're about to do a job that will look fine from the front and wrong from every other angle. We'd rather ask five questions upfront than take on a job we can't finish properly.

The cloth decides the outcome

The five suit cloths we lengthen most often — and how each one behaves

Lengthening is more sensitive to cloth than any other suit alteration, because the outcome depends on how well a new piece of cloth can be blended with the existing jacket. Here's our honest breakdown by cloth type, the same one we'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Solid worsted wool

The easiest cloth to lengthen. Solid-colour worsted wool is smooth, uniform and consistent across the panel — which means a new extension can be made from any part of a matching length of cloth and blend perfectly. The only challenge with worsted is that it holds a press line permanently. When the allowance is released, the old press line has to be steamed out completely or it shows as a shadow across the new hem. We steam-press over a ham and let the cloth settle for 24 hours before finishing. If the client has grown 2 to 3 centimetres and the allowance is healthy, this is a straightforward and very satisfying job.

Chalk stripe and pinstripe

Striped cloth adds a specific challenge to lengthening: the stripes have to run continuously through the extension. If the extension is cut with the stripe running at even a slightly different angle, or with a stripe that doesn't line up with the jacket's stripes, the extension reads immediately as a wrong piece of cloth. On chalk stripe and pinstripe cloth, we cut the extension from a specific position in a matching length of cloth so the stripes align perfectly. Getting this right sometimes means buying more matching cloth than we strictly need, so we can pick the right starting point. The trade-off is honest: better result, slightly higher material cost.

Checks and plaids

The hardest patterned cloth to lengthen. A Prince-of-Wales check, a windowpane check or a bold plaid has a repeating pattern in two directions — vertically and horizontally. Both have to align with the jacket's pattern across the extension. On a small check, this is manageable. On a large check, it's very difficult because the pattern repeat is often too large to fit across the extension at all. On some checked jackets, the honest answer is that a seamless extension is not achievable and the client should consider a different solution — a shorter jacket, a different style, or a new jacket. We tell clients this upfront rather than after taking the money.

Fresco, hopsack and open-weave cloth

Open-weave wool — fresco and hopsack — has a visible texture that makes matching an extension easier than on a tightly woven cloth, because the texture distracts the eye from small colour or weave differences. The trade-off is that open-weave cloth frays aggressively at a cut edge, so every raw edge on the extension has to be overlocked immediately. Fresco and hopsack also crease visibly — but that's a property of the cloth, not of the alteration. A lengthened fresco jacket will crease the same way a new one does, which is reassuring for clients worried the extension will look different from the rest of the jacket.

Flannel, tweed and brushed cloth

Brushed cloth — flannel, tweed, brushed wool — has a nap direction. The nap runs a specific way on the jacket, and any extension has to be cut and set with the nap running the same direction. If the nap direction is wrong, the extension looks lighter or darker than the surrounding cloth even if the colour and weave match exactly. We mark the nap direction on the jacket before cutting and orient the extension to match. Brushed cloth also has a softness that makes it forgiving on the press — small press marks disappear with a light steam, which is a small mercy on an alteration this fiddly.

What we won't lengthen

We turn down jacket lengthening jobs every month, and we'd rather do that than take money for a result the client won't like. On a boldly patterned jacket with a hem allowance of less than 1 centimetre, a seamless extension is usually not achievable and we tell clients to consider alternatives. On a jacket whose lining has already been replaced once (which usually means a previous tailor has been inside it and consumed the allowance), we're cautious — sometimes the previous work has compromised the hem and a lengthening job makes things worse. On a jacket where a previous tailor has already added an extension at the hem, we almost always refuse — a second extension on top of the first is genuinely ugly and there's nothing we can do about it. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money. It's the same standard we hold on re-lining services.

If you don't know what your jacket is made of, send a close-up photo of the cloth on WhatsApp. We'll identify the weave and tell you straight what's possible.

A short note from the bench

The hem allowance is the whole job — so we check it before we quote

Every week, a client brings me a suit jacket that's too short and asks me to lengthen it. Every week, I open the inside hem first — before I say a word about price — and I measure how much cloth is actually folded in there. That single measurement decides everything about the job. If the allowance is 2 to 5 centimetres, I can quote the jacket lengthening at KSh 3,500 and know the outcome will be clean. If the allowance is 0 to 1.5 centimetres, I have to tell the client honestly that the job will either require an extension (which may or may not be invisible, depending on the cloth) or it can't be done. There is no third option. Any tailor who tells you a jacket can be lengthened without checking the inside hem is either guessing or hoping you won't notice the seam.

I want to explain what hem allowance actually is, because most clients have never seen it. When a jacket is made, the tailor doesn't cut the front and back panels to their finished length. He cuts them longer — usually by 2 to 5 centimetres — then folds the excess up inside the jacket and stitches it into the hem. That folded cloth is invisible from the outside. It sits between the outer cloth and the lining. When you need to lengthen the jacket, we open the lining at the hem, release the fold, press the new hem line, and re-anchor the lining. The outer cloth is untouched. No seam appears anywhere. The jacket just gets longer, cleanly. This is what "letting down" means, and it's the reason a well-made suit jacket can be adjusted for years as the wearer's body changes.

Here's the problem: not every jacket has enough allowance. Cheap fused jackets from mass-market retailers often have almost none — sometimes less than a centimetre — because the manufacturer was saving cloth. Some fast-fashion suits have literally zero. On those jackets, the only way to lengthen is to add a new strip of cloth at the hem, which introduces a horizontal seam. On a solid-colour wool, that seam can be blended almost to invisibility if we find a good cloth match and align the grain. On a chalk stripe, the stripes have to run continuously through the new strip — which means cutting the extension from a specific position in a matching length of cloth so the stripes line up perfectly. On a bold Prince-of-Wales check or a large plaid, the pattern repeat is sometimes too large to fit across the extension at all, and the honest answer is that the jacket cannot be lengthened cleanly. That's not a limitation of my skill — it's a limitation of physics. You can't make cloth match when the pattern repeat is bigger than the strip you're adding.

There are two things that always surprise clients when we talk about lengthening a jacket. First, the lining hem swing. Everyone thinks about the outer hem when they think about lengthening. Very few think about the lining. The lining of a jacket stops 2 to 4 centimetres above the outer hem — that gap is called the hem swing, and it exists so the lining doesn't show below the jacket when you walk or sit. When we lengthen the outer hem, we have to adjust the lining hem swing too, or the lining either drops below the new hem (and shows) or stops too short inside the jacket (and pulls). This is invisible when it's done right and immediately visible when it's done wrong. Second — the vent. Almost every suit jacket has a vent at the back — a single centre vent or a pair of side vents — that lets the jacket move when you sit. When we lengthen the hem, the vent has to be re-set so its opening runs the correct distance from the hem. If we skip this step, the vent stops short of the hem and the jacket looks wrong from behind. Both of these are things I check on every lengthening job, without being asked.

The last thing I want clients to know is this: I turn down jacket lengthening jobs every month. Not because the work is too hard — I've been lengthening jackets for fourteen years — but because the honest answer for some jackets is that the job can't be done cleanly. If the allowance is gone and the cloth is a bold check, adding an extension is going to produce a visible band across the hem, and the client is going to be disappointed. I'd rather tell them that upfront than take their money for a result they won't like. I'd rather they buy a new jacket that fits and wear it every week, than pay me KSh 5,000 for a lengthening they'll stare at every time they put the jacket on. That's the standard — and it's the reason the clients who've been with me for years keep coming back.

Make the lengthened hem last

Eight habits that protect a lengthened jacket

A lengthened jacket is a jacket whose hem has been touched — either a released allowance or an added extension. Both are slightly more delicate than a factory hem, because the new press line is younger than the surrounding cloth. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect a lengthened jacket.

1. Give the new hem a week before the first wear

After a lengthening job, the cloth around the new hem needs a week to settle. The new press line softens over those first seven days, and the vent and lining hem swing settle into their new proportions. Wear the jacket briefly during that week — try it on twice, stand in it for a few minutes — so the cloth adjusts to being worn at the new length. Don't wear it to an event until that week is done. The difference in the final shape is small but real, and a jacket that goes straight into a wedding the day after collecting always feels slightly stiff at the hem.

2. Brush the hem before storing

The bottom of a jacket — the hem, the vent, the bottom of the side seams — collects dust from the ground, from car seats and from the underside of restaurant tables. That dust works into the new hem line and dulls the press. Brush the hem lightly with a soft clothes brush before the jacket goes back on the hanger. Two minutes per jacket, and it keeps the new hem looking clean and settled. This matters more on a lengthened jacket than a factory hem, because dust shows more against a fresh press line.

3. Steam, don't press — the new hem should never be ironed flat

Never press a lengthened jacket hem with a home iron. The new press line is the thing that defines the shape, and a flat iron will ruin it — pressing directly on the hem flattens the vent, distorts the lining hem swing, and leaves a shine mark on the wool. If the hem looks wrinkled, hang the jacket in a steamy bathroom for ten minutes. The steam relaxes the cloth without pressing it flat. If it needs a real press, bring it to us — we press over a ham, which is the only safe way to press a curved hem line on a jacket.

4. Hang on a wide shoulder-shaped hanger

After a lengthening job, the vertical balance of the jacket is slightly different — the jacket hangs a touch lower than it used to. On a thin wire hanger, the extra weight of the lengthened cloth concentrates at the shoulder and stretches the shape. On a wide wooden hanger with a shaped shoulder, the weight distributes evenly and the jacket hangs the way it's supposed to. Never hang a lengthened jacket from a single hook at the collar. A wide hanger costs KSh 500 and saves the shoulder of a jacket worth 100 times that.

5. Check the vent after every wear

The vent — the slit at the back of the jacket — is the seam closest to the new hem, and it takes the most movement stress. After every wear, glance at the vent: is the opening still crisp, or has the bottom corner started to fray or pull? A single loose thread at the vent corner is a KSh 500 repair today. Left for a month, it becomes a KSh 1,800 vent re-set. Vent damage is always cheapest to fix the moment it appears. This is especially important on a lengthened jacket, because the vent has just been re-set and the new stitching is younger than the rest of the jacket.

6. Rotate the jacket with your other suits

A lengthened jacket still benefits from the same rotation rule as any wool suit: 24 to 48 hours between wears. The cloth needs to recover its shape — moisture evaporates, fibres relax, the vent settles. If you wear the same lengthened jacket three days in a row, the new hem and the re-set vent never get recovery time. If you own another suit jacket, alternate. Two jackets worn alternately last roughly twice as long as one jacket worn daily — including the lengthened one.

7. Keep the hem dry — especially on brushed or flannel cloth

On brushed cloth — flannel, tweed, brushed wool — the new hem press line is more sensitive to moisture than the surrounding cloth. If the hem gets wet and dries without re-pressing, it can develop a slight waviness that reads as an older press line. Dry the jacket flat or on a wide hanger — never near a radiator, never in direct sun. If the hem does get soaked (walking in the rain, an accidental spill), let it dry naturally and then bring it to us for a steam. We'll re-set the press line without charge on any jacket we lengthened.

8. Fix small hem issues before they grow

A single stitch starting to loosen at the hem is a KSh 500 touch-up today. Left for six months, it becomes a KSh 1,500 hem re-press and re-anchor. Left longer, the hem can sag unevenly and the whole lengthening job has to be re-done. Same for the lining hem swing: a lining corner starting to drop is a KSh 400 fix today, a KSh 1,000 re-anchor if you wait. The clients whose lengthened jackets last the longest are the ones who message us the moment they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less.

Follow those eight habits and your lengthened jacket will hold its new hem line for a decade. And when it does eventually need work — a sleeve adjustment, a lining refresh, a vent repair — we're one WhatsApp message away.
