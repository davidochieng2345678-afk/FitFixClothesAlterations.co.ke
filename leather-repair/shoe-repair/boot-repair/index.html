What Makes Boot Repair Different From Shoe Repair

Boots take heavier loads than shoes. The leather is thicker, the sole is thicker, the stitching carries more weight and the shape of the boot is more rigid. When a boot fails it usually fails at a load point. The heel collapses because the heel block was never reinforced. The pull tab tears off because the boot is pulled on by the tab every single day. The eyelet panel splits because the laces are pulled too tight around it. The welt separates because the sole is carrying more weight than the original stitching was built for.

Boot repair needs to respect the way a boot works. A welted boot needs a welted resole. A cemented work boot needs a bonded and stitched resole. A Chelsea boot with a worn elastic gore needs the gore replaced without opening the boot. A hiking boot with a Vibram sole needs the correct Vibram compound so the boot keeps its grip on wet rock and mud.

At FitFix every boot is assessed for its construction and its load points before any work starts. David Ochieng has spent fourteen years handling leather footwear in Nairobi including welted boots, cemented work boots, Chelsea boots, chukka boots, hiking boots and riding boots. That experience is what keeps the repair strong enough to hold under the load the boot was built for.

Why Boots Fail Faster in Nairobi

Nairobi is not a harsh desert and it is not a tropical rainforest, but the combination of the two destroys boot leather and boot construction faster than most owners realise.

Strong UV at Altitude

Nairobi sits at altitude with strong midday sun for most of the year. Ultraviolet light breaks down the oils inside boot leather and dries out the waxes that hold the finish together. Boot leather that sees regular sun loses its suppleness and starts to crack at the flex point across the toe. Heel blocks harden and split. Sole edges crack and darken unevenly.

Repeated Wetting and Drying

The April, May, November and December rains soak boots through, then the sun dries them fast. This cycle pulls the oils out of the leather, hardens the boot shaft and causes the cracks that eventually split the leather along the flex point. On welted boots it weakens the welt stitching and starts the separation between the sole and upper. On cemented boots it softens the bond between the midsole and the outsole.

Dust and Fine Grit

Nairobi's dry months produce fine dust that works its way into every welt channel, stitch line and sole edge. Inside a welt that dust acts like sandpaper, grinding the stitching every time the boot flexes. On a work boot the same dust works into the eyelet panel and wears the lace holes oval over time. Regular brushing of the welt and eyelet panels makes a measurable difference to how long a boot lasts.

Mud, Gravel and Rough Surfaces

Boots see surfaces that shoes rarely touch. Gravel estate roads, mud paths, construction sites, wet grass and rocky trails all wear boot soles faster than tiled pavements wear shoe soles. Vibram style compound soles handle those surfaces far better than plain leather or thin rubber, which is why most of our boot resoles upgrade the sole material at the same time as replacing it.

Bad Previous Repairs

A significant portion of our boot work involves undoing previous repairs. Heel blocks fitted unevenly so the boot rolls outward. Pull tabs restitched with thread that is too thin. Elastic gores replaced with the wrong tension so the boot never closes correctly again. Soles glued instead of stitched on welted boots so the sole peels at the toe. Correcting these costs more than doing the job properly the first time, which is exactly why we do not cut corners.

Storage Without Boot Trees

Boots stored without boot trees lose their shape, particularly at the shaft and the heel counter. Once the leather has creased permanently at the flex point, the crease becomes the crack. A pair of boot trees is one of the cheapest forms of boot insurance you can buy, and for tall boots the difference is measurable within a single year.
