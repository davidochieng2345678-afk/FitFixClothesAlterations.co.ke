What a Resole Actually Involves

People imagine a resole as peeling off an old sole and gluing on a new one. In a proper workshop it is a sequence of at least nine steps, each of which changes the outcome of the finished shoe.

  1. Assessment and construction identification. David identifies whether the shoe is welted, cemented, Blake stitched or a hybrid. This decides the sole material, the attachment method and the price.
  2. Wear pattern reading. Where the sole wore thin first tells us how the owner walks. That influences how the new sole is cut, how the heel sits and how the shoe will feel after the resole.
  3. Removal of the old sole. The old sole is carefully removed without cutting into the midsole, the welt or the upper. Rough removal is where a lot of kiosk jobs go wrong.
  4. Midsole preparation. The exposed midsole is cleaned, levelled and prepped so the new sole bonds flat and sits level with no trapped air.
  5. Structural correction. Any collapsed midsole, torn welt, split heel seat or lifted insole board is corrected before the new sole goes on. Skipping this is why some resoles squeak or roll.
  6. Sole cutting and shaping. The new sole is cut to the right thickness, trimmed to the shoe outline, bevelled at the edges and shaped at the waist so it matches the original look.
  7. Attachment. The sole is bonded with footwear grade contact adhesive and, depending on construction, reinforced with stitching around the welt or through the sole. Both methods are used together on the pairs that need to hold longest.
  8. Edge finishing. The cut edge of the new sole is burnished, waxed or painted so it looks clean and holds up to water and dust.
  9. Finishing and quality check. Sole thickness is checked, heel heights are compared, edges are blended into the upper and the pair is polished and returned ready to wear.

Each of those steps is what separates a KES 1,500 resole that lasts three years from a KES 500 resole that fails in three months. You are not paying for the piece of sole material. You are paying for the sequence of decisions that make the sole sit correctly, wear evenly and hold through Nairobi heat and rain.

Sole Materials We Use and When We Use Them

Not every shoe wants the same sole. A welted Italian dress shoe and a pair of chunky work boots need completely different sole materials, and matching the material to the shoe is a large part of what makes a resole feel right.

Leather Soles

Leather is the traditional sole for welted dress shoes and for Italian leather footwear that wants a smart, slim profile. It breathes, moulds to the foot over time and looks correct on an Oxford or a fine Derby. It wears faster than rubber, needs occasional conditioning to stop drying, and is not the ideal choice for daily wear in heavy Nairobi rain. Best for dress shoes, weddings, formal events and shoes that rotate rather than being worn every day.

Rubber Soles

Rubber gives grip, water resistance and longer wear. It is the sensible choice for shoes that are worn daily, for work shoes and for anything that sees Nairobi roads, estates and pavements. A good rubber sole can be stitched the same way as a leather sole so the shoe keeps its welted feel while gaining durability. We use rubber on Derbies, loafers, casual shoes, walking shoes and work shoes.

Vibram Style Compound Soles

Vibram and Vibram style compounds are engineered for maximum grip and abrasion resistance. They are heavier than plain rubber, hold their tread pattern longer and handle wet and rough surfaces better. Ideal for hiking boots, chunky leather boots, Chelsea boots worn outdoors, and safety boots where grip matters. A stitched Vibram style resole is the longest wearing option we offer.

Combination Soles

A combination sole puts leather at the waist and heel of the shoe for a smart profile and rubber at the forefoot for grip and durability. This is common on higher end dress shoes and on shoes used for both office and street. It gives you the look of leather with the practical grip of rubber where you actually need it.

Crepe and Microcellular Rubber

Crepe and microcellular soles are soft, quiet, cushioned and light. Common on desert boots, chukka boots and casual leather shoes. They wear faster than solid rubber but feel comfortable and are the correct choice when a shoe was built for that specific look and feel.

Dainite Style Studded Rubber

Dainite and Dainite style studded rubber soles are the classic English dress shoe grip sole. Low profile, smart, water resistant and hard wearing. A common choice for brogues, Derbies and country style shoes where the look must stay formal but grip must be practical.

Signs Your Shoes Need a Resole and Signs They Do Not

Not every worn sole needs a full resole. Some shoes only need a sole reattachment, a heel top, a half sole or a polish. Others need the full rebuild. Knowing the difference helps you spend the right amount and helps us quote accurately from your photo.

Signs a Full Resole Is Needed

  • The sole has worn through and you can see the midsole or, worse, feel water coming through.
  • The sole has cracked along the flex point where the shoe bends.
  • The sole has hardened to the point where it no longer grips and feels plasticky underfoot.
  • The sole edge is crumbling or flaking away.
  • The sole is a thin pad and the shoe has never had a proper full sole under it.
  • The sole has separated along one or both sides past the point where re bonding alone will hold.

Signs a Partial Repair Will Do

  • The sole has lifted at the toe or the side but the rest of the sole is still sound. A re bond and re stitch usually does it.
  • The sole is fine but the heel top piece has worn through. That is a heel top replacement, not a resole.
  • The sole is fine but the shoe squeaks. That is often a trapped air pocket or a loose layer that can be corrected without a full sole change.
  • The sole is fine but the shoe is scuffed and dull. That is a polish and colour restoration, not a resole.

If you are not sure which applies to your shoes, send a photo of the sole from two angles plus a photo of the shoe from the side. David will tell you which category the shoe falls into and what the honest options are. There is no charge for that assessment and no pressure to proceed.

Why Resoling in Nairobi Is Tougher Than People Realise

Nairobi is not a harsh desert and it is not a tropical rainforest, but the combination of the two is what destroys shoe soles faster than most owners expect.

Strong UV at Altitude

Nairobi sits at altitude with strong midday sun for most of the year. Ultraviolet light dries out leather, cracks rubber and hardens the waxes and oils inside a sole. A pair of shoes left in the boot of a car through a Nairobi summer will come back with a sole that has gone hard and brittle. That is why we prefer penetrating conditioners and protective waxes over surface shine alone.

Heavy Seasonal Rain

The April, May, November and December rains soak shoes thoroughly and repeatedly. Water works into the welt, the stitching channels and the gap between the sole and the midsole. If the sole has been glued with cheap adhesive, the water gets in, freezes the glue at its weakest point and starts the peel that leads to a detached sole. Properly bonded and stitched soles handle this without issue.

Dust and Fine Grit

Nairobi's dry months produce fine dust that works its way into every seam, welt and edge. Inside a shoe sole that dust acts like sandpaper, accelerating wear along the stitching and between sole layers. Regular brushing and a proper edge waxing routine make a measurable difference.

Pavement and Estate Surfaces

Nairobi walking includes a mix of smooth tiled pavements, rough concrete, gravel estate roads and everything in between. That mix wears soles unevenly, with the outside back corner of the heel usually the first to go. That is why a resole at FitFix accounts for the walk pattern rather than simply matching the shape of the old sole.

Bad Previous Resoles

A large portion of our resoling work involves undoing previous repairs. Half glued rubber pads sitting on top of an old sole. Heel blocks fitted unevenly so the shoe rolls outward. Stitching that pulled through because the welt was already damaged and was not repaired first. Nails driven into a sole where a rivet belonged. Correcting these costs more than doing the job properly the first time, which is exactly why we do not cut corners.

What Affects the Final Resoling Price

Five things move the final figure up or down from the starting price.

  • Shoe construction. A welted shoe takes longer to resole than a cemented shoe because the welt has to be respected or rebuilt during the process.
  • Sole material. Leather is cheaper than rubber. Rubber is cheaper than Vibram style compound. Combination soles sit between leather and Vibram because of the two materials involved.
  • Condition of the midsole and welt. If the midsole is sound, the job is straightforward. If the welt has split or the midsole has collapsed, additional repair time is added.
  • Shoe size. Larger shoes use more sole material and more edge work. A pair of work boots uses more material than a pair of women's loafers.
  • Additional work required. Heel replacement, colour restoration, stitching repair and lining work can be added to the same resole if the shoe needs it. Each is priced separately and quoted before starting.

Materials That Hold Up in Nairobi Conditions

Nairobi is hard on footwear. Products designed for mild European climates do not always survive here. Here is what we use and why.

  • Footwear grade contact adhesives. Formulated to stay flexible through heat and cold, not the brittle all purpose glue that cracks within months.
  • Bonded nylon and waxed polyester thread. Holds tension, resists abrasion, does not rot like cotton when it gets wet.
  • Genuine leather, rubber and Vibram style compound sole material. Correct thickness, correct hardness, correct grip for the shoe and the way you walk.
  • Penetrating leather conditioners and dyes. Feed the leather and move with it, unlike surface paints that crack the first time the shoe flexes.
  • Edge paints and burnishing waxes. Seal cut edges so they stop absorbing dirt and water and stop darkening unevenly.
  • Matched hardware. Eyelets, hooks, buckles and rivets in brass, nickel, gunmetal and antique finishes, matched to what the shoe already carries.

Stitched, Not Just Glued

The single biggest reason a resole fails in Nairobi is that it was glued only. Glue alone does not hold through the combined stress of heat, rain, dust and walking. A properly stitched resole uses adhesive to bond the sole flat and stitching to lock it in place, so even if the adhesive eventually softens the stitching keeps the sole attached.

On welted shoes we stitch around the welt. On cemented shoes we stitch through the sole where the construction allows. On boots we stitch through the full thickness of the sole. It takes more time, it uses more thread and it is the reason a FitFix resole holds through Nairobi conditions.

Practical Shoe Care You Can Do at Home

You can extend the time between resoles with simple habits. None of these require special equipment and all of them make a measurable difference in Nairobi conditions.

  • Brush the sole edge and welt after every wear. Dust between the sole and the welt accelerates wear and shortens resole life.
  • Wipe rain off promptly. When leather gets wet, blot it with a dry cloth and let it dry slowly. Do not rub, because rubbing pushes dirt into the grain.
  • Condition leather soles twice a year. A light application of leather conditioner in January and again in July keeps the sole from drying out and cracking along the flex point.
  • Rotate your shoes. Never wear the same pair of leather shoes two days in a row. Twenty four hours of rest lets moisture escape and the leather recover its shape.
  • Use shoe trees or paper. Cedar shoe trees absorb moisture and hold shape. Rolled newspaper works reasonably well if trees are not available.
  • Dry away from direct heat. Never dry wet leather shoes on a radiator, in an oven or in direct sun. Let them dry slowly at room temperature.
  • Keep the heel top piece from wearing through. A heel top replacement at KES 600 is far cheaper than a full heel rebuild at KES 800 or a resole at KES 1,500.
  • Use a shoe horn. A shoe horn saves the heel counter from being crushed every time the shoe goes on.
