Two Straps, One Body

A backpack is the only bag that is worn on the body all day. Every other bag is carried in one hand or slung over one shoulder. A backpack hangs on two shoulders with the full weight of the contents distributed across a webbing and padding system that has to mould to the shape of the back, breathe with the body, and hold a load that swings against the wearer every time they walk, run, cycle, board a matatu or sit down. That is a completely different set of demands from a briefcase, a laptop bag, a messenger bag or a travel duffel, and it produces a completely different set of failures.

The first failure point on almost every backpack is the shoulder strap anchor. The strap runs from the top of the backpack, over the shoulder, down the chest and under the arm, and it is anchored at the top back panel and at the bottom of the bag through a webbing tab or a D ring. Those two anchor points take the full weight of the backpack every time it is picked up, put down or shifted. On a loaded bag that is two hundred grams of webbing holding several kilos of contents, and the anchors concentrate that load into a very small area of stitching. That is why the top anchor almost always fails first, and why the failure looks like the webbing has torn away from the seam rather than the webbing itself having broken.

The second failure point is the bottom panel. A backpack sits down on the ground every time the wearer sets it down, and it sits on floors, pavements, tarmac, bus seats, cafe tiles and the floor of the matatu. That constant set down abrades the base of the backpack and the bottom corners faster than any other part of the bag. Over a year of school or a year of commuting, the base panel takes hundreds of impacts and the corner seams take hundreds of set downs. That is why the base and the lower corners are the second common failure point.

The third failure point is the back panel. The back panel of a backpack is the padded panel that sits against the wearer's back. It has foam inside it that has to breathe, mould to the wearer's back, and stay in place across years of use. When the foam collapses from years of sweat and pressure, the backpack loses its shape and the foam starts to shift inside the panel. When the panel fabric tears, the foam comes out. When the foam has gone, the backpack no longer sits correctly on the back and the shoulder straps start to slide.

The fourth failure point is the zip. A backpack has at least three zips on the main compartment, front pocket and side pockets. The main compartment zip is usually the longest zip on the whole bag and takes the most strain because it is what closes a full pack. A backpack that has been packed to the limit on a school run or a hiking trip has been forced to close against the pressure of the contents, and that puts tension on the zip tape and slider that eventually leads to failure.

Everything else fails too, but those four zones fail first. That is why our repair process starts with assessing all four before quoting. A repair that fixes only the visible failure and leaves the other three understrength will come back with the next failure within a few months.

The Internal Construction of a Backpack

A backpack is not just a shell with straps attached. It is a layered construction of at least eight components, and each one has a different job. When a backpack comes to us for repair, we check all eight.

The outer shell is the visible leather, nylon, polyester or canvas. On leather backpacks this is usually a full grain hide on the main panels with lighter leather or fabric on the sides. On nylon and polyester backpacks the shell is a heavy duty woven material, often with a water resistant coating. On canvas backpacks the shell is a heavy cotton canvas. The shell takes the abrasion, the rain, the sun and the visual wear. On a backpack the shell also has to bend with the wearer's body every time they move, which is why a backpack shell is often softer than the shell on a briefcase.

The internal frame or stiffener is what holds the backpack in its shape. On soft backpacks this is a lightweight internal frame sheet behind the back panel. On load bearing backpacks this is a shaped aluminium or plastic stay that runs from the top of the backpack down to the hip belt and transfers load from the shoulders to the hips. The frame is what stops the backpack collapsing into a soft bag when it is set down and what keeps the shoulder straps in the correct position on the shoulders.

The shoulder straps are the primary load bearing components. Each strap runs from the top anchor point behind the shoulder down across the chest and back to a lower anchor point on the side of the backpack. The straps are padded at the shoulder, usually with foam inside a woven fabric cover, and they are usually adjustable through a slider above the sternum strap. The shoulder strap anchor points are where the load concentrates most.

The sternum strap is the small strap that crosses the chest between the two shoulder straps. It stops the straps from sliding off the shoulders and it distributes some of the load. Sternum straps fail more often than people realise because the buckle is small and takes a lot of tension against a moving chest.

The hip belt is the strap that wraps around the wearer's hips, usually found on hiking and travel backpacks. On a load bearing backpack the hip belt transfers most of the weight from the shoulders to the hips. On smaller daypacks the hip belt is just a strap that helps stabilise the load. The hip belt anchors are the same kind of stress point as the shoulder strap anchors and fail the same way.

The back panel is the padded panel that sits against the wearer's back. It is usually a foam panel with a mesh or woven fabric cover, sometimes with a mesh raised design to let air move through. The back panel takes the sweat, the pressure and the movement, and its foam and cover are the two components that age fastest.

The base panel is the bottom of the backpack. It takes the set down impact every time the bag is put on the ground. On most backpacks the base panel is a heavier material than the rest of the shell, often with reinforced corner panels. On leather backpacks the base is usually a single heavy leather panel. On nylon backpacks the base is usually a heavier nylon or a leather reinforcement panel.

The zips and hardware are what let the backpack open and close. A typical backpack has a main compartment zip, one or two front pocket zips, one or two side pocket zips and sometimes a top pocket zip. The hardware includes the zip sliders, the shoulder strap sliders, the sternum strap buckle, the hip belt buckle and the D rings or loop attachments. Every one of those pieces can fail independently, and each one has a different replacement part and a different repair method.

The interior lining is the fabric inside the backpack. It takes the abrasion of everything that goes in and out of the bag, from books to a laptop to a water bottle to keys. On well built backpacks the lining is a smooth, tough fabric that resists tearing. On cheaper backpacks it is a lighter fabric that tears more easily at the seams.

Every one of those eight components can fail. When a backpack comes to us for repair, we check all eight. Often two or three have failed at the same time and the customer only noticed the last one, because that is the one that finally made the backpack unusable. Our job is to fix the whole set so the backpack does not come back three months later with the next failure on the list.

Why Backpacks Fail Faster in Nairobi

Nairobi is hard on backpacks in a way that surprises people who have carried the same bag in other cities.

Sweat and Body Heat

Nairobi is warm for most of the year, and a backpack sits directly against the wearer's back for hours at a time. The back panel, the shoulder strap undersides and the hip belt all absorb sweat on every wear. Salt from sweat is abrasive against foam and against stitching. Over months of daily wear it breaks down the foam inside the back panel, eats through the padding inside the shoulder straps, and weakens the stitching that holds the webbing to the shell. Backpacks worn by school children and by commuters are the ones we see the highest volume of sweat damage on.

Heavy Loads on Two Shoulders

A backpack is loaded more heavily than almost any other bag. School backpacks routinely carry five to eight kilos of books. Commuter backpacks carry a laptop, charger, water bottle, notebook and lunch. Hiking backpacks carry fifteen to twenty kilos on multi day trips. That load is concentrated onto two shoulder strap anchors and, on hiking backpacks, onto two hip belt anchors. The load is heavier than the original design assumed on most bags, and the anchors take the strain.

Repeated Wetting and Drying

The April, May, November and December rains soak backpacks on the walk to school, the walk to the matatu stop and the walk from the car park. Water sits in the base seam, in the zip channels and behind the shoulder strap anchors where it dries slowly. Each wet and dry cycle pulls the oils out of leather and weakens the coating on nylon and polyester. The stitching that holds the backpack together weakens with every wet and dry cycle, and the base of the backpack is where water sits longest.

Fine Dust and Road Grit

Nairobi's dry months produce fine dust that works its way into every zip channel, every webbing weave and every seam. Inside a zip channel that dust acts like sandpaper, grinding the slider against the teeth every time the bag is opened. In the webbing of the shoulder straps that dust works into the weave and dulls the surface. Around the base of the backpack the dust settles into the stitching and the corner panels, and stays there if the bag is not brushed after use.

Set Down on Any Surface

A backpack is set down dozens of times a day. School corridors, class floors, bus seats, matatu floors, cafe tiles, pavement kerbs, car boots, office carpets. Each set down puts the full weight of the loaded backpack onto the base panel and onto the two bottom corners. Over a school year that is thousands of set downs, and the base panel and corners take all of them. That is why the base is the second most common failure point after the strap anchors.

Bad Previous Repairs

A significant portion of our backpack work involves undoing previous repairs done elsewhere. Shoulder strap anchors re stitched with cotton thread that has rotted. Webbing reattached with glue only, without stitching or reinforcement. Zips replaced with the wrong gauge hardware so the slider catches. Base panels patched with fabric that does not match and comes loose within weeks. Correcting these costs more than doing the job properly the first time, which is exactly why we do not cut corners.

Storage Without Support

A backpack stored empty and folded flat for months loses its shape. The internal frame sheet deforms, the back panel foam compresses, the shoulder straps go soft at the anchors. A backpack stored with weight inside it, or squashed under other luggage, can take a permanent set that is difficult to reverse. Storage matters for a backpack because the shape of the back panel is part of what makes the bag sit correctly on the shoulders.
