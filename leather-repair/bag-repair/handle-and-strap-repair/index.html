The Part That Always Fails First

Ask anyone who has thrown a bag away and they will tell you the same story. The leather was fine. The zip was fine. The lining was fine. The bag was still holding its shape. What failed was the handle, the strap or the point where the handle or strap attached to the bag. That is what happens to almost every bag that dies before its time. The load bearing part fails, the rest of the bag is still good, and the owner assumes the bag is finished when the actual problem is one section of stitching or one anchor point.

That is why we treat handle and strap repair as its own specialist service, separate from bag repair in general. Rebuilding a handle is not the same as restitching a seam. Replacing a shoulder strap is not the same as fixing a zip. Getting a handle or strap to hold the weight it was designed to carry, without failing at the same point six months later, needs a specific approach that most repair shops do not bother with.

The reason handles and straps fail is not complicated. Every bag has two or three load bearing points, and those points take ten to a hundred times more stress than the rest of the bag. On a briefcase the load bearing point is the handle anchor. On a laptop bag it is the handle anchor and the shoulder strap anchor. On a backpack it is the shoulder strap anchors and the hip belt anchors. On a messenger bag it is the crossbody strap anchor. On a travel duffel it is the top handle anchors and the shoulder strap anchors. On a handbag it is the hand carry handle anchors and, where relevant, the shoulder strap anchor.

Every one of those anchor points was engineered for a specific load and a specific type of movement. A briefcase handle is engineered for a load that hangs vertically and swings sideways when the wearer walks. A backpack shoulder strap is engineered for a load that hangs on both shoulders and bounces with the walk. A messenger bag strap is engineered for a load that swings across the body. A duffel strap is engineered for a load that gets dragged, lifted and set down repeatedly. Each of those demands is different, and each one produces a different failure pattern.

The Anatomy of a Bag Handle

To understand why handles fail, it helps to understand what a handle actually is. A bag handle is not just a strip of leather or fabric lying across the top of the bag. It is a multi-layer construction with at least four distinct components stitched together.

The visible outer layer is what you see when you look at the handle. On a leather bag it is the finished leather. On a fabric bag it is the visible webbing or the finished fabric. On a padded handle it is the leather or fabric cover that wraps the whole handle. The outer layer takes the abrasion from the wearer's hand and from contact with surfaces.

The internal core is what gives the handle its structure. On a rolled leather handle the core is usually a strip of stiffer leather, a strip of vulcanised fibre board or a length of dense foam. On a padded handle the core is a foam cylinder. On a webbing handle the core is usually the webbing itself folded and stitched. The core is what makes the handle hold its shape and what stops it collapsing when the bag is empty.

The anchor tabs are the pieces at each end of the handle that attach the handle to the bag. On a leather bag the anchor tabs are usually strips of leather folded under the top panel and stitched through. On a fabric bag the anchor is a webbing tab that is folded and stitched to the bag interior. On many bags the anchor is also reinforced with rivets, with a metal D ring or with a metal loop that the handle passes through. The anchor is where the load concentrates most, and the anchor is where failures start.

The reinforcement is what stops the anchor pulling out. Reinforcement can be a plastic or metal insert sewn inside the anchor tab, a double or triple row of stitching through the anchor, a rivet at each corner of the anchor, or a bar tack across the top of the anchor. On cheaply made bags the anchor often has minimal reinforcement, which is exactly why those bags fail earlier than the leather or fabric itself.

Every one of those four components can fail. When you send us a photo of a torn handle, we are looking at which of the four has failed and how the load was being carried at the moment of failure. A handle that has pulled out at the anchor usually has a failed reinforcement. A handle that has frayed along its length usually has a failed outer layer. A handle that has gone soft and floppy usually has a failed core. Each failure needs a different repair, and doing the wrong repair leaves the handle to fail again.

The Anatomy of a Bag Strap

A bag strap has a similar set of components but with a few important differences.

The visible strap itself is usually leather, webbing, nylon or a leather and fabric combination. The strap material has to be strong enough to carry the load, flexible enough to move with the wearer, and soft enough not to cut into the shoulder. This is why a good shoulder strap is usually wider at the shoulder and narrower at the anchor, and why webbing straps are usually wider than leather straps of the same load capacity.

The padding is what stops the strap cutting into the shoulder. On a padded shoulder strap the padding is a foam cylinder or a foam panel covered in a fabric sleeve. The padding takes the pressure and the sweat of every wear, and its foam is the component that ages fastest.

The buckle or slider is what lets the strap adjust and what holds it at the correct length. On a shoulder strap the slider is usually a metal or plastic piece on the top section of the strap. On a sternum strap or a hip belt the buckle is usually a plastic side release buckle. On a crossbody strap the buckle is usually a metal or plastic slider with a locking mechanism.

The anchors are the same as on a handle. They are the leather tabs, webbing tabs, D rings or metal loops that attach the strap to the bag. The anchors take the full load of the bag every time the strap moves, and the anchors are where strap failures start.

Finally there is the stitching. Every strap and every handle is held together by stitching, and the stitching is where the load is transferred from the bag to the handle or strap. When a handle or strap fails at a seam, it is almost always the stitching that has failed, not the leather or the webbing. That is why every repair we do reinforces the stitching as well as the material.

Why Handles and Straps Fail Faster in Nairobi

Nairobi is hard on the parts of a bag that take the load, in a way that surprises people who have carried the same bag in other cities.

Strong UV at Altitude

Nairobi sits at altitude with strong midday sun for most of the year. A handle and a strap are the two parts of a bag that see the most sun, because they are the parts that sit outside the bag's body when it is carried. The top of a briefcase handle and the shoulder section of a laptop bag strap dry out from UV faster than any other part of the bag. That is why a leather strap almost always shows its first cracks along the shoulder and why a top handle almost always cracks along the grip before anywhere else.

Sweat and Salt

Sweat is abrasive against leather, webbing and foam. A handle is held in a hand that sweats. A shoulder strap sits against a shoulder that sweats. A backpack strap sits across a chest and over two shoulders that sweat all day. Over a year of daily use, sweat and salt work into the material, dry it out, break down the surface finish and eventually crack the leather or fray the webbing. Handles and straps worn by Nairobi commuters and school children are the ones we see the highest volume of sweat damage on.

Repeated Wetting and Drying

The April, May, November and December rains soak handles and straps on the walk from the car or matatu to the office, school or house. Water sits in the folds of a leather strap and in the weave of a webbing strap where it dries slowly. Each wet and dry cycle pulls the oils out of the leather or weakens the coating on the webbing. A strap that has been caught in the rain repeatedly shows its first failures at exactly the fold points and the anchor points.

Load Concentration

A handle or strap anchor is a very small area of material that has to take the full weight of a loaded bag. On a briefcase the entire weight of the case is transferred through two anchor tabs that may be no more than two centimetres wide each. On a laptop bag the entire weight of the bag is transferred through two shoulder strap anchors that are similar in size. When the bag is loaded heavier than the anchor was designed for, the anchor sees a load it cannot hold, and it fails earlier than the bag itself. This is why overpacked bags fail at the anchors first.

Fine Dust and Grit

Nairobi's dry months produce fine dust that works its way into every stitch line and every webbing weave. Inside a stitch line that dust acts like sandpaper, grinding the thread against the leather or fabric every time the handle or strap flexes. In a leather strap the dust dries the surface further and dulls the finish. In a webbing strap the dust works into the weave and stays there, softening the fabric.

Bad Previous Repairs

A significant portion of our handle and strap work involves undoing previous repairs done elsewhere. Handles reattached with glue only, without rivets or stitching. Strap anchors re stitched with cotton thread that has rotted within months. Buckles replaced with the wrong sized hardware so the strap slips. Padding replaced with foam that is too thin or too soft. Correcting these costs more than doing the job properly the first time, which is exactly why we do not cut corners.

Storage Without Support

A bag stored hanging by one handle from a hook for months can take a permanent set at the handle anchor. A strap stored folded over a hook stretches at the fold. A padded handle stored squashed under other luggage compresses permanently. Storage matters for handles and straps more than for any other part of a bag, because they are the parts that take the load and the parts that have to sit correctly on the wearer.
