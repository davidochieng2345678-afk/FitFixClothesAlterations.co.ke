A Travel Bag Is Built to Lose

No other leather item in a person's life is expected to survive what a travel bag survives. A leather jacket is worn on a body that protects it. A briefcase sits under an arm in an office. A wallet lives in a pocket. A travel bag is thrown into the hold of a Kenya Airways flight, dragged across airport floors by one handle at an angle, sat on at the gate, squeezed into an overhead locker by a stranger, checked into a bus cargo bay, strapped to a boda boda rack, lifted by whoever grabs the nearest strap and thrown into the back of a matatu boot. It is expected to survive all of that and come back the same shape and colour it went out with, carrying twenty kilos of contents that themselves change the weight distribution of the bag every trip.

There is no other piece of leather gear that sees this kind of violence repeatedly over years. That is why travel bags fail more often than any other item we work on, and it is why the failures happen in specific places.

A travel bag has three load bearing zones that take almost all the stress, and those three zones almost always fail first. The first is the top handles and their anchors. When a bag is picked up out of the hold, or off a luggage carousel, or out of a matatu boot, it is grabbed by whichever handle is nearest and lifted at an angle. That concentrates the entire weight of a loaded bag on a single anchor point on one side, and the anchor has to be strong enough to hold that load while the bag swings to a vertical position. Repetition of that single movement kills anchors faster than anything else on a bag.

The second load bearing zone is the shoulder strap anchor. Even for bags that have a shoulder strap in addition to the top handles, the shoulder strap is what the wearer uses most in Nairobi. It is used to carry the bag on one shoulder through the airport, up and down stairs, along the terminal and through the parking lot. That means the entire loaded weight of the bag swings from two anchor points on either side of the bag, and the anchors have to take the load of a bag that is bouncing against the wearer's hip every step they take. The anchor and the strap near the anchor are the second point of failure.

The third load bearing zone is the bottom panel and its seams. Whenever a bag is set down, the bottom takes the full impact and the full weight of the contents. Over a year of travel, the bottom panel sees hundreds of set downs on hard airport tile, concrete bus parks, metal luggage carousel plates and car boots. The seams along the bottom perimeter are the third point of failure, and they fail with a split along the seam, not with a tear in the middle of the panel.

Everything else on a travel bag fails too, but those three zones fail first. That is why our repair process focuses on assessing all three before quoting. A repair that fixes only the visible failure and leaves the other two understrength will come back with the next failure within a few trips.

The Internal Construction of a Travel Bag

A travel bag or duffel is a layered construction of at least seven components, and each one has a job. When a bag comes to us, we check all seven.

The outer shell is the visible leather, canvas, nylon or waxed cotton. On leather duffels this is usually a heavy full grain hide. On canvas travel bags it is a heavy cotton canvas or nylon. On premium travel bags it is often a mix of leather panels and heavy fabric. The shell takes the abrasion, the rain, the sun, and every impact of every set down.

The internal frame or stiffener panel is what holds the bag in its shape. On soft duffels this is a light internal base panel. On wheeled travel bags this is a heavier frame with a molded base and a telescoping handle tube. The frame is what stops the bag collapsing into a puddle when it is set down on a floor, and it is what takes the load when the bag is dragged by the wheels.

The top handles are the primary load bearing components. On a duffel, the top handles are usually two parallel straps with a wrap joining them into a single grip. The handles are anchored to the sides of the bag at four points, and those anchors are reinforced with rivets and stitching. On a wheeled travel bag the top handles are usually a single padded grip with a wider anchor base.

The shoulder strap is the secondary load bearer. It is usually a wide strap that clips to two D rings or two metal loops on either side of the bag. Some travel bags have shoulder straps that are permanently sewn into the side panels; others have clip on straps that can be removed. Both arrangements put the load on the anchor point, which is the strongest part of the strap and the weakest part of the bag.

The bottom panel is what takes the set down impact. On most leather duffels this is a single heavy leather panel. On canvas bags it is a heavier canvas panel. On wheeled bags it is usually a molded base with corner protectors and a wheel housing.

The zips are what hold the bag closed. Travel bags usually have a single long main zip that runs across the top of the bag, sometimes with a protective flap that covers the zip. Some travel bags have two zips for a double compartment, or a shoe pocket zip on the side. The main zip takes the most load because it is what gets packed to the limit and what gets forced closed on a full bag.

The lining is the interior fabric. It takes the abrasion of everything that goes in and out of the bag, from a laptop to shoes to a water bottle to a charger block. On premium travel bags the lining is a smooth fabric like ripstop nylon that resists tearing. On cheaper bags it is a lighter fabric that tears more easily.

Every one of those seven components can fail. When a travel bag comes to us, we check all seven. Often two or three have failed at the same time and the customer only noticed the last one, because that is the one that finally made the bag unusable. Our job is to fix the whole set so the bag does not come back three months later with the next failure on the list.

Why Travel Bags Fail Faster in Nairobi

Nairobi is hard on travel bags in a way that surprises people who have carried the same bag in other cities.

Rough Handling in Public Transport

Travel bags in Nairobi go through matatus, buses, boda bodas, tuk tuks, taxis, hotel shuttle vans and the boot of private cars. On any of these a travel bag is often thrown into a luggage compartment by whoever is helping load it, pulled out by a single handle at an angle and handed over to the owner without any care for how the bag is being held. That kind of handling is much rougher than a typical airport check in, and the anchors take a disproportionate share of the strain.

Fine Dust and Road Grit

Nairobi's dry months produce fine dust that works its way into every seam, every zip channel and every corner of a travel bag. Inside a zip channel that dust acts like sandpaper, grinding the slider against the teeth every time the bag is opened. Around the base it works into the stitching and dulls the finish on the bottom panel. A travel bag that is set down on any dusty surface collects abrasion into the places where the bag holds together.

Repeated Wetting and Drying

The April, May, November and December rains soak travel bags on the walk from the matatu stop or the car park to the house or hotel. Water sits in the base seam, along the zip channel and behind the handle anchors where it dries slowly. Each wet and dry cycle pulls the oils out of the leather or fabric and weakens the stitching that holds the bag together. A travel bag that has been caught in the rain repeatedly shows its first failures at exactly these points.

Overpacked Trips

Most travel bags in Nairobi get overpacked at least once a year. A bag designed for fifteen kilos carries twenty on a trip home to the village or a work trip that involved shopping. Overpacking does two things: it forces the zip shut past its design limit and it puts extra strain on the base and the anchors every time the bag is lifted. Even a single overpacked trip is enough to start a slow failure that shows up three months later.

Airport Carousel and Belt Exposure

Anyone who flies through JKIA regularly knows how badly bags are treated on a luggage carousel. The bag is dropped from a height onto a moving belt, pushed through a curtain of hanging rubber strips and then dumped into a pile where the next bag lands on top of it. A leather travel bag goes through this cycle every time it flies, and the corners, base and zip ends are the parts that take the load. Frequent flyers see the effect within a year.

Bad Previous Repairs

A significant portion of our travel bag work involves undoing previous repairs done elsewhere. Handles reattached with glue only, without rivets or stitching. Base panels re stitched with cotton thread that has rotted. Zips replaced with the wrong gauge hardware. Corners patched with fabric that does not match the bag. Correcting these costs more than doing the job properly the first time, which is exactly why we do not cut corners.

Storage Without Support

A travel bag stored empty and folded flat in a cupboard for months loses its shape. The base stiffener deforms, the handles go soft at the anchor, the base panel takes a permanent crease. A bag stored with weight inside it, or squashed under other luggage, can take a permanent set that is difficult to reverse. Storage matters for a travel bag because the base has to hold its structure for the bag to stand up when it is set down on a floor.
