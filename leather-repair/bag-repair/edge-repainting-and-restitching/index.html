Nobody Looks at the Edges Until They Look Tired

Ask anyone who has owned a good leather bag, a handmade belt or a leather wallet for a few years what has aged first, and they will almost always say the edges. Not the leather panels, not the stitching, not the hardware. The cut edges that run around the whole outline of the item. Those are the parts that go dark, dry out, crack along the fold points and eventually start to look like the item is worn out even when everything else is fine.

This is the cheapest repair we offer on most leather items and the one that changes how the item looks the most. A bag whose edges have been repainted and sealed looks like a bag that has been looked after. A wallet whose edges have been repainted and burnished looks like a wallet that came from a shop last week. A belt whose edges have been refinished sits correctly against fabric and does not mark the wearer's clothing. And a bag whose split edge seam has been restitched is a bag that is not going to open up in the middle of a commute two weeks later.

Edges matter because they are exposed on both faces. Every other surface of a leather item has one side against the air and one side protected, either by being folded inside the item or by being stitched into a seam. A cut edge has nothing on either side. It is the cross section of the hide, showing the fibres directly to the air. That means it dries out faster, absorbs dust and sweat faster and cracks faster than any other part of the item. Over time the edge loses its original finish, darkens unevenly, curls slightly and then starts splitting along the fold points where the leather bends most often.

What a Leather Edge Actually Is

To understand why edges fail and how to bring them back, it helps to understand what an edge actually is. A cut edge on a leather item is not a flat side, and it is not the same as the flat surface of the leather panel. It is a cross section of the hide, and it behaves in a specific way.

Leather is a network of collagen fibres that are tightly woven in the top grain (the flat surface of the hide) and more loosely packed in the middle and bottom (the flesh side). When a hide is cut, the flat surface still has its original top grain finish, but the edge exposes all three layers. That means the edge is more porous, more absorbent and more prone to drying out than the surface. It is also more prone to fraying because the fibres on the edge are not held together by anything except the original finish, and once that finish wears through, they start to lift.

Because of that structure, edges need to be finished with a specific product and a specific technique. A surface polish will not seal an edge. A surface wax will not stop the fibres from lifting. What seals an edge is either edge paint, which is a flexible acrylic or polyurethane coating applied in thin layers, or burnishing, which is a process of slicking the edge with wax and friction so the fibres fuse into a smooth sealed band. Each of those finishes is correct for a different type of leather.

Edge paint is the standard finish on most modern leather items. It is used on bags, briefcases, laptop bags, wallets, belts, and most leather accessories. It comes in a wide range of colours and is matched to the panel finish so the edge looks like part of the same piece of leather. It is applied in thin layers, allowed to dry between coats, sanded where necessary and finished with a sealant. The result is a smooth hard edge that repels water, resists abrasion and matches the colour of the item.

Burnishing is the traditional finish on vegetable tanned leather. It is used on higher end belts, saddlery style leather, some wallets and a small number of bags that come from makers who prefer the traditional finish. Burnishing works by wetting the edge slightly, applying a gum or wax and then pressing it with a slicker or a burnisher at high speed until the fibres fuse into a smooth shiny band. The result is a natural look where the edge is slightly darker than the panel and gets darker with use. Burnishing does not use paint, so the colour is inherent to the leather itself.

Getting this distinction right matters because the wrong finish on the wrong leather does not hold. Edge paint on a vegetable tanned belt edge will crack along the fold within months because the leather is too soft and too flexible for paint. Burnishing on a modern chrome tanned bag edge will not seal properly because the leather is too dense at the edge for the fibres to fuse. The correct repair always starts with identifying the leather and its original finish.

What Causes Edge Damage

Edges fail in a predictable way on Nairobi items, and the causes are usually a combination of the same three or four things.

The first is UV and dry air. Nairobi's strong sun and dry season air pull the oils out of the edge finish faster than they pull them out of the panel. A bag that lives in the car or on a desk near a window will have faded edges within a year. A belt that is worn every day will have darker, drier edges within six months if it is not conditioned.

The second is friction. Every time an edge rubs against a fabric pocket, a wooden chair back, a car seat belt or the inside of another bag, the finish on the edge is abraded. A wallet that lives in a pocket and gets pulled in and out a dozen times a day has edges that see more friction in a year than the rest of the wallet sees in a decade.

The third is moisture and dust. Rain and sweat work into the edge, darkening it and softening the finish. Dust works into the softened finish and stains it. Repeated wetting and drying cycles leave the edge looking grey, patchy and dry even when it was originally a deep rich colour.

The fourth is overloading and dropping. When a bag is packed too full, the seam along the edge takes more load than it was designed for. When a bag is dropped at an angle, the impact hits the edge rather than the flat panel. Both of those things start the seam splitting, and once a seam has split along an edge, it needs to be restitched before the leather separates further.

Repainting and restitching handles all four of those. The repaint restores the colour and seals the edge so it stops absorbing water and dust. The burnish (where applicable) seals the raw fibres of a vegetable tanned edge. The restitch closes any split seam so the edge holds together under load. And the refinishing conditions the edge leather so it stops drying out and cracking further.

Why Edges Fail Faster in Nairobi

Nairobi is hard on leather edges in a way that surprises people who have carried the same item in other cities.

Strong UV at Altitude

Nairobi sits at altitude with strong midday sun for most of the year. Cut leather edges dry out from UV faster than any other part of the item because they are exposed on both faces and have no panel backing to hold moisture in. A bag that lives in the car or on a desk near a window will have edges that fade and darken within a year. A belt worn outdoors daily will have edges that lose their original colour within six months. UV damage to edges is the single biggest cause of edge repainting work we do.

Dry Season Air

Nairobi's dry months pull moisture out of leather far faster than most people expect. Edges lose their finish first because they have the highest ratio of exposed surface to volume of leather. Once the edge is dry, the paint loses adhesion to the leather fibres and starts to lift, the fibres start to fray, and the whole edge loses its structure. Conditioning the item twice a year is what slows that process down.

Dust and Fine Grit

Nairobi's dry months produce fine dust that works its way into every edge, every fold and every stitch line. Dust that sits on a dry edge darkens it unevenly and dulls the finish. Dust that works into the fibres of a burnished edge stops the burnish from fusing and leaves the edge looking rough. Regular brushing of the edges after use is the cheapest way to extend the life of an edge finish.

Repeated Wetting and Drying

The April, May, November and December rains soak leather items on the walk from the car, matatu or boda. Water pools in the edge fold points and dries slowly. Each wet and dry cycle strips the oils out of the edge leather and leaves the finish looking grey or patchy. Repeated rain exposure is the leading cause of faded edges on tan and brown leather, and it is the reason we recommend a protective finish on any item that is worn outdoors.

Friction Against Clothing and Furniture

Every leather edge on an item that is worn or carried rubs against something. A belt rubs against trouser loops, chair backs and seat belts. A wallet rubs against the inside of a pocket. A bag rubs against the wearer's side, chair edges and car door frames. That friction wears the edge finish down long before the panel finish shows any wear. That is why edges on worn items need refinishing years before the panels do.

Bad Previous Repairs

A significant portion of our edge work involves undoing previous edge finishes done elsewhere. Edge paint applied too thick so it has cracked along the fold. Edge paint applied in the wrong shade so it does not match the panel. Burnish done with the wrong wax so it goes sticky in heat. Edges restitched with cotton thread that has since rotted. Correcting these costs more than doing the job properly the first time, which is exactly why we do not cut corners.

Storage Without Movement

A leather item stored for months without being moved lets the edge finish take a set at the fold points. When the item is finally used, the paint has to flex at the same line it has dried against, and it cracks. Storing leather items flat with light stuffing and conditioning the edges before storage is what keeps the finish flexible.
