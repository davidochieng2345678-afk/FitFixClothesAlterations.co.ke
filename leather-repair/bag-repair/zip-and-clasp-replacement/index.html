What a Zip Actually Is

To understand why zips fail, it helps to understand what a zip actually is. A zip is not one piece. It is a construction of at least four distinct components stitched together, and every one of them can fail independently.

The teeth are the individual metal or nylon elements that lock together to close the zip. On a metal zip the teeth are pressed onto the tape. On a nylon zip the teeth are extruded onto the tape as part of the tape itself. On a plastic zip the teeth are molded. Teeth can fail by bending, breaking or losing their interlocking shape. On a well used zip the teeth almost always fail at the two ends where the slider passes most often, and rarely in the middle.

The tape is the woven fabric strip that the teeth are attached to. The tape is what the zip is stitched into the seam of the bag or jacket. The tape takes the abrasion of the slider passing over it thousands of times, and it takes the tension of the bag being closed under load. Tape fails by fraying along the edge, by splitting along the line where the teeth attach, or by losing its weave structure after years of sun exposure and rain cycles.

The slider is the metal or plastic piece that moves up and down the zip and locks the teeth together or separates them. The slider has a small internal wedge that forces the teeth into alignment. When that wedge wears out, the slider stops locking the teeth and the zip opens behind the slider. The slider is the single most common point of zip failure, and it is the cheapest part to replace.

The pull is the small metal or plastic tab that hangs off the slider. The pull attaches to the slider through a small hole or a small pin. Pulls fail by wearing through their pin, by snapping at the shank, or by tearing off when they get caught on something.

On top of those four components, every zip has two stops. The top stop is the small metal or plastic block at the top of the zip that stops the slider from coming off. The bottom stop is the small block at the bottom that stops the two sides of the zip from separating when closed. Both stops can be worn out, bent, broken or lost, and both are critical to keeping the slider on the tape.

Understanding this matters because the repair depends on which component has failed. If the slider has failed and the teeth and tape are sound, we replace the slider and the zip is done for a fraction of the cost of a full replacement. If the tape has failed along the seam, a slider swap will not hold and the full zip needs to be replaced. If the stops have failed, we can usually replace just the stops and keep the rest of the zip. Each failure needs a different repair, and doing the wrong repair leaves the same failure to happen again.

What a Clasp Actually Is

A clasp, a lock, a snap fastener and a buckle are all small mechanical pieces that hold a bag or jacket closed. They work on different mechanisms and they fail in different ways.

A snap fastener (also called a press stud) has two halves, one with a spring-loaded ball and one with a matching socket. The two halves snap together and hold until pulled apart. Snap fasteners fail when the spring weakens, when the socket wears out its grip or when the rivet holding the snap to the leather works loose.

A magnetic clasp uses two magnets, usually hidden inside leather tabs, to hold a flap closed. Magnetic clasps fail when the magnets lose their strength (rare), when the leather tabs holding them wear through, or when the leather between the two magnets stretches so the magnets no longer touch.

A buckle is a frame with a prong or a bar that the strap passes through. Buckles fail when the prong bends or snaps, when the frame cracks, or when the plating wears off and the buckle starts to corrode.

A lock on a briefcase is usually a small metal mechanism with a spring-loaded latch and a key cylinder or a push-release. Locks fail when the spring weakens, when the latch wears down or when the housing comes loose from the leather. Replacing a lock is more involved than replacing a snap because the new lock has to be fitted into the existing housing with the correct depth and alignment so the two halves meet cleanly.

Every one of those pieces is small, and every one of them can be replaced. That is why a bag with a failing clasp is almost never a write off, and why the correct repair is almost always a replacement of the hardware rather than the bag itself.

Why Zips and Clasps Fail Faster in Nairobi

Nairobi is hard on zips and hardware in a way that surprises people who have carried the same bag in other cities.

Strong UV at Altitude

Nairobi sits at altitude with strong midday sun for most of the year. Ultraviolet light dries out the wax and oil inside zip tape and cracks the surface finish on zips that see direct sun. A bag that is carried with the zip facing the sun will dry out its tape within a year and a half. Once the tape is dry, the teeth start to separate and the slider loses grip. That is why the zips on the top of a bag, on the outer flap of a jacket and along the shoulder strap line are the ones that fail first.

Dust and Fine Grit

Nairobi's dry months produce fine dust that works its way into every slider and between every tooth. Inside a zip that dust acts like sandpaper, grinding the slider against the teeth every time the zip is opened and closed. In a bag that is set down on dusty surfaces, that dust also builds up around the zip tape and the bottom stop where the slider passes most often. A slider that is carrying dust loses its grip within weeks even when the rest of the zip is sound. That is why regular brushing of the zip channel keeps a zip working for years.

Repeated Wetting and Drying

The April, May, November and December rains soak bags and jackets on the walk from the car, matatu or boda to the office, school or house. Water sits inside the slider channel and along the zip tape where it dries slowly. Each wet and dry cycle pulls the wax out of the tape and starts the corrosion that dulls metal hardware. On a leather bag or jacket the water also works into the seam where the zip is stitched, weakening the stitching over time. Repeated wetting and slow drying is the leading cause of zips that stick halfway and sliders that stop locking.

Forcing a Full Bag or Jacket Closed

Most zips on bags and jackets fail because the item was overpacked or the wearer forced the zip against resistance. When you force a zip shut on a bag that is packed to the limit, the teeth are pressed together with more force than the slider was designed to handle. The wedge inside the slider wears faster, the tape stretches, the top stop pulls out. The same thing happens on a leather jacket when the wearer tries to close a jacket over heavy clothing. A zip that is forced one time too many starts to fail. Zips are not designed to be overstressed, and the correct solution is to close a bag or jacket only when the zip moves freely.

Worn Hardware Plating

Every metal zip and clasp has a plated finish. Brass, nickel, gunmetal and antique finishes are all applied as thin layers over a base metal. Over years of contact with sweat, rain and dust the plating wears through and the base metal starts to corrode. Once the plating has gone, the hardware no longer matches the rest of the bag or jacket and the mechanism starts to bind. That is why replacing worn hardware with matching new hardware keeps an item looking correct.

Bad Previous Repairs

A significant portion of our zip and clasp work involves undoing previous repairs done elsewhere. Zips replaced with the wrong gauge hardware so the slider catches. Zips re stitched through the leather face rather than the original seam. Snaps and locks fitted with the wrong sized rivets so the leather around them cracks. Buckles fitted in a finish that does not match the rest of the bag. Correcting these costs more than doing the job properly the first time, which is exactly why we do not cut corners.

Storage Without Movement

A bag or jacket stored for months without being opened or closed lets the zip take a set at the fold. When the item is finally used, the slider has to force past the set and the tape often splits along the fold. Opening and closing the zip every few weeks during storage is what keeps the tape supple and the slider free.
