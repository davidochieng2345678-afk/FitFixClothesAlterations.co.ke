Why a t-shirt alteration is closer to knitwear than to shirt tailoring

Most people think of a t-shirt as "a casual shirt." A tailor thinks of it as a piece of knit fabric with a specific gauge, a specific stretch, and a specific failure pattern — completely different from the woven cotton of a dress shirt. If a tailor uses the same technique on both, one of them will look wrong within a month. That's the reason our t-shirt bench is the same bench we use for sweaters, polo shirts and knitwear, not the one we use for shirts.

The t-shirt is a tubular knit — most of them have no side seams

The single most important fact about a t-shirt's construction: most are made on a circular knitting machine, which produces a continuous tube of fabric. That tube is then cut at the neckline, the armholes and the hem — but there are no side seams because the fabric was never flat. This changes everything about slimming. On a woven shirt, slimming happens at the two side seams. On a tubular t-shirt, there are no side seams to open — so we have to create them, or slim by re-cutting the armhole and hem, or work with the fabric's natural stretch. Most Nairobi tailors don't understand this. They try to take in a side seam that doesn't exist and end up with a t-shirt that twists after the first wash. Every t-shirt body-slimming job we do starts with a look at how the garment was constructed — tubular or flat-knit — because the technique is completely different for each.

Jersey has stretch — and stretch changes the alteration

Jersey knit — the fabric most t-shirts are cut from — has natural stretch in every direction. That stretch is what makes a t-shirt comfortable. It's also what makes a t-shirt alteration tricky. A seam that's stitched with normal tension on a woven-fabric machine will pop the moment the wearer moves — because the fabric stretches but the seam doesn't. We use a stretch stitch or a cover-stitch on every t-shirt seam we alter, and ballpoint needles so the needle pushes between the knit loops instead of cutting them. We also build a small amount of "give" into every re-cut seam — because a slimmed t-shirt that has no room to stretch will be uncomfortable to wear and will fail at the armhole within a few months.

The collar is the #1 failure point on any t-shirt

Ask anyone who owns a t-shirt they wear regularly: the collar is what goes first. It stretches, it loses its shape, it goes wavy, it develops a permanent fold at the front. The collar is the piece of a t-shirt that receives the most wear because it's the part that gets pulled over the head every time the garment is put on. On a tubular t-shirt, the collar is usually a separate ribbed band that's attached to the neckline — either by cover-stitching, or by a taped seam that runs around the inside of the neck. Both of these have to be preserved when we alter the neck or the collar. We also rebuild stretched collars — re-knitting the rib band by hand with matching yarn if the original has lost its grip. Collar repair is one of the most common t-shirt jobs we do, and it's the repair that most Nairobi tailors avoid because it requires a ribbing machine or a hand-linking needle.

Graphic prints are not just decoration — they're structural

A t-shirt with a graphic print — screen-printed, heat-transferred, embroidered or patch-applied — has a decorative layer that sits on top of the fabric. That layer is stiff, doesn't stretch, and doesn't move with the fabric underneath. When you slim a graphic tee, you have to plan the seam so it doesn't run through the print, because a seam running through a screen-print leaves a visible crease across the design. We always plan the alteration around the graphic — sometimes reshaping the body from the sides only, sometimes shortening the sleeve but leaving the body untouched, sometimes re-shaping the neckline instead of the sides. On heavily-printed tees where the graphic covers the entire front, slimming has to be done from the back panel only. This is slow, careful work, and it's one of the reasons our graphic-tee resizing costs more than slimming a plain cotton tee.

Washing changes the t-shirt — always

A t-shirt that's been washed and dried a dozen times is not the same fabric it was when it was new. Jersey cotton shrinks 3–5% on the first few washes, then continues to shrink slightly (0.5–1% per year) with regular hot washing. A t-shirt that was tagged "size medium" at the shop may now measure as a "small" through the chest and a "medium" through the length. This is why we always measure the actual garment, never the label. We also ask how many times the t-shirt has been washed — because a t-shirt that has already shrunk significantly will behave differently under a needle than a fresh one. On a t-shirt that's already been through the wash a few times, we cut with the assumption that it won't shrink any further, because the fibre has already been through its main shrinkage cycle. On a fresh t-shirt, we cut with a small allowance for the shrinkage that's coming.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 1,000 slim on a KSh 800 market t-shirt doesn't make economic sense — the t-shirt will fail at the armhole within a year, and the alteration has cost more than the garment. A KSh 1,000 slim on a KSh 3,000 premium jersey tee saves the tee for another three years. What matters is whether the t-shirt is worth saving — and that's often a matter of the graphic, the fit, or the memory attached to it rather than the price. We turn down t-shirt alterations every month when the honest answer is that the fabric is pilling, the collar can't be saved, or the print is cracking off the fabric. But on a t-shirt with good fabric and a print worth preserving, an alteration pays for itself many times over. It's the same standard we hold on sweater alterations and polo shirt alterations.

Not all t-shirts are the same job

Three t-shirt tiers — three completely different alterations

A KSh 500 market tee and a KSh 8,000 heavyweight loopwheeled Japanese tee require completely different handling. One is thin jersey that will pill within a year; the other is dense knitwear that ages gracefully and deserves careful alteration. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be ruined. Here's how we categorise t-shirts before deciding what technique to use.

Entry tier

Basic cotton and poly-cotton tees

Mass-produced t-shirts from market stalls, promotional giveaways, fast fashion and budget retail. Fabric weight 140–180 gsm. Usually a cotton-polyester blend or a light single-jersey cotton. Collar ribbing is often narrow (2.5–3 cm) and quickly loses its shape. Graphic prints are usually heat-transferred decals that crack within a year. Common in Nairobi retail under KSh 1,500. The everyday tee.

What we do differentlyOn these tees we work quickly and pragmatically — the goal is to make the tee fit, not to add years of life it can't support. We use a stretch stitch on all seams and finish with a cover-stitch where the hem is visible. We don't rebuild collar ribbing on this tier unless it's an otherwise-wearable tee with a specific memory attached — the labour cost approaches the value of the tee. On these, a shortens-and-slips job is worth doing; a full restyle usually isn't.

Everyday tier

Mid-weight premium cotton tees

Better-quality cotton t-shirts from mid-tier brands — Everlane, Uniqlo U, Uniqlo Supima, Massimo Dutti, and premium local makers. Fabric weight 180–220 gsm. 100% cotton — often organic, ring-spun or Supima. Collar ribbing is wider (3–3.5 cm) with a taped neck seam. Graphic prints are screen-printed or embroidered and hold up over years. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 1,500 and KSh 5,000. The everyday premium tee.

What we do differentlyOn this tier, we treat the tee like what it is — a proper garment worth altering properly. We use a fine-gauge needle, a ballpoint tip, and cover-stitch every visible hem. On taped-neck tees, we preserve the taping when we adjust the neckline. On graphic tees at this tier, the print is usually screen-printed and durable — but it still shows a seam crease if we cut through it, so we plan the alteration around the print as carefully as we would on a design-led garment.

Premium tier

Heavyweight, ring-spun and Japanese loopwheeled tees

Heavyweight premium tees from Japanese and Italian makers — Loopwheeled (also called "loopwheel") tees, Heavyweight Supima, Kurabo fabric, Sunspel, The Real McCoy's, and high-end designer tees. Fabric weight 240–300+ gsm. Dense knit with a distinct texture that ages beautifully. Collar ribbing is often 4 cm with double-needle cover-stitching and a full taped neck seam. Sometimes hand-finished at the hem. Common above KSh 6,000 — and the t-shirts clients wear for a decade.

What we do differentlyOn this tier every alteration is done with a fine-gauge needle at low tension, and every visible seam is finished with the same construction as the original — double-needle cover-stitch on the hem, taped neck preserved on neckline work. Loopwheeled fabric has a specific texture that a bad alteration will leave a permanent mark on — we don't press it, we block it. On a heavyweight tee that's going to be worn for another ten years, an extra hour on the bench is worth it.

If you're not sure which tier your t-shirt falls into, send a close-up photo of the knit structure, the hem (front and back), and the collar rib on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five parts of a t-shirt that affect the alteration

What a t-shirt actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a t-shirt as "a plain top with short sleeves." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a t-shirt has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a tee.

01

The body — tubular or flat-knit

The body of a t-shirt is either tubular or flat-knit. Tubular means the fabric was knit as a continuous tube on a circular knitting machine — no side seams. Flat-knit means the body was cut from a flat sheet of fabric and sewn at the two side seams. Tubular is faster and cheaper to make, and the vast majority of t-shirts in Nairobi are tubular. Flat-knit is more common on designer and premium t-shirts because it allows the fabric to be laid out and pattern-matched. The distinction matters because slimming a tubular t-shirt requires a completely different technique than slimming a flat-knit one. On tubular tees, we have to create side seams where none existed. On flat-knit tees, we open the existing seam and re-cut.

02

The collar and neck ribbing

The collar is a separate ribbed band attached to the neckline of the t-shirt. It's ribbed — usually a 1x1 or 2x2 rib — because the rib gives the collar the stretch it needs to pass over the wearer's head. The collar is usually attached by cover-stitching, and on better tees it's reinforced with a taped seam that runs around the inside of the neck. The collar is the piece that receives the most abuse — it's stretched every time the t-shirt is put on, it absorbs sweat at the neck, and it's usually the first thing that fails. When we alter a t-shirt, the collar is what we check first. On tees where the collar is still intact, we preserve it and work around it. On tees where the collar has stretched out of shape, we rebuild the rib band — sometimes with new matching yarn, sometimes by re-knitting the original rib.

03

The sleeves — set-in or drop-shoulder

T-shirts come in two sleeve constructions. Set-in sleeves have a seam that runs from the underarm up over the shoulder — a proper tailored sleeve. Drop-shoulder sleeves have no shoulder seam at all — the sleeve fabric continues from the body, which gives a relaxed, boxy look. Set-in sleeves can be shortened from the cuff easily, but sleeve slimming is more involved because the armhole has to be re-shaped. Drop-shoulder sleeves are simpler to alter because there's no shoulder seam to work around — but the alteration has to preserve the boxy drape or the tee looks awkward. On any sleeve work, we preserve the original sleeve finish — a machine hem, a rolled finish or a raw edge depending on the tee.

04

The hem and side seams

The hem is the finished bottom edge of the t-shirt. On a mass-produced tee, it's usually a single-needle straight stitch with a small fold — quick but visible. On premium tees, it's a double-needle cover-stitch or a rolled finish — slower but with a specific look. On some designer tees, the hem is raw, folded flat, or finished with a contrast stitch. When we shorten a t-shirt, we match the original hem finish — never substitute a different style. On a tubular tee, the hem runs continuously around the body. On a flat-knit tee, the hem meets the side seams. We always check whether the original hem was single-needle or cover-stitch and use the same construction on the new hem.

05

The graphic — print, transfer, embroidery or patch

Any decoration on the t-shirt — screen-printed, heat-transferred, embroidered, patch-applied, or appliqué — is a layer that sits on top of the fabric. It doesn't stretch with the fabric, and it doesn't move the way the fabric does. When we alter a decorated tee, we plan the alteration to avoid running a seam through the decoration. On screen prints, a seam through the print leaves a permanent crease. On heat transfers, a seam can cause the transfer to lift at the edge. On embroidered designs, a seam through the embroidery destroys the design. On patch-applied or appliqué designs, a seam through the patch will pop the stitches holding the patch. Every graphic-tee alteration we do is planned around the graphic first — before we think about the fit.

If a tailor quotes a t-shirt alteration without asking about the construction — tubular or flat, set-in or drop-shoulder, print or plain — they're about to do a job that will look wrong by the third wash. We'd rather ask five questions upfront than take on a job we can't finish properly.

The fabric decides the technique

The five t-shirt fabrics we work with most often — and how each one behaves

The fabric decides how the alteration behaves under a needle, how it presses, and how it holds shape after the first wash. Here's our honest breakdown by fabric type, the same one we'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Standard cotton single-jersey

The classic t-shirt fabric. Single-jersey cotton is knit in a single continuous loop structure — light, breathable, and natural-feeling. Fabric weight 140–180 gsm. It's the fabric most market and mid-range tees are cut from. Single-jersey takes alterations well but also shrinks — usually 3–5% on the first wash. We cut with a small allowance on a fresh single-jersey tee to account for that shrinkage, and we don't add the allowance on a tee that's already been through several washes. Single-jersey also has natural stretch in every direction, which means every seam has to be stitched with a stretch stitch. A standard straight stitch on single-jersey will pop when the wearer moves.

Ringspun cotton and Pima/Supima

A finer, softer variant of cotton. Ringspun cotton uses longer, smoother fibres that create a tighter, more uniform knit — softer feel, fewer surface fibres, less pilling. Pima and Supima are premium American-grown cotton varieties with even longer fibres and a natural sheen. Fabric weight 160–220 gsm. These fabrics hold their shape better than standard single-jersey and don't pill as aggressively. On ringspun and Pima tees, we use a fine-gauge needle at low tension so the surface stays smooth. The trade-off is that these fabrics show needle marks more — a heavy needle leaves a permanent indent along the seam. This is a fabric that rewards careful work.

Heavyweight knit — 200 gsm and above

The heavyweight tier — dense cotton knit that feels substantial and drapes with weight. Fabric weight 200–300 gsm. Sometimes called "heavyweight Supima," "heavyweight ring-spun," or "loopwheeled" — the last one refers to a Japanese knitting technique that produces the fabric on a slow, low-tension circular machine, giving it a distinctive texture and unusual durability. Heavyweight knits hold their shape for years. They also take alterations beautifully because the fabric has enough body to hold a seam without stretching out. On heavyweight knit, we work at low speed and use a slightly heavier needle than on light jersey. This is the fabric tier that pays back the alteration with another decade of wear.

Cotton-polyester blends

Common on promotional tees, sports tees and budget retail. The poly content gives the fabric better shape retention and durability than pure cotton but takes away the natural feel. Fabric weight 140–200 gsm. Polyester-cotton blends are less prone to shrinking but more prone to pilling where the fabric rubs. They also respond differently to heat — the poly content can scorch under an iron at cotton settings. On poly-blend tees, we press through a muslin cloth at medium-low heat and never with direct high heat. On sports tees with moisture-wicking coatings, we avoid steaming entirely because the coating can lift. Cotton-poly is more forgiving to alter than people think — it's the fabric we're least worried about, provided the heat is controlled.

Tri-blend and performance blends

Tri-blend (cotton-polyester-rayon) is a soft, draping fabric used on premium casual tees. Performance blends are polyester-elastane or nylon-elastane blends used on sports and technical tees. Both stretch differently from pure cotton — tri-blend has a soft drape that doesn't hold a shape the way cotton does; performance blends have significant stretch that a normal stitch will pop. On tri-blend, we work carefully with a fine needle at low tension — the fabric marks easily. On performance blends, we use a stretch stitch or a cover-stitch throughout and never press with heat, because the elastane content can melt. On performance tees, we also finish every hem with a stretch cover-stitch that matches the original construction — otherwise the hem will pop the first time the wearer reaches overhead.

What we won't alter

We turn down t-shirt alterations every month, and we'd rather do that than take money for a result the client won't like. On tees where the fabric is heavily pilled all over (common on cheap poly-blends after two years of washing), the pilling doesn't stop when we alter — it accelerates along the new seam. On tees where the graphic is cracking off the fabric (common on cheap heat-transferred prints), any alteration that involves stretching or repositioning the print area will make the crack worse. On tees where the collar has already been rebuilt once by someone else, we're cautious — a second rebuild often can't achieve a clean neckline. And on any tee where the fabric has gone thin and see-through (common on very old single-jersey), the alteration will show the thinness rather than fix it. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money. It's the same standard we hold on sweater alterations.

A short note from the bench

The one thing most tailors get wrong about t-shirts

Most tailors treat a t-shirt like a cotton shirt that's had its collar removed. They set the machine at the same tension they'd use on woven cotton, they take in the same side seams, they press with the same heat. And then the t-shirt comes back to them a week later — twisted at the side seams, popped at the armhole, or looking fine but feeling uncomfortable because there's no give in the alteration. I know this because most of the t-shirt rescue jobs I do are fixing alterations that were done the wrong way by someone else. The t-shirt wasn't the problem. The technique was.

Here's what most people don't know about t-shirts. Almost every t-shirt in Nairobi — including the ones from market stalls and promotional giveaways — is a tubular knit. It was knit as one continuous tube of fabric on a circular machine. There are no side seams because the fabric was never flat. This is cheap, fast and efficient, and it's how almost the entire global t-shirt industry works. But it means that if you want to slim a t-shirt, you can't just open the side seams and take them in. There are no side seams to open. You have to create new ones — and creating a new side seam on jersey has to be done with a stretch stitch, at the correct tension, with a ballpoint needle, otherwise the seam will pop the first time the wearer reaches over their head. I've seen tailors spend an hour slimming a t-shirt and hand it back with a straight-stitched side seam that fails within a week. The client thinks t-shirts can't be altered. What actually happened is that the tailor didn't know what they were working with.

The second thing about t-shirts is the collar. The collar is the piece that fails first, because it's stretched over the wearer's head every single time the t-shirt is put on. On a well-made t-shirt, the collar is a ribbed band that's cover-stitched to the neckline and reinforced with a taped seam running around the inside. That tape is what gives the collar the strength to survive hundreds of head-passings without deforming. On a cheap t-shirt, the tape is missing or thin and the collar stretches within six months. When a client brings me a t-shirt with a stretched collar, I check the tape first. If the tape is intact, I can often save the collar with a re-tensioning job. If the tape is gone and the rib has relaxed, I rebuild the rib — matching the yarn, matching the rib gauge, hand-linking the new rib to the neckline. This takes a bit of time, but it turns a t-shirt that's about to be donated into one that gets worn another two years. It's one of the repairs I'm proudest of because nobody expects a collar to be fixable — and then it is.

The third thing, and this is the one that matters most on graphic tees: the print is not just decoration. It's a physical layer on the surface of the fabric. Screen prints, heat transfers, embroideries, appliqués — each one behaves differently under a needle, and each one fails differently. A screen print that gets a seam crease through it is permanently creased. A heat transfer that gets stretched at the wrong angle starts to lift at the edges. An embroidery that gets a seam through it loses its design. On a graphic tee, the print has to be planned around, not altered through. If a t-shirt's graphic is on the front panel and the client wants the body slimmed, I plan the alteration to take place entirely at the back and sides — the front panel where the graphic sits is left untouched. If the graphic covers the whole front, I sometimes have to tell the client that a full slimming job isn't possible without running a seam through the design. That's an honest answer, and it's better than charging for a job that ruins the t-shirt.

Fourteen years of doing this has taught me that t-shirts are the most personal garments in any wardrobe. Not the most expensive, not the most formal — the most personal. The t-shirt you wear every Saturday. The t-shirt from a specific holiday. The t-shirt from a specific concert or a specific team. Those t-shirts don't get replaced easily, and a good alteration is often the difference between a shirt that gets worn for another three years and one that quietly disappears from the drawer. That's the reason I take t-shirt work as seriously as suit work — because the emotional value of the garment has nothing to do with its price tag. And it's the reason I'll always tell a client honestly when a t-shirt is worth altering and when it isn't.

Make your t-shirts last between alterations

Eight habits that keep t-shirts out of the workshop

T-shirts are the most-worn, most-washed garment in any wardrobe — and the one that ages fastest if the care is wrong. Most of the damage we repair in the workshop was preventable. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect an altered t-shirt.

1. Wash cold, inside out

Cold water — 30°C or below — preserves the colour, the shape and the knit structure of a t-shirt. Hot water shrinks cotton by 3–5% on the first wash and continues to shrink it slowly over the first year. Turning the t-shirt inside out before washing protects the visible surface from friction in the drum, which is especially important on graphic tees where the print is on the outside. Wash similar colours together — t-shirts fade unevenly if mixed with darks over time.

2. Skip fabric softener and dryer sheets

Fabric softener leaves a residue on the fabric that dulls the colour over time and weakens the fibre. On graphic tees, the residue can also break down the screen-print or the heat transfer over a year or two. Skip the softener entirely. If you want a softer feel, use a small amount of white vinegar in the rinse cycle — it softens the fabric without leaving a residue. Never use dryer sheets on a graphic tee — the sheet coating transfers to the print and makes it feel slightly greasy.

3. Never tumble dry a t-shirt on high heat

Tumble drying on high heat is the fastest way to shrink a cotton t-shirt, to pill the fabric, and to distort the collar. If you must use a dryer, use the lowest heat setting and remove the t-shirt while still slightly damp — then hang or lay it flat to finish drying. The best option is to skip the dryer entirely: lay the t-shirt flat on a drying rack or a towel, reshape it with your hands, and let it air dry at room temperature. On a graphic tee, tumble drying accelerates the cracking of the print.

4. Hang by the shoulders, not the collar

Hanging a wet or dry t-shirt by the collar stretches the collar out of shape — the same failure mode that most collar rebuilds are trying to fix. Always hang t-shirts by the shoulders on a wide hanger, or fold them flat in a drawer. On a heavy jersey t-shirt, the weight of the fabric pulling from the collar is what makes the collar lose its shape over a few months. If you're short on drawer space, hang the t-shirt on a wide wooden hanger with the shoulders supported — never on a narrow wire hanger by the collar.

5. Rotate between t-shirts

Cotton jersey needs 24 hours to recover its shape between wears. Wear the same t-shirt three days in a row and the collar stays compressed, the hem stays stretched, and the whole garment ages twice as fast. If you own five or six t-shirts and rotate them, each one gets worn a fifth as often and lasts roughly five times as long. This one habit alone roughly doubles the working life of a t-shirt — cheaper than any alteration, and it protects the collar from failing prematurely.

6. Deal with collar stretch before it's permanent

The first sign that a collar is about to fail is a slight wobble at the front — the fabric starts to fold over itself when you're not wearing the t-shirt. At that point, a KSh 500 collar repair can save the collar. Left for another month, the rib loses its grip entirely and needs to be replaced at KSh 800. Left for longer, the neckline starts to stretch out of shape too, and the whole repair becomes a KSh 1,200 job. Collar damage is always cheapest to fix the moment it appears.

7. Never iron directly onto a graphic print

A hot iron on a screen-printed or heat-transferred graphic is the fastest way to crack the print. If a t-shirt needs pressing, turn it inside out and press the back of the print — or press through a thin cotton cloth at low heat. On embroidered t-shirts, pressing directly onto the embroidery flattens the stitches and leaves shine marks around them. Always press from the inside, or cover the decorated area with a cotton cloth before pressing. On a very creased graphic tee, the safest option is to hang it in a steamy bathroom for ten minutes — no iron required.

8. Fix small hem issues before they grow

A single loose stitch at the hem is a KSh 400 fix today. Left for three months, the hem runs and the whole bottom edge starts to unravel — that becomes a KSh 600 full hem re-cut. Same for a small tear at the sleeve cuff: a KSh 400 repair now, a KSh 700 sleeve shortening if you wait. The clients whose t-shirts last the longest are the ones who message us the moment they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less, and they preserve more of the original garment.

Follow those eight habits and your t-shirts will hold their fit and colour for years. And when they do eventually need work — a collar rebuilt, a sleeve shortened, a hem repaired — we're one WhatsApp message away.
