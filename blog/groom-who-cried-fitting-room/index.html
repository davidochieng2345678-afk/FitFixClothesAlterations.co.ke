The Groom Who Cried in the Fitting Room

By David Ochieng · Master Tailor, FitFix Nairobi · 6 min read

Note on this story: The groom in this piece is a composite of real conversations I have had with Nairobi grooms over fourteen years of tailoring. Names, dates and small details have been changed to protect privacy. What follows — the mistakes, the timeline, the costs — is exactly what I see on the workshop bench every season.

He walked in with a suit in a plastic bag and a face that had already given up. His name, for the purposes of this story, is Peter. He was getting married in three weeks. He had ordered a KSh 8,000 "custom" suit from a tailor a friend had recommended. When he finally tried it on, nothing fit. The jacket was tight across the chest. The trousers dragged over his shoes. The shoulders sloped unevenly. And under the fluorescent light of his Kilimani apartment, the fabric had the tell-tale plastic shine of cheap polyester.

"Can you fix it?" he asked. "Or is it too late?"

The honest answer was: maybe. The suit could have been altered, but the fabric and the fused interlining would still have looked cheap in photographs. And the wedding was in twenty-one days — which is a tight timeline for any tailor, including this one.

I offered him something else. A proper made-to-measure suit, built from scratch in three weeks, at a rush fee on top of the standard price. Italian wool, full canvas, two fittings. KES 58,000, of which KES 3,000 was the rush fee.

He asked, "How much would I have paid if I had come to you three months ago?"

"About KES 55,000," I said. "And you would have had three fittings instead of two, and no stress in the last month."

He put down a 50 percent deposit that afternoon. We booked him in.

The Three Weeks That Followed

Peter called us on a Tuesday with twenty-one days to the wedding. Here is what the next three weeks looked like in the workshop.

Day 1: Measurement session. Twenty measurements, posture analysis, fabric selection. We chose Super 120s navy Italian wool, which is the standard recommendation for a Nairobi wedding because it breathes in the heat and photographs beautifully under both daylight and reception lighting. The fabric was ordered that afternoon.

Day 5: The basted fitting. The garment was held together with temporary white stitching so we could check the balance and drape on Peter's actual body. We adjusted the shoulder line slightly and took in the waist. He looked at himself in the mirror and said, "This already looks better than the suit I paid for."

Day 12: The forward fitting. Collar, sleeves, pockets and buttonholes finalized. The trousers were adjusted for the exact shoes he planned to wear. At this point the suit was 90 percent finished.

Day 17: The final fitting. Peter tried on the finished suit with the shirt and shoes he would wear to the wedding. He looked at himself in the mirror. His mother was in the room, and his bride was in the room, and for the first time in months, he looked like the man he was about to become. He cried. So did his mother.

Day 18: Delivery. The suit was pressed, bagged and delivered to Peter's apartment in a garment bag.

Day 21: The wedding. Peter wore the suit for fourteen hours. He danced, he knelt, he hugged his mother, he was photographed from every angle. He told me a week later that the suit still looked like it had just been pressed when he finally took it off.

What I want every groom to understand from this story: Peter's suit came out well because we are experienced at rush jobs and because we were honest with him about the risk. But this is not the way to do a wedding suit. Three weeks is a compressed timeline. It costs more. It is more stressful. And a rushed suit has less margin for correction if the first fitting is off. Twelve weeks is the honest recommendation, and it costs less.

What the Photographs Look Like Now

Peter sent me a photograph from his first anniversary. He and his wife were on the coast, laughing, and he was wearing the same suit — to dinner, not to a wedding, but to dinner — and the suit still looked new. He told me he had worn it to his own wedding, to two other weddings as a groomsman, to a corporate event, and to that anniversary dinner. That is about seven wears in the first two years.

I want to be honest about the cost-per-wear math, because I see a lot of pages online get this wrong. Seven wears for a KSh 58,000 suit is roughly KSh 8,285 per wear in the first two years. That is not a bargain by the numbers. It becomes one over a decade — if Peter wears it three or four times a year for the next eight years, the total will be around thirty-four wears and the cost per wear drops to about KSh 1,700. Still not free, but a very different number.

The real value of the suit was never in the per-wear math. It was in the photographs. It was in his mother's face in the fitting room. It was in the fact that Peter never had to think about his suit on his own wedding day. That is not something you can divide by the number of wears.

Why Wedding Suits Are Different

A wedding suit is the most photographed garment you will ever own. It will appear in every ceremony photo, every reception photo, every group shot, every candid your photographer catches when you are not looking. It will be printed, framed, shared on social media, and shown at anniversaries for decades. It is, in the truest sense, a permanent record.

That changes what "good enough" means. A business suit can be "good enough." A wedding suit cannot. It has to look right in every photograph, under every light, from every angle. It has to stay sharp through fourteen hours of wear. It has to move when the groom dances, when he kneels, when he embraces his mother. And it has to feel like his own, not like a rental.

This is why made-to-measure wedding suits are the standard for any groom who is serious about the day, and why the grooms who try to save money on the suit are the ones who end up in a workshop like ours with three weeks to spare.

What a Made-to-Measure Suit Actually Does Differently

1. The pattern is drafted for your body, not a block

A real made-to-measure suit is drafted from twenty measurements, not four. Shoulder slope, chest, waist, seat, sleeve length, jacket length, posture. A cheap suit is cut from a block pattern and adjusted with side seams. It will never fit properly because it was never designed to.

2. The fabric is real wool

Wedding photography is unforgiving. Real Italian wool photographs with weight and depth. Cheap polyester photographs with a plastic shine that no amount of editing can fix. If you want to see how different the two look under a professional lens, put on a cheap suit, take a photo in daylight, then put on a wool suit and take the same photo. The difference is immediate.

3. The canvas is stitched, not glued

A real wedding suit has a floating horsehair canvas in the chest and lapels, hand-stitched into the garment. This is what gives a jacket its shape and keeps it looking sharp through a long day. A cheap suit uses glue. It will bubble and separate under heat and pressure, and once it bubbles, no tailor can fix it.

4. The fittings are real

A cheap tailor measures you once and delivers once. A real tailor fits you at least twice. On a wedding suit, this is the difference between a garment that photographs beautifully and one the groom has to tug at all day.

5. The guarantee is real

If a seam fails or a button loosens in the first year, we fix it. That is what the FitFix workmanship guarantee covers. A cheap suit offers no guarantee at all.

How much does a made-to-measure wedding suit cost at FitFix?

Made-to-measure wedding suits start from KES 55,000. The final price depends on fabric, lining, detailing and timing. A rush job — under six weeks to the wedding — carries an additional fee, usually KES 2,000 to KES 4,000. Every price is confirmed in writing after the consultation and before any work begins. There are no surprise charges on delivery.

How far in advance should a groom book his wedding suit?

Twelve weeks is the ideal window. It allows for a basted fitting, a forward fitting and a final fitting without pressure, at the standard price. We can compress the timeline for a rush fee, but we are honest that it costs more and creates more stress for the groom. If the wedding is under four weeks away, call us on 0705 728 896 to check availability before assuming we cannot help.

What is included in the fittings?

Every FitFix wedding suit includes at least two fittings. A basted fitting where the garment is held together with temporary stitches so we can check the balance and drape. A forward fitting where the collar, sleeves and pockets are finalised. A final fitting before delivery confirms the suit is ready. Rush jobs sometimes combine the basted and forward fittings into a single session — which is one of the reasons we recommend twelve weeks.

What happens if the suit does not fit on the wedding day?

We will not release a suit until it is right. If a workmanship issue appears — a seam opens, a button loosens, a zip fails — we repair it under our workmanship guarantee. Our advice to every groom is to try the suit on with the shirt and shoes he will wear at least three days before the wedding, so any last-minute adjustment can be handled calmly rather than on the morning of the wedding.

Do you fit the groomsmen as well?

Yes. We offer groomsmen suit alterations on the same matched-fit basis as the groom's suit. Most grooms ask their groomsmen to source their own suits and then bring them to us in one batch for matched alterations. This keeps the wedding party visually consistent in photographs, which matters more than most couples realise on the day.

What fabrics do you recommend for a Nairobi wedding?

For most Nairobi weddings we recommend Super 120s Italian wool in navy, charcoal or mid-grey. It breathes in the heat, holds its shape through a long day, and photographs beautifully under both natural and artificial light. For evening weddings or cold-season dates, a slightly heavier wool or a wool-silk blend works well. We do not recommend polyester blends for a wedding suit — they trap heat and photograph with a visible shine.

What is the deposit and payment structure?

A deposit of 50 percent secures the wedding date in our schedule and covers the fabric order. The balance is paid on delivery, by M-Pesa, cash or bank transfer. The deposit is standard on every wedding suit because we order fabric specific to your commission and hold the date against other bookings.

Can you rush a suit in less than four weeks?

Sometimes. A rush job is possible where capacity allows, at a rush fee, and it usually compresses three fittings into two. We will tell you honestly on the call whether we can meet your date. What we will not do is take the booking and hope. If we cannot deliver to the standard we would put on our own wedding, we will tell you to find another tailor.
