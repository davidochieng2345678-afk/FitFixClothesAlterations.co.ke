Why Lawyers, Bankers and CEOs Eventually Buy Made-to-Measure

By David Ochieng · Master Tailor, FitFix Nairobi · 8 min read

Note on this post: What follows is a pattern I have observed on the FitFix bench over fourteen years of dressing Nairobi professionals. The specific people and conversations described here are composites of many real interactions, with names, employers and details changed to protect privacy. The observations are honest. The point is not to claim that every lawyer, banker or CEO wears a made-to-measure suit. It is to describe the wardrobe pattern I have seen most often at the senior end of Nairobi's professional class, and to explain why it usually happens later than it should.

There is a conversation I have had, in slightly different words, with dozens of Nairobi professionals over the years. It usually starts the same way. The client is in his late thirties or early forties. He has been in his career for ten or fifteen years. He is good at what he does. And he has, on some level, known for a while that his suits are not doing him any favours — but he has been putting off the decision.

Then something happens. A promotion comes up. A client introduces him to a new team. A younger colleague walks into the meeting looking sharper than he does. A photograph from a conference lands on LinkedIn and he sees himself in the background, and does not like what he sees. And he finally picks up the phone.

"I don't know why I waited this long," he says. "I knew I needed this five years ago."

I hear that sentence more than any other. And I want to write about it, because I think it points to something real about how professionals think about their wardrobes — and why the timing of the decision matters almost as much as the decision itself.

The Pattern, Honestly Stated

First, the observation. Most of the senior Nairobi professionals I have dressed did not buy their first made-to-measure suit early in their careers. They bought it later — usually somewhere between their mid-thirties and late forties. Before that, they wore off-the-rack suits, either bought in Nairobi or picked up on a trip to Dubai, London or Johannesburg. Some of those suits were good. Most of them fit imperfectly. A few were genuinely bad but were worn for years anyway because the owner did not know what to look for.

There are exceptions, of course. Some professionals buy made-to-measure in their twenties because they grew up in families where that was normal, or because a mentor told them to, or because they had a specific body-fit problem that made off-the-rack shopping unbearable. But the exceptions are not the pattern. The pattern is: years of wearing suits that do not quite work, then a decision to fix it properly, then a sense of regret that it took so long.

I want to be careful here. I am not saying every senior professional needs a made-to-measure suit, or that the ones who do not buy one are somehow failing. Plenty of excellent lawyers, bankers and CEOs wear off-the-rack suits and look perfectly fine. The question is not "should you own a made-to-measure suit?" It is "given your specific work and how often you wear a suit, is the investment worth it for you?" — and the answer depends on the person, not the title.

What I am not claiming: I am not claiming a made-to-measure suit causes a promotion, wins a case, or closes a deal. What I have observed, honestly, is that the professionals who come to me for made-to-measure are usually at an inflection point in their careers — a promotion, a partner track, a first client-facing role, a shift from doer to leader. The suit is part of that shift, not the cause of it. The suit is the outside catching up to the inside.

Why They Wait Too Long

If the upgrade is worth it eventually, why do most professionals wait so long to make it? I have asked this question to enough clients to see the pattern, and the answers fall into four buckets.

1. The suit feels like a cost, not an investment

Most professionals think about their wardrobe the way they think about their phone bill — as a recurring cost that needs to be kept as low as possible. A KSh 8,000 suit and a KES 55,000 suit both cover the body. The cheaper one leaves more money for other things. So the brain defaults to the cheaper one and only revisits the decision when the cheap suit starts to fail visibly.

What this misses is the math. A KES 55,000 suit worn three times a week for five years works out to roughly KES 70 per wear. A KSh 8,000 suit worn twice a week for one year before it starts to look tired is roughly KES 77 per wear — nearly identical, but the made-to-measure suit is still going strong at year five while the cheap suit was replaced four times. When professionals actually run this math, the made-to-measure decision stops looking like a luxury. It starts looking like the obvious default.

2. They do not know what "good" looks like

Most professionals can spot a badly fitted suit on someone else. They cannot spot the subtler problems in their own — the shoulder line that sits half a centimetre too wide, the jacket that stops 2 centimetres above where it should, the fabric that shines under office lighting because it is more polyester than wool. They have been wearing the same thing for so long that they have stopped seeing it. It takes a photograph, or a colleague in a better suit, or a tailor who tells them the truth, to break the spell.

I do not blame anyone for this. Tailoring is a specialist trade, and most people — including most senior professionals — have never had anyone teach them what to look for. If a client comes to me wearing a fused suit that fits imperfectly, I tell them honestly what I see. Sometimes that conversation ends with a commission. Sometimes it ends with "the suits you have are fine, save your money for now." I would rather have the honest conversation than the commission.

3. They think their work speaks for itself

Many senior professionals — especially lawyers, doctors, engineers, academics — believe, on principle, that their work should be judged on its substance rather than its presentation. That is a good principle. It is also a principle the world does not fully honour. A partner across the table in a boardroom, or a judge on the bench, or a client in a pitch meeting, is forming impressions in the first two seconds — before your case studies, before your numbers, before your analysis. A suit that fits is part of the signal that everything else is built on.

I am not saying the signal is fair. I am saying it exists. And the professionals who understand that — who make their peace with the reality that appearance is part of communication — are the ones who end up in the made-to-measure conversation earlier than their peers.

4. They have been meaning to, but never quite got to it

The simplest reason is also the most common. Made-to-measure has been on the mental to-do list for years. There was always another priority: a case, a deal, a quarter-end, a family obligation, a renovation. The suit kept getting pushed to next month, then next quarter, then next year. And then a photograph appeared, or a client meeting happened, or a colleague got promoted, and the priority suddenly became urgent.

The irony is that made-to-measure takes less time than most people assume. A consultation, two fittings, a delivery. Four to six weeks end to end, with maybe three hours of the client's time across the whole process. It is not the reason to delay. It is the reason it is worth starting now rather than later.

The common thread is not the colour or the cut. It is the underlying architecture: real wool, proper canvas, a fit drafted to the body, and a shoulder line that sits exactly where it should. What differs between roles is the finish. A court lawyer's suit needs to look restrained — nothing the eye can latch onto except the fit. A banker's suit can have a slightly sharper line at the waist because the client sitting across the desk expects precision. A CEO's suit, paradoxically, often works best when it is the quietest thing in the room. The more senior the role, the less the suit needs to do, because the person wearing it is already doing the work.

What a Made-to-Measure Suit Actually Changes

When a senior professional commissions their first made-to-measure suit, four things change that they did not expect.

The shoulder line

The most common flaw in an off-the-rack suit is the shoulder line. It sits too wide, or too narrow, or slopes unevenly, or ends a centimetre before the sleeve begins. In a photograph, this reads as "the suit is not quite his." In a made-to-measure suit, the shoulder is drafted to the client's actual shoulder slope and width. The jacket sits where it should. The room does not consciously notice the difference, but the room's impression of "he looks the way he should look" is immediate.

The jacket length

An off-the-rack jacket that stops above the seat shortens the torso and makes the legs look shorter. A made-to-measure jacket is drafted to the client's actual body length, finishing just at the base of the seat where it should. This single change alters the way the entire figure reads, especially in photographs where the proportions are all a viewer has to work with.

The waist suppression

A cheap suit is cut straight through the waist. It hangs like a box. A made-to-measure suit tapers from chest to waist to seat, following the client's actual shape. The result looks slimmer and more expensive even on a body that is neither slim nor expensive-looking in the mirror. It is not a trick. It is the correct architectural line for the body.

The fabric

This is the part clients notice on themselves before anyone else notices it on them. Real wool sits on the body differently than polyester. It moves with the wearer, not against them. It breathes in Nairobi's heat. It stays matte under office lighting. It does not shine at the elbows and knees after eighteen months. A professional who has worn cheap suits for a decade and then moves to Super 120s wool feels the difference the first time they put the suit on.

The Honest Math for Professionals

I have been asked many times: "Is this actually worth it?" The honest answer depends on how often you wear a suit and how long you intend to keep it. Here is the real math.

The off-the-rack path. A good off-the-rack suit in Nairobi runs KSh 15,000 to KSh 30,000. With proper alterations it fits reasonably well and lasts about two to three years of regular wear before it starts to show its age. If you replace it every two years, you spend roughly KSh 22,000 every two years — KSh 110,000 over ten years, and you own no suit at the end of it.

The made-to-measure path. A made-to-measure suit at KES 55,000, worn three times a week for five years with proper care, is roughly 780 wears. Cost per wear: KES 70. If the suit is still going at year eight — which many well-made suits do — the cost per wear drops to KES 44. You own one suit at the end of it, and it is still yours.

The made-to-measure path becomes cheaper per wear around year three. Before that, the off-the-rack path is cheaper. This is honest math, and it matters: made-to-measure is not always the financial winner. It is the winner if you actually keep the suit and wear it. For a professional who wears suits four or five times a week and expects to do so for the next decade, the made-to-measure path is dramatically cheaper over the full period. For a professional who wears suits twice a week and likes to change their look every two years, the off-the-rack path may be genuinely better value.

The uncomfortable truth: the made-to-measure case is strongest for professionals who already wear suits most days of the week. If you are at that frequency, the math flips within three years and the visual improvement is immediate. If you wear a suit once a week, buy a good off-the-rack suit and have it altered properly. What to Look for in a Made-to-Measure Tailor

If you decide to move forward, the tailor matters as much as the fabric. A made-to-measure suit is only as good as the person drafting the pattern and cutting the cloth. Here is what to look for.

Ask if they draft a new pattern for each client. A true made-to-measure tailor drafts from your measurements, not from a block pattern. If the tailor reaches for a template and adjusts it, that is not made-to-measure — it is adjusted off-the-rack, and it will never fit as well as a proper drafted garment.

Ask how many measurements they take. Twenty is the minimum for a proper made-to-measure suit. Fewer than that, and the tailor is guessing at things like shoulder slope, posture and jacket length — the exact things that make the difference.

Ask about fittings. At least two fittings are non-negotiable. A basted fitting where the garment is loosely stitched together so the tailor can check the drape, and a forward fitting where the details are finalised. If the tailor measures you once and delivers once, you are not getting made-to-measure. You are getting expensive off-the-rack.

Ask about the construction. Full floating horsehair canvas is the standard for a serious suit. Fused construction is cheaper and will bubble within months of regular wear. If the tailor cannot tell you which one they use, that is your answer.

Ask about the guarantee. A professional tailor stands behind their work. If a seam opens in the first year, they fix it. If the fit is off at delivery, they adjust it. The FitFix workmanship guarantee covers seam failure, slider failure and fabric defects for a full year. Ask any tailor what their equivalent is.

How much does a made-to-measure suit cost at FitFix?

Made-to-measure men's suits start from KES 55,000. Executive suiting with Super 150s wool and additional hand-finishing starts higher. Every price is confirmed in writing after the consultation and before any work begins — no surprise charges on delivery.

Do I need made-to-measure if I only wear a suit two or three times a week?

Not necessarily. A well-fitted off-the-rack suit altered properly by a good tailor is often the right choice at that frequency. Made-to-measure becomes the better investment when you wear suits four or more times a week, when your body is difficult to fit off the rack, or when the specific work context — court, boardroom, client-facing — makes the visual signal important. We are honest about this on the call.

What makes a suit look senior?

Three things, in order. The fit, especially the shoulder line and the jacket length. The fabric — real wool reads matte and structured under office lighting. The construction — a floating canvas holds shape over years while a fused suit bubbles. Colour and detailing matter, but a well-fitted navy suit in real wool outclasses a poorly-fitted charcoal suit every time.

How long does a made-to-measure suit take?

Four to six weeks is the standard window. It allows for a basted fitting, a forward fitting, final construction and delivery. Rush service is available at an additional fee where capacity allows, but we are honest that rush jobs compress the fitting process and reduce the margin for correction.

What is the difference between a lawyer's suit, a banker's suit, and a CEO's suit?

Less than people assume, and more than people realise. A lawyer in court needs a conservative cut, deep navy or charcoal, and structured shoulders — the room reads authority first. A banker is often in navy or mid-grey with a sharper waist and lighter finish, sitting across from a client rather than standing in front of a judge. A CEO has the widest latitude and usually settles on quieter fabrics — navy, mid-grey, subtle windowpanes — because confidence does not need decoration. Fit and fabric are the same standard across all three. The cut and colour shift slightly.

Is made-to-measure worth it if I already own good suits?

Sometimes yes, sometimes no. If your existing suits fit you well and are made of real wool, an alteration may be all you need. If your suits fit across the chest but pull at the waist, sit too short in the jacket, or have fused construction that has started to bubble, made-to-measure will give you a cleaner result than repeated alterations can. We assess existing suits honestly on the call before recommending a commission.

What fabric works best for a Nairobi business suit?

Super 120s Italian wool in navy, charcoal or mid-grey is the standard recommendation for Nairobi. It breathes in the heat, holds its shape through a full working day and moves with the body without creasing badly. For senior roles in climate-controlled offices, Super 130s or 150s wool adds a finer hand without sacrificing durability. Lighter summer weights and heavier cold-season weights are available on request.

The Closing Observation

If you are a Nairobi lawyer, banker, CEO or consultant, and you are reading this, you probably recognize some part of the pattern. You have a wardrobe that mostly works. You have thought about upgrading it, at least a few times. And you have not quite got to it.

I want to say something gently. The suit is not the work. The suit does not win the case, close the deal, or earn the promotion. The work does that. But the suit is the thing the room sees before the work has a chance to speak. And the professionals who recognize that early — who treat their wardrobe as part of their professional communication rather than an afterthought — are the ones who look, in the photographs of their own careers, the way they always intended to look.

If you are ready to make that decision, message us. If you are not, that is fine too. Keep wearing the suits you have, keep doing the work. The suit can wait. The work cannot.
