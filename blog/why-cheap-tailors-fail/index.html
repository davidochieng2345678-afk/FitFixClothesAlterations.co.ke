The Mathematical Impossibility of the "Cheap" Tailor

Let's break down the numbers. A high-quality, custom-tailored suit requires premium wool fabric. That fabric alone costs anywhere from KSh 15,000 to KSh 50,000, depending on the mill and the blend. Then there is the thread, the canvas, the buttons, the lining, the zipper, the tailor's time, the overheads of the shop, and the wages of the workers.

If a tailor quotes you KSh 3,000 for a "custom suit," do the math. They are either not buying the fabric at all, or they are using a substandard, plastic material that will fray and shine after a week. But there is a third, much darker possibility—one that we call the Ponzi Scheme of Tailoring.

Before we get into the mechanics of that scheme, let us be absolutely clear about what a legitimate custom garment actually costs to produce. This is not about justifying high prices for the sake of it. It is about understanding the raw economics of an honest tailoring business in Nairobi in 2026.

The True Cost Breakdown of an Honest Custom Suit

When a legitimate tailor quotes you a fair price for a made-to-measure suit, that price is not arbitrary. It is built on a foundation of real, unavoidable costs. Here is what is actually inside the price tag:

  • Fabric (KSh 12,000–KSh 40,000+): A genuine wool suit requires 3.5 to 4 meters of cloth. Premium Italian or British wool costs between KSh 3,000 and KSh 10,000 per meter. Cheap "wool blends" from informal markets cost a fraction of that—but they are mostly polyester, and they will pill, shine, and trap heat.
  • Lining (KSh 1,500–KSh 4,000): Bemberg cupro or silk linings breathe and glide. Polyester linings feel clammy and create static. The difference in cost is significant, and the difference in comfort is night and day.
  • Canvas and Interfacing (KSh 1,000–KSh 3,000): A floating horsehair canvas gives a jacket its shape and longevity. Fused glue interlining is cheap—and it bubbles and delaminates within months.
  • Trims (KSh 800–KSh 2,500): Horn buttons, quality zippers, sleeve heads, shoulder pads, pocketing fabric. These small details add up quickly.
  • Skilled Labour (KSh 12,000–KSh 25,000+): A master tailor spends 30 to 40 hours cutting, basting, fitting, and finishing a bespoke suit. At a fair living wage, that labour is the single largest component of an honest price.
  • Overheads (KSh 5,000–KSh 15,000 per garment): Rent for a professional workshop, electricity for industrial machines, machine maintenance, insurance, business permits, and administrative costs.
  • Fittings and Aftercare: Multiple fitting sessions, pressing, final adjustments, and a workmanship guarantee all cost time and money.

Add it all up, and you arrive at a realistic floor of KSh 40,000 to KSh 50,000 for a genuinely good custom suit—and significantly more for premium fabrics and complex detailing. This is not greed. This is arithmetic.

So when a tailor quotes you KSh 3,000, they are not offering you a discount. They are offering you a completely different product—one built on missing materials, missing labour, or missing ethics. And more often than not, all three are missing at once.

The Ponzi Scheme: How Your Deposit Is Stolen

Most tailors accept money to do the work. They accept a little money that cannot buy the necessary fabrics used to build the made-to-measure garment, tailor the cloth, and pay themselves. That is where the game starts.

Here is exactly how the cycle works:

  • Client A gives the tailor a deposit for a suit. The tailor promises it will be ready in two weeks.
  • The tailor uses part of Client A's money for personal use—paying rent, buying food, settling a personal debt, or paying school fees.
  • Client B gives the tailor a deposit for a dress. The tailor promises it will be ready in ten days.
  • The tailor uses part of Client B's money for personal use.
  • The remaining amount from Client B is finally used to buy the fabric for Client A.
  • The deadline to collect items for Client A and Client B reaches before the work is done. Both clients call, both are told "it's almost ready."
  • Client C gives the tailor money for a new garment. This money is used to remove the tailor from the cell (bail them out of a financial hole) and complete Client A's product.

The cycle goes on and on, an endless loop of robbing Peter to pay Paul. The tailor is not running a tailoring business; they are running a high-stakes cash flow gamble. They are perpetually behind, perpetually stressed, and perpetually lying to their clients. And you, the client, are the one who suffers.

Why This Scheme Feels Normal to the Tailor

What makes this scheme so insidious is that the tailor rarely sets out to defraud anyone. In their own mind, they are not a criminal—they are a survivor. They genuinely believe that if they can just get one more client, one more deposit, one more week, everything will balance out. They convince themselves that the work will get done, that the clients will understand, that the money will come.

But the mathematics of the scheme are unforgiving. Every new deposit creates a new obligation. Every new obligation requires more time than the tailor has. The hole gets deeper with every client they take on. Eventually, the only way to keep the illusion alive is to take on more work than any human being could possibly complete—which is exactly where the Domino Effect begins (we will cover that in Section 2).

"The cheap tailor is not running a business. They are running a live experiment in how long a lie can survive before it collapses."

From the outside, the shop looks normal. There are fabrics on the shelves, a sewing machine in the corner, photos of beautiful suits on the wall. But behind that facade is a frantic, daily scramble to keep the plates spinning. The tailor wakes up every morning already behind. They spend their day answering panicked calls, making excuses, and promising delivery dates they know they cannot meet. It is a miserable way to work—and it produces miserable results for the client.

The Three Types of Cheap Tailors (And Why They All Fail You)

Not every cheap tailor operates the same way. Over fourteen years, we have identified three distinct archetypes of the "bargain" tailor—and each one fails the client in their own unique way. Understanding which type you are dealing with can help you spot the warning signs before you hand over your deposit.

Type 1: The Desperate Hustler

This is the most common type. They are often talented with a needle, but they are terrible with money. They take on every job that walks through the door, regardless of whether they have the capacity to complete it. They live from deposit to deposit, always one client away from financial disaster.

When you engage a Desperate Hustler, the first few days go well. They are responsive, enthusiastic, and full of promises. But as the deadline approaches and the money runs out, they become evasive. Calls go unanswered. Deadlines slip. And the garment that was supposed to be a masterpiece arrives late, rushed, and riddled with compromises.

The warning signs: They push hard for an upfront deposit. They have no written receipts or contracts. They speak vaguely about "sourcing fabric" without showing you any samples. They are always "just about to finish" whatever they are working on.

Type 2: The Backroom Assembler

This tailor is not really a tailor—they are an assembler. They do not cut, draft, or fit properly. They take your measurements, send them to a cheap factory (often in another country), and have the garment mass-produced on a block pattern. The "custom" part is purely marketing.

When you receive the garment, it will look vaguely like what you asked for, but nothing will fit correctly. The shoulders will be too wide or too narrow. The sleeves will be the wrong length. The jacket will feel like it belongs to someone else—because it does. It belongs to the imaginary "average" person the block pattern was designed for.

The warning signs: They do not have a proper cutting table or industrial machines. They cannot show you a work-in-progress garment. They quote suspiciously fast turnaround times for "custom" work. They blame the "factory" when things go wrong.

Type 3: The Vanishing Artist

This is the most dangerous type. They are charming, talented, and convincing. They may even produce genuinely beautiful garments for the first few clients. But their business is built on a house of cards, and when the cards collapse—as they always do—they disappear.

The Vanishing Artist takes deposits from multiple clients, uses the money to fund a lifestyle or a side venture, and then, when the deadlines pile up and the money runs out, they vanish. They change their phone number. They close their shop. They reappear months later in a different part of town under a different name, and the cycle begins again.

The warning signs: They have no verifiable physical address. They operate primarily through social media or WhatsApp. They ask for unusually large deposits. They have no reviews or testimonials from past clients. They are vague about their background and experience.

All three types share one thing in common: they cannot deliver what they promise. And the client—you—is the one who pays the price.

The Psychology of the Bargain: Why Smart People Fall for Cheap Tailors

If cheap tailors are so obviously a bad deal, why do so many intelligent, successful people fall for them? The answer lies in a combination of cognitive biases and emotional needs that the cheap tailor exploits—often without even realizing they are doing it.

1. The Anchoring Effect

When you first start shopping for a custom suit or dress, you look at the premium tailors and see prices like KSh 55,000 or KSh 99,000. These become your mental "anchor"—the reference point against which you judge everything else. When a cheap tailor then quotes you KSh 3,000, your brain immediately compares it to the anchor and screams, "What a deal!"

What your brain fails to do is ask the more important question: "Why is it so cheap?" The anchoring effect makes us focus on the relative saving rather than the absolute value. A KSh 3,000 suit is not a bargain if it falls apart after one wear—it is a KSh 3,000 mistake.

2. The Optimism Bias

We all believe, deep down, that we are the exception. Other people get scammed, but not us. Other people's tailors disappear, but ours will deliver. This optimism bias is powerful—and it is exactly what the cheap tailor relies on.

When you hand over your deposit, you are not just buying a garment. You are buying a story: the story of the hidden gem, the underappreciated master, the bargain that no one else knows about. It is a flattering story, and we want to believe it. The cheap tailor is counting on that.

3. The Sunk Cost Fallacy

Once you have paid a deposit, you are emotionally invested. Even if the tailor starts missing deadlines and making excuses, you are far more likely to "wait it out" than to cut your losses and walk away. The longer you wait, the harder it becomes to abandon the project—because walking away means admitting you were wrong.

The cheap tailor knows this. They know that once they have your deposit, you are far more likely to tolerate delays, excuses, and even outright lies than to start over with someone else. You have become a captive client.

4. The Illusion of Control

Many clients believe that by "keeping a close eye" on the tailor—calling regularly, visiting the shop, sending reminders—they can prevent a bad outcome. But the truth is, you cannot supervise your way out of a broken business model. If the tailor has already spent your deposit on rent, no amount of phone calls will conjure the fabric back into existence.

These psychological traps are powerful. They are why so many otherwise savvy professionals end up in tears on the morning of their wedding. The cheap tailor does not need to be a master manipulator—they just need to understand human nature, and exploit it.

Why Cheap Tailors Keep Getting Away With It

You might be wondering: if cheap tailors are this unreliable, why do they keep getting customers? Why do they keep getting away with it? The answer is a combination of industry structure, consumer behavior, and the sheer difficulty of holding them accountable.

An Unregulated Industry

In Kenya, tailoring is a largely unregulated industry. There is no licensing board, no mandatory certification, and no consumer protection watchdog specifically for tailoring. Anyone with a sewing machine and a shop sign can call themselves a tailor. There is no minimum standard for training, no requirement for a physical address, and no penalty for taking deposits and failing to deliver.

This means that the burden of due diligence falls entirely on you, the consumer. It also means that when things go wrong, your options are limited. You can report the tailor to the police, but for small deposits, the case is unlikely to go anywhere. You can sue, but the legal fees will quickly exceed the value of the garment. Most clients simply cut their losses and move on—which is exactly what the cheap tailor is counting on.

The Word-of-Mouth Problem

Cheap tailors often rely on word-of-mouth recommendations from well-meaning friends and family. Someone's cousin had a good experience, so they recommend the tailor to everyone they know. What they do not know is that the "good experience" was probably a fluke—or that the tailor is now three months behind and about to disappear.

Word-of-mouth is powerful, but it is also unreliable. It does not scale, and it does not account for the fact that a tailor's quality can vary wildly from one client to the next. A tailor who did a great job on your friend's dress last year may be a completely different operation today.

The Review Economy

In the age of Google and social media, you might think that bad tailors would be exposed quickly. But the reality is more complicated. Most cheap tailors operate without a digital footprint. They do not have a Google My Business profile, they do not have a website, and they do not have reviews. They operate entirely in the informal economy, where reputation is built on personal connections and word-of-mouth—not online transparency.

This makes it incredibly difficult for consumers to research a tailor before handing over their money. You cannot read reviews, you cannot see past work, and you cannot verify that the tailor even exists as a legitimate business. You are essentially gambling with your money and your event.

Why People Don't Speak Up

Even when clients are burned, they often do not speak up. There are several reasons for this. First, they may feel embarrassed—they do not want to admit that they were scammed. Second, they may not want to cause conflict—they know the tailor personally, or they were referred by a friend, and they do not want to damage the relationship. Third, they may simply not know where to report the problem. There is no central database of bad tailors, no consumer protection hotline, and no way to warn other potential clients.

The result is a cycle of silence. Each burned client keeps quiet, and the next client walks into the same trap. The cheap tailor continues to operate, and the cycle repeats.

The Myth of the "Backroom Factory"

When you walk into a cheap tailor's shop and see racks of fabric and photos of beautiful garments on the wall, you assume there is a team of people working in the back. You assume there is an assembly line—one person cutting, one person sewing, one person pressing. This is the illusion they want you to believe.

The reality is vastly different. In 99% of these shops, the "factory" is one overworked individual sitting behind a single sewing machine, surrounded by piles of uncompleted work. They do not have a team. They do not have industrial blind-stitchers or specialized pressing tables. They have a basic machine, a pair of scissors, and a desperate need for cash flow.

We have visited these workshops. We have seen the reality behind the glossy Instagram photos. The fabric is piled on the floor. The ironing board is held together with tape. The single fluorescent bulb flickers overhead. This is not a factory. It is a pressure cooker. And you—the client—are about to be added to the pile.

The Capacity Illusion

Here is the fundamental problem: a cheap tailor has almost no capacity to scale. They cannot hire more staff because they cannot afford to pay them. They cannot buy more machines because they have no capital. They cannot turn away clients because they need every deposit to survive.

So they do the only thing they know how to do: they say "yes" to everyone. They take on more work than they can possibly complete, and they hope—against all evidence—that somehow, someway, they will manage to finish it all. This is not optimism. It is delusion.

The result is a workshop that is permanently overwhelmed. Every surface is covered in half-finished garments. Every deadline is missed. Every client is frustrated. And the tailor is trapped in an endless cycle of overcommitment, exhaustion, and lies.

Turning away a client means turning away the money that pays for rent, food, or their child's school fees. They would rather overpromise and underdeliver than admit that they are not capable of taking on more work.

This is a classic trap of the informal economy. The tailor is so focused on surviving today that they cannot see the long-term consequences of their actions. They do not realize that every missed deadline, every botched garment, and every angry client is destroying their reputation and making it even harder to attract future work.

But by the time they realize this, it is usually too late. The damage is done. The clients are gone. And the tailor is left alone in their workshop, surrounded by the wreckage of broken promises.

The Mathematics of Tailoring

It takes a skilled tailor with professional equipment roughly 30 to 40 hours of continuous, focused labor to construct a bespoke suit from scratch. This includes taking measurements, drafting the pattern, cutting the fabric, basting, conducting multiple fittings, setting the canvas, hand-stitching the sleeve heads, and pressing.

If five clients need suits by Saturday, the tailor has just committed to 150 to 200 hours of work in a 96-hour window. It is mathematically impossible. Even if they never slept, they could not finish the work. But instead of being honest with the clients, the tailor begins the frantic scramble that we call the Domino Effect.

Let us break this down even further, because the numbers are more brutal than most clients realize:

  • A bespoke suit: 30–40 hours of skilled labor
  • A custom dress: 15–25 hours of skilled labor
  • A pair of tailored trousers: 8–12 hours of skilled labor
  • A simple alteration (hem, waist): 1–3 hours of skilled labor

Now, imagine a tailor who has taken on five suits, three dresses, and a dozen small alterations in a single week. That is 150–200 hours of suit work, 45–75 hours of dress work, and 12–36 hours of alteration work. Total: 207–311 hours of labor.

Even if the tailor worked 24 hours a day, seven days a week, they would only have 168 hours available. The math is not just tight—it is physically impossible. The tailor has committed to more work than a team of five people could complete in a week.

This is the reality of the cheap tailor's workshop. They are not just behind—they are perpetually, mathematically, hopelessly behind. And no amount of late nights, skipped meals, or frantic sewing can close the gap.

The Hidden Cost of Rushing

When a tailor is forced to work at double or triple their normal speed, quality is the first casualty. Here is what happens when a suit is rushed:

  • Skipped fittings: A proper bespoke suit requires at least two fittings. A rushed suit gets one—or none at all.
  • Shortcut stitching: Hand-stitched details are replaced with machine stitching. Glue is used instead of canvas. Seams are sewn quickly and unevenly.
  • Poorly matched patterns: If your fabric has stripes or checks, matching them at the seams takes time. A rushed tailor skips this step, resulting in a visibly mismatched garment.
  • Burned or damaged fabric: Rushed pressing with a hot iron can scorch the fabric. Rushed cutting can leave jagged edges. Rushed sewing can pucker the seams.
  • Crooked hems and uneven sleeves: These are the hallmarks of a rushed garment—and they are almost impossible to fix without completely re-doing the work.

By the time you receive the garment, it is not just late—it is also compromised. You have paid for a bespoke suit, but you have received a hastily assembled costume.

The Anatomy of a Tailoring Delay: Day by Day

Let's break down exactly how the Domino Effect unfolds over the course of a single week. When a cheap tailor takes on too much work, here is the grim reality of what happens behind the scenes:

Monday: The Illusion of Progress

On Monday, the tailor is still riding the high of collecting five deposits. They buy the fabric for Client A, the one who is screaming the loudest or who has the earliest deadline. They cut the fabric. They sew a few seams. They feel productive. They tell themselves they will catch up on Tuesday.

What they do not realize—or refuse to admit—is that they have already lost the game. Client A's suit alone will take 30–40 hours. They have spent perhaps four hours on it today. That leaves 26–36 hours of work remaining for a suit that needs to be ready in five days. And they have four other clients waiting.

Monday is the day of denial. The tailor convinces themselves that everything is under control. They ignore the growing pile of fabric on the floor. They ignore the unanswered WhatsApp messages from Client B and Client C. They focus on the one garment in front of them, and they tell themselves that tomorrow will be better.

Tomorrow will not be better.

Tuesday: The Bottleneck

On Tuesday, Client A's suit requires the internal canvas to be hand-stitched and the sleeve heads to be set. This is the most time-consuming part of tailoring. It requires absolute focus. It requires patience. It requires a quiet, undisturbed workspace.

But the tailor's workspace is anything but quiet. The phone is ringing. Client B is calling to ask if they can come in for their final fitting. Client C is sending messages on WhatsApp asking for a progress update. Client D has shown up at the shop in person, demanding to see their garment. Client E's mother has called to say her daughter's dress needs to be ready by Thursday, not Saturday, because the church function has been moved.

The tailor is distracted. They rush the canvas. They skip a step. They tell Client B to come in tomorrow. They tell Client D that the garment is "in the final stages." They tell Client E's mother that Thursday is no problem.

Super Numbers Explained: What 120s, 150s, and 180s Actually Mean

You will often hear tailors brag about "Super 120s" or "Super 150s" wool. But what does that actually mean? The "Super" number refers to the fineness of the wool fiber—specifically, the number of times the yarn can be spun from a single strand of wool. The higher the number, the finer, softer, and more expensive the fabric.

  • Super 100s: A durable, everyday wool. Good for suits that will be worn frequently.
  • Super 120s: A premium wool with a smooth finish and excellent drape. Ideal for business suits.
  • Super 130s: A finer, softer wool with a luxurious hand-feel. Suitable for special occasions.
  • Super 150s: A very fine, delicate wool. Beautiful but less durable. Best for occasional wear.
  • Super 180s and above: Extremely fine, almost silk-like wool. Very expensive and very delicate. Reserved for the highest-end bespoke garments.

At FitFix, we use Super 120s and 150s wool for our made-to-measure suits. This is the sweet spot: fine enough to feel luxurious, durable enough to wear regularly. A cheap tailor claiming to offer "Super 150s" for KSh 3,000 is either lying about the fabric or using a counterfeit label.

2. The Canvas: The Hidden Skeleton

A good suit jacket has a "floating canvas"—a layer of horsehair and wool woven into the chest and lapels. This gives the jacket its shape and allows it to mold to your body over time. The canvas alone costs money, and installing it requires hours of hand-stitching.

Cheap suits use a "fused" construction. They glue a layer of synthetic interlining to the fabric using a heat press. It takes minutes, not hours, and it costs pennies. But after a few months, the glue breaks down, causing the fabric to bubble, wrinkle, and lose its shape permanently.

The Fused vs. Canvassed Test

How can you tell if a suit is fused or canvassed? Here is a simple test you can do in the shop:

  • The Pinch Test: Pinch the fabric of the jacket between your thumb and forefinger, just below the collar. If you can feel a third layer floating between the outer fabric and the lining, it is canvassed. If it feels flat and stiff, it is fused.
  • The Drape Test: Hold the jacket up by the shoulders and let it hang. A canvassed jacket will drape naturally, following the contours of the garment. A fused jacket will feel stiff and rigid, like a cardboard cutout.
  • The Wrinkle Test: Squeeze a section of the jacket in your hand and release it. A canvassed jacket will spring back to its original shape. A fused jacket will remain wrinkled, because the glue has already begun to break down.

These tests are not foolproof, but they are a good starting point. If a tailor cannot tell you whether their suits are fused or canvassed, they are not a professional.

The Anatomy of a Tailor's Excuses: The Top 7 Lies

1. The "Fabric Delay" Lie

This is the most common excuse in Nairobi. "The fabric hasn't arrived from the supplier," they tell you. "There was a delay at the factory." They will tell you this with a straight face, even though you know the local fabric market in town is open every single day.

Another classic. "My sewing machine broke down," they say. "I'm waiting for a mechanic." It sounds plausible. Machines do break.

The Truth: Their machine might be fine, or if it is broken, it is because it is a cheap, second-hand machine that they refuse to service. How to counter it: Ask to see the broken machine. Ask when the mechanic is coming. Offer to recommend a good repair technician. If they cannot show you the machine or give you a specific repair date, they are lying.

How to counter it: Ask for a photo of the garment. Ask what specific "final touches" are remaining. Ask if you can come and see it in person right now. If they refuse, the garment does not exist.

The Gaslighting Playbook: 6 Techniques Bad Tailors Use

Gaslighting is not just a vague feeling. It is a set of specific, repeatable techniques. Here are six of the most common ones:

The Block Pattern Trap: What "Custom" Really Means

When a cheap tailor takes your measurements, they are not drafting a new pattern. They are reaching for a pre-existing "block pattern"—a standard template for a size 40 Regular, a size 42 Long, or a size 38 Short.

These templates are mass-produced and designed for an imaginary "average" human being. But an average human being does not exist. You might have the chest of a size 42, the waist of a size 40, and a stooped posture that requires a completely different shoulder cut.

The cheap tailor will take your size 42 block, lay it on the fabric, cut it, and sew it together. If it is too big in the waist, they will hack at the side seams at the very end to take it in. It results in a garment that feels fundamentally wrong, no matter how many alterations they attempt.

The History of the Block Pattern

To understand why the block pattern is such a problem, you need to understand where it came from. Block patterns were not designed for bespoke tailoring. They were designed for mass production—specifically, for military uniforms in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

When armies needed to clothe thousands of soldiers quickly, they could not draft individual patterns for each man. Instead, they developed a system of standard sizes—Small, Medium, Large, and so on—based on statistical averages. Each size was represented by a block pattern, and each soldier was assigned the size that fit him best.

This system worked well for its purpose. It allowed armies to produce uniforms at scale, quickly and cheaply. But it was never intended for bespoke tailoring. It was never intended to produce garments that fit individual bodies with precision. It was a compromise—a way to clothe the masses, not a way to dress the individual.

When cheap tailors adopted the block pattern system, they inherited all of its limitations. They are using a tool designed for mass production to create garments that are supposed to be custom. This is a fundamental contradiction, and it is why their garments never quite fit.

This is partially true, but only up to a point. A skilled tailor can make small adjustments to a well-made garment. But a block pattern is fundamentally the wrong shape for your body. No amount of alteration can fix a garment that was never designed for you.

This is why we say that a block pattern cannot be "customized." It can only be hacked at. And the result will never be a garment that fits you properly.

The Missing Fitting: The "One and Done" Disaster

In true bespoke tailoring, the process involves multiple fittings. First, there is the "basted fitting," where the garment is loosely stitched together to check the overall balance and drape. What Happens at a Real Fitting

To understand what you are missing, let us walk you through what actually happens at a real bespoke fitting. This is the process that a master tailor follows—and it is the process that the cheap tailor skips entirely.

The Basted Fitting: This is the first fitting, and it is the most important. The garment is held together with temporary white stitches—basting stitches—so it can be adjusted without leaving permanent marks on the fabric. At this fitting, the tailor checks:

  • The balance of the jacket—does it hang straight, or does it pull to one side?
  • The shoulder line—does it sit flat on your shoulders, or does it slope or lift?
  • The sleeve pitch—do the sleeves hang naturally, or do they twist when you move?
  • The collar—does it hug your neck, or does it gap at the back?
  • The chest—is there enough room to move, or does the jacket pull when you breathe?
  • The waist—is the suppression in the right place, or does it look forced?
  • The length—does the jacket cover your seat, or does it stop too short?

At the basted fitting, the tailor is looking for problems. They are looking for the ways the garment does not fit your body. They are looking for the adjustments that need to be made before the garment is permanently sewn. This is the moment where a master tailor earns their fee.

The Forward Fitting: This is the second fitting, and it happens after the basted fitting has been adjusted. At this stage, the collar, sleeves, pockets, and buttonholes are finalized. The tailor checks:

  • The sleeve length—are the sleeves the right length to show a quarter-inch of shirt cuff?
  • The button stance—are the buttons in the right place for your body?
  • The pocket placement—are the pockets in the right place for your hand to reach comfortably?
  • The lapel roll—does the lapel roll naturally, or does it look forced?
  • The vents—do the vents lie flat, or do they flare open?

At the forward fitting, the tailor is fine-tuning the details. They are making sure that every element of the garment is in the right place. They are making sure that the garment is not just wearable, but beautiful.

The Final Fitting: This is the last fitting, and it happens after the garment is complete. The tailor checks the overall fit one last time, ensures that the garment has been properly pressed, and presents it to the client. If there are any final adjustments needed—a button moved, a seam let out—they are done at this stage.

This is what a real bespoke process looks like. It is a collaboration between the tailor and the client. It is a series of conversations, adjustments, and refinements. It is slow, meticulous, and deeply personal.

The Anatomy of a Ghosting: How It Unfolds

Ghosting is not usually a sudden event. It is a process—a gradual withdrawal that unfolds over days or weeks. Understanding this process can help you recognize the warning signs before it is too late.

At first, everything seems fine. The tailor is responsive and friendly. They answer your messages promptly. Stage 2: The Slowing

Then, the responses start to slow down. A message that used to be answered in minutes now takes hours. A phone call that used to be answered on the first ring now goes to voicemail. You tell yourself that the tailor must be busy. You give them the benefit of the doubt.

But the slowing is not an accident. It is a deliberate strategy. The tailor is testing how much silence you will tolerate. If you do not complain, they will continue to withdraw. If you do complain, they will offer a new excuse and buy themselves a little more time.

When you finally do complain, the tailor responds with an excuse. "My phone was stolen." "I was in the hospital." "There was a family emergency." "I had to travel out of town." If you accept the excuse, the cycle returns to Stage 1. The tailor reassures you, promises to deliver soon, and then begins to withdraw again. If you do not accept the excuse, the tailor moves to Stage 4.

Stage 4: The Silence

This is the final stage. The tailor stops responding entirely. Your calls go straight to voicemail. Your messages are left unread. You go to the shop, and it is closed—perhaps permanently. In the informal economy, there are few consequences for ghosting. The police are unlikely to pursue a case over a few thousand shillings. The legal system is too slow and expensive to be practical. The tailor can simply change their phone number, move to a new location, and start over.

Hold the fabric up to your mouth and try to breathe through it. If air passes through easily, the fabric is breathable. If you feel resistance, or if you cannot breathe through it at all, the fabric is probably synthetic. This is a simple but effective way to assess breathability.

4. The Wrinkle Test

Scrunch a section of the fabric in your hand and release it. Natural fabrics like wool will spring back to their original shape, with minimal wrinkling. Synthetic fabrics will often remain wrinkled, because the plastic fibers do not have the elasticity of natural fibers. A fabric that wrinkles permanently after a light scrunch is not a good sign.

5. The Burn Test

This is a more advanced test, and you should only do it on a small sample or a loose thread—not on a garment in a shop. But if you have a sample, you can burn it and observe the results. Natural fibers like wool, cotton, and silk will burn with a smell like burnt hair or burnt paper, and the ash will crumble easily. Synthetic fibers will melt, smell like plastic, and leave a hard, bead-like residue.

6. The Label Test

Always check the label. A high-quality fabric will list its composition clearly—for example, "100% Wool" or "95% Wool, 5% Cashmere." A cheap fabric will often use vague language like "Wool Blend" or "Premium Fabric" without specifying the exact composition. If the label is vague or unreadable, ask the tailor for clarification. If they cannot or will not tell you, walk away.

7. The Water Test

Drop a small amount of water on the fabric. Natural fibers like wool will absorb the water slowly, and the fabric will feel damp. Synthetic fibers will repel the water, and it will bead on the surface. This test can tell you a lot about the breathability and comfort of the fabric.

These tests are not perfect, but they can help you avoid the worst offenders. The more you handle high-quality fabrics, the more you will develop a sense for what is genuine and what is cheap.
