The Anatomy of a Suit That Actually Fits

By David Ochieng · Master Tailor, FitFix Nairobi · 9 min read

Note on this post: What follows is what I see on the bench, drawn from fourteen years of tailoring and from the thousands of measurements I have taken. There is no fabricated customer story here. The purpose is to explain what "fit" actually means as an engineering problem, and how twenty measurements translate into a suit that follows your body rather than a template. If you want the short version of what to look for when trying on a suit in a shop, that is at the end.

Most people think they know what a well-fitted suit looks like. They do not. They know what a suit that is clearly too small or clearly too big looks like. But the subtle failures — the shoulder that sits half a centimetre too wide, the jacket that stops two centimetres above where it should, the trousers that pull at the crotch when the wearer sits — those are the failures that quietly mark a suit as cheap even when the wearer cannot say why.

Fit is not a mystery. It is a set of measurements, a pattern drafted from those measurements, and two fittings to confirm the pattern matches the moving, breathing body it was designed for. When a tailor says "twenty measurements," they mean twenty separate points of data that together describe how your body is shaped. Lose any one of them and the suit will feel off, even if nobody can see exactly why.

This post walks through what those measurements are, what each one actually tells the tailor, and what the finished suit should look like if they were taken properly. If you are trying to understand why one tailor's suits feel better than another's, this is the answer.

Why the "Average Body" Does Not Exist

The single biggest problem with off-the-rack suits is not the fabric. It is not the construction. It is the assumption behind them — the assumption that there is such a thing as an "average" man and that a size 40 Regular fits that average man reasonably well.

There is no average man. If you take a thousand men of roughly the same height and measure them, you will find almost no two with identical proportions. One has a longer torso and shorter legs. Another has wider shoulders and a narrower waist. Another has one shoulder that sits a centimetre lower than the other — which is entirely normal and affects roughly forty per cent of the population. Another has a full chest that makes the front of a jacket pull. Another has a flatter seat that makes trousers hang poorly.

Off-the-rack suits accommodate none of this. They are drafted for a body that does not exist and adjusted with a few side seams, then sold as if they were universal. The result is that the vast majority of Nairobi professionals walk around wearing suits that fit them in the shoulders and chest, and fail everywhere else. They tug at the waist when they sit. The trousers ride up when they cross their legs. The jacket creases across the back. None of it is catastrophic. All of it is visible, in photographs and in the room.

Made-to-measure solves this by abandoning the average body entirely. The pattern is drafted from your measurements. It is not adjusted to fit you; it is built to fit you from the first cut. This is what twenty measurements buy. Not a "better fit" — a fit.

The 20 Measurements, and What Each One Actually Reveals

Different tailors count slightly differently. Some divide the jacket into more components, some combine measurements that others separate. The following is the FitFix list — the twenty numbers I take for every made-to-measure suit. What matters is not the exact count but whether the tailor is measuring the points that determine fit.

Measurement What it tells the tailor
NeckCollar size, and indirectly the shirt size the jacket will sit over. Too tight and the collar digs into the throat when the wearer turns. Too loose and the collar gaps at the back.
Chest (fullest point)The base size of the jacket. Measured around the fullest part of the chest, not at the armpits, because that is where the jacket actually sits.
Waist (natural)The narrowest point above the hips. Combined with the chest, this gives the "drop" — the difference between the two, which determines whether the jacket tapers or looks boxy.
Seat (fullest point)The fullest part of the seat. Determines how the trousers hang. A flat seat needs a different cut from a fuller one to avoid the trousers sagging at the back.
Shoulder width (left and right)Measured separately. Most bodies are not perfectly symmetrical. The two numbers are drafted into the pattern asymmetrically so the jacket sits level.
Shoulder slopeThe angle from the base of the neck to the shoulder point. Determines whether the jacket needs padded shoulders, no padding, or padding on one side only.
Sleeve length (left and right)Measured separately, from shoulder to wrist bone, with the arm at rest. This is what tells the tailor where the sleeve should end so a quarter-inch of shirt cuff shows.
BicepUpper arm circumference. Determines the sleeve width. A tight sleeve pulls at the elbow when the arm bends; too loose and it billows.
WristCuff circumference. Determines how the sleeve sits over the watch or shirt cuff.
Jacket length (back and front)Measured from base of collar to hem, at back and front. This is where the jacket stops on the body — the single measurement that most affects how the whole figure reads.
InseamInside leg, crotch to ankle. The base measurement for trouser length.
OutseamOutside leg, waist to ankle. Together with the inseam, this determines the trouser rise.
RiseWaist to crotch, front and back. Determines how high the trousers sit and how they behave when the wearer sits down. Off-the-rack rise is one of the most common failure points for men with longer torsos.
ThighFullest part of the thigh. Determines trouser leg width at the top. A suit trouser needs room to move without billowing.
KneeKnee circumference. Determines how sharply the trouser tapers below the knee.
CalfCalf circumference. Determines how the trouser drapes at the ankle.
Ankle / hem widthThe opening at the bottom of the trouser. Combined with shoe size and style preference, this determines the final look of the trouser.
Back width (across shoulders)Measured across the shoulder blades. Determines the back panel of the jacket and how it moves when the wearer reaches forward or rotates.
Stomach (front projection)How far the front of the body projects at the waist. A prominent stomach needs the jacket to be cut with more room at the front and a lower button stance.
PostureNot a number but an observation. Erect, normal, stooped. Recorded alongside the measurements because it shifts how the pattern is drafted. A stooped back needs a different jacket front than an erect one.

That is the list. None of these measurements is optional. Each one feeds a specific decision in the pattern. A tailor who takes only four — chest, waist, shoulder, sleeve — is guessing at everything else, and the guesswork shows up in the finished garment.

What Each Measurement Actually Changes in the Finished Suit

Numbers alone do not mean much. Here is what each measurement actually produces in the suit you wear.

The shoulder line

The shoulder measurements — width and slope — are the single biggest determinant of whether a jacket reads as bespoke or as off-the-rack. A properly drafted shoulder sits level on your body, ends exactly at the shoulder point, and does not pull or pucker. On an off-the-rack suit, the shoulder is the first thing to fail because it cannot be adjusted without rebuilding the entire jacket. If your off-the-rack suit has a dropped shoulder that does not match yours, no amount of alteration will fix it. The made-to-measure suit drafts it correctly from the start.

The jacket length

The jacket length is the second most important measurement. It determines the overall proportion of the figure. The jacket should cover the seat — the base of the seat, not the top — and this is where most off-the-rack jackets fail for men who are shorter or taller than the template assumes. A jacket that is two centimetres too short makes the torso look compressed and the legs look shorter. A jacket that is two centimetres too long looks dated and swallows the wearer. The made-to-measure suit is drafted to sit exactly at the seat, whatever body type you have.

The drop

The drop — the difference between chest and waist — is what makes a jacket look like it was made for the wearer rather than borrowed. A well-drafted jacket tapers from chest to waist in a clean line. On an off-the-rack suit with a standard 6-inch drop, a man with an 8-inch drop ends up with a boxy jacket and a waist that billows. He can have it taken in, but the alteration pulls the pockets and changes the shape. Made-to-measure drafts the drop into the pattern, so the taper is inherent to the cut.

The rise

Rise is where off-the-rack trousers fail most visibly. A standard rise sits at the hip bone. On a man with a longer torso, that means the trousers sit too low and the crotch pulls. On a man with a shorter torso, the trousers sit too high and look uncomfortable. Made-to-measure trousers are drafted to the correct rise for the individual body — where the waist actually is, not where the average is. The result is a trouser that sits where it should and moves with the body rather than against it.

The armhole

The armhole is not one of the twenty measurements in the strict sense — it is derived from the chest, shoulder and bicep measurements combined. But it is the single feature that separates a suit the wearer can move in from one they cannot. A properly drafted armhole is high and small, which allows the arm to move freely without the entire jacket lifting. A poor armhole is low and large, and the jacket shifts with every arm movement. Off-the-rack suits almost always have armholes that are too low, because they are cut for a wider range of body types and the easiest way to accommodate more bodies is to give more room everywhere.

The Two Fittings, and What Each One Catches

A made-to-measure suit is not finished when the measurements are taken. It is finished when the wearer has tried it on twice and both the tailor and the wearer are satisfied. The two fittings each serve a different purpose.

The basted fitting

This is the fitting where the suit is held together with temporary white stitching — basting stitches — and the garment is loose enough to be adjusted without cutting or re-sewing. At this fitting, the tailor checks:

  • The balance of the jacket — does it hang straight, or does it pull to one side?
  • The shoulder line — is it level on both sides, or does one side sit lower?
  • The sleeve pitch — do the sleeves hang naturally, or do they twist?
  • The collar — does it hug the neck, or does it gap at the back?
  • The chest — is there room to breathe without the jacket pulling?
  • The waist — does the suppression look natural, or does it look forced?
  • The jacket length — does it cover the seat at the right point?
  • The trouser rise — is the trouser sitting where the wearer wants it?

The basted fitting is where the tailor is still able to fix things. After this fitting, the garment is re-cut and re-sewn according to the corrections made. It is the last chance to correct a shoulder that is too wide, a balance that is off, or a jacket that is too short. A tailor who skips this fitting is not doing made-to-measure — they are guessing.

The forward fitting

This is the second fitting, after the corrections from the basted fitting have been applied. The garment is now close to finished. At the forward fitting, the tailor finalises:

  • The sleeve length — is a quarter-inch of shirt cuff showing at the wrist?
  • The button stance — are the buttons positioned at the right point on the torso?
  • The pocket placement — can the wearer reach into the pockets comfortably?
  • The lapel roll — does the lapel roll naturally, or does it look pressed flat?
  • The vents — do they lie flat, or do they flare open when the wearer stands?

After the forward fitting, the suit is finished. It is pressed, delivered and ready to wear. If everything went well at the basted fitting, the forward fitting is a matter of refinement rather than correction. If the basted fitting was skipped, the forward fitting becomes a matter of discovering problems that can no longer be fixed.

What the two fittings are not: they are not the same as a "try-on." A try-on is what happens when you collect a finished suit from a shop and put it on for the first time. A fitting is a conversation between the tailor and the body the suit is being built for. If a tailor offers only a try-on and calls it a fitting, the suit is not made-to-measure.

What Fit Can and Cannot Fix

Not every fit problem can be solved by any suit. This is where honesty matters more than salesmanship. Here is the spectrum.

Easy to fix (any good tailor)

Sleeve length, trouser hem, waist suppression, seat adjustment, minor jacket length changes within the seam allowance. These are the alterations that can be done on an off-the-rack suit for a few thousand shillings and are often enough to make a mediocre suit wearable.

Fixable at a cost (a specialist tailor)

Shoulder reduction, jacket lengthening (if there is enough seam allowance inside), armhole adjustment, collar reshaping. These take more time and skill and can cost more than the original suit was worth. On a good suit, they are worth doing. On a cheap suit, they are not, because the underlying fabric and construction are still cheap.

Not fixable at any price

The fundamental balance of the jacket, the armhole position if it is too low, the drop if it is too far off, the collar shape on a jacket that was cut for a different neck type. These are structural problems that come from the original pattern. No amount of alteration can change them. The only fix is a different suit, drafted correctly from the start.

This is why a made-to-measure suit is worth the investment for professionals who wear suits regularly. It is not just that the suit looks better on day one. It is that the suit looks better on day five hundred, because the pattern was right to begin with and no amount of re-alteration has pulled it out of shape.

The Quick Test: What to Look for in the Mirror

If you are trying on a suit in a shop and want to know whether it actually fits, here are the eleven things to check. You do not need a tailor to see most of them.

  1. The shoulder line. It should end exactly at your shoulder point, not half a centimetre past it. The sleeve should start where your arm actually starts.
  2. The shoulder symmetry. Look in the mirror straight on. If one shoulder looks higher than the other, the jacket is not drafted for your body — or you need an asymmetrical cut, which off-the-rack cannot provide.
  3. The chest button. With the jacket buttoned, you should be able to slide your palm flat under the lapel without the fabric pulling. If it pulls, the jacket is too tight.
  4. The waist suppression. The jacket should taper from chest to waist, not hang straight down. If it looks like a box on you, the drop is wrong for your body.
  5. The jacket length. Stand with arms at your sides. The hem of the jacket should sit at the base of your seat, roughly where your knuckles are if you curl your fingers. If it sits higher, the jacket is too short. If it sits lower, it is too long.
  6. The sleeve length. With arms at your sides, a quarter-inch of shirt cuff should show at the wrist. More than that looks too long; less looks too short.
  7. The collar. When you turn your head, the collar should not lift off the back of your neck. If it does, the collar shape is wrong for your neck.
  8. The trouser rise. The waist of the trouser should sit at your natural waist, not your hips. If it sits at your hips, the rise is too low for your body.
  9. The trouser drape. The trouser should fall in a straight line from hip to hem without a break at the knee or a bulge at the thigh. If it pulls at the crotch or the knee, it is too tight.
  10. The trouser hem. There should be a slight break — a small fold of fabric at the top of the shoe. Not a full pool of fabric, not a high-water gap. Just a small break.
  11. The pockets. With the jacket buttoned, you should be able to put your hand into the jacket pocket without the front of the jacket pulling. If it pulls, the jacket is too tight across the chest.

If a suit passes all eleven, it fits. If it fails three or four, it does not. If it fails five or more, the pattern is wrong for your body and no amount of alteration will fix it. That is when you start considering made-to-measure.

Eight Questions About Fit, Answered

How many measurements does a made-to-measure suit need?

At FitFix we take over twenty measurements for a made-to-measure suit. A block-pattern tailor takes four or five. The difference is not marketing — it is the difference between a garment that follows your body and a garment that follows an average nobody actually matches. Twenty is the practical minimum for a suit that accounts for shoulder slope, posture, jacket length, drop and seat.

What if I have one shoulder lower than the other?

That is normal, and it is exactly what made-to-measure is for. Off-the-rack suits are cut on a symmetrical pattern, so a dropped shoulder creates a jacket that sits unevenly and wrinkles on one side. In a made-to-measure suit the shoulder line is drafted asymmetrically to match your body. The suit hangs level even though your shoulders do not.

Can any suit be altered to fit perfectly?

No. Some things can be changed easily — sleeve length, waist, hem, seat. Some things can be changed at a cost — shoulders, jacket length. Some things cannot be changed at all — the fundamental balance of the jacket, the armhole position, the collar shape. A good tailor can tell you in a five-minute consultation which of your existing suits are worth altering and which are not.

How long does a made-to-measure suit take?

Four to six weeks is the standard window. It allows for a basted fitting, a forward fitting, final construction and delivery. Rush service is available at an additional fee where capacity allows, but we are honest that rush jobs compress the fitting process and reduce the margin for correction.

What is the difference between a basted fitting and a forward fitting?

A basted fitting is where the garment is held together with temporary white stitching so the tailor can see the balance and drape on your actual body and make corrections before the final fabric is cut. A forward fitting is where the collar, sleeves, pockets and buttonholes are finalised. Both are essential for a made-to-measure suit. A tailor who skips the basted fitting is not doing made-to-measure — they are doing high-priced off-the-rack.

What is the "drop" and why does it matter?

The drop is the difference between your chest measurement and your waist measurement. A man with a 40-inch chest and a 34-inch waist has a 6-inch drop. A man with a 40-inch chest and a 32-inch waist has an 8-inch drop. Off-the-rack suits typically use a 6-inch drop, so anyone with an 8-inch drop ends up with a jacket that looks boxy around the waist. In a made-to-measure suit the drop is drafted to your actual body, so the jacket tapers to your actual waist.

The Point of All of This

A suit is not a piece of clothing. It is a small piece of architecture that either follows the body or fights it. When it follows the body, the wearer forgets they are wearing it. When it fights the body, the wearer spends the whole day tugging at the lapels, adjusting the cuffs, and hoping nobody notices. The room notices. Not always consciously, but the room notices.

The twenty measurements exist because the body is not a template. Every measurement is a decision that the tailor makes — a decision about how the jacket should sit, how the trousers should hang, how the shoulder should fall. A tailor who takes only four measurements is making those same decisions, but by guessing. A tailor who takes twenty is making them with information.

If you have a suit that fits you well, you already know what a difference this makes. If you have never had one, this post is the reason to try. Not because made-to-measure is a luxury, but because it is the only way to get a suit that follows your body rather than someone else's average.
