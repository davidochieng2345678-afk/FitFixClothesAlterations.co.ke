What Happens to a Suit After 47 Wears

By David Ochieng · Master Tailor, FitFix Nairobi · 8 min read

What "47 Wears" Actually Means

Forty-seven wears in one year works out to roughly once a week, plus a few extra. This is a realistic number for a working professional in a suit-heavy role: a banker, a lawyer, a consultant, a senior government official, a corporate executive. Someone who is in a suit four or five times a week will wear it 200 times a year. Someone who wears it once a week will wear it fifty times a year. Forty-seven sits in the "regular but not extreme" bracket.

The question is: what does the suit look like at wear forty-seven? And what does it look like at wear two hundred and forty-seven, which is what five years of wear at that rate comes to?

The Cost-Per-Wear Math, Honestly

I want to give you the real numbers, because most pages on this subject hide the awkward middle part of the calculation.

The cheap suit: a KSh 3,000 to KSh 5,000 fused suit from a backroom tailor will typically survive twenty to thirty wears before the shine, the bubbling, or the seam failure makes it unwearable. Call it twenty-five wears at KSh 4,000. That is KSh 160 per wear.

The made-to-measure suit: a KES 55,000 FitFix suit worn forty-seven times in the first year works out to KSh 1,170 per wear in year one. On those numbers alone, the cheap suit is dramatically cheaper per wear. Anyone who tells you otherwise in year one is doing the math dishonestly.

The math flips over time. If you wear the same suit forty-seven times a year for five years — which is 235 wears — the cost per wear drops to KSh 234. By year ten, at the same rate, it is KSh 117 per wear. This is the point at which the made-to-measure suit overtakes the cheap suit, and it is a real point — but it takes time and it takes actually wearing the suit.

So the honest summary is this: a made-to-measure suit is not automatically cheaper. It becomes cheaper only if you wear it enough times. For a professional who wears a suit weekly, the math flips within five years. For someone who wears a suit ten times a year, the math never flips — and if that is you, an off-the-rack suit altered by a good tailor is genuinely the right call, and I will tell you that in the consultation.

Where I am being honest about the limits of the argument: the made-to-measure case is not "it lasts forever, so it is always cheaper." It is "if you wear a suit more than once a week, you will almost certainly wear a made-to-measure suit more often than you would wear a cheap one, and the combination of longer life and more wears makes the math work in your favour." If you wear a suit rarely, the math does not work in your favour. Buy a good off-the-rack suit and have it altered.

What Actually Wears Out on a Suit

This is the part that most pages skip, and it is the part that matters. A made-to-measure suit does not stay new forever. It degrades in specific, predictable ways, and knowing those ways tells you when to repair, when to replace, and how to make the suit last longer.

These are the wear patterns I see on the bench. The wear numbers are approximate, based on suits I have examined at various ages. They are not formulas — different fabrics and different wear habits shift them — but they are honest ranges.

The trouser knee

The knee is the first place most suits show visible wear. The fabric compresses at the bend of the knee, the fibres flatten, and eventually the surface takes on a shine that no amount of pressing can remove. For a suit worn weekly, this typically appears between wear thirty and wear fifty. A pure wool trouser holds longer than a wool-synthetic blend. A regular rotation of at least two trousers (which is why the "one suit, one wear log" premise is a bad idea in practice) roughly doubles the lifespan.

The trouser seat

The seat of the trousers wears at the seam where the fabric folds when you sit. This shows up around the same time as the knee shine — sometimes slightly later, sometimes slightly earlier, depending on how much you sit versus stand. The good news is that the seat is fixable: a competent tailor can let out the seam or patch it invisibly, and the garment carries on.

The lining at the underarm

The underarm is where sweat, friction and movement combine. A Bemberg cupro lining will typically survive 200 to 300 wears before it needs replacing. A cheaper polyester lining will often fail at half that. If you are choosing fabric for a suit you intend to wear weekly, this is one of the strongest arguments for the more expensive lining.

The jacket cuffs

The cuffs take constant friction against desk edges, car doors and computer keyboards. The surface of the fabric can wear through to a shine after roughly 100 to 150 wears. A tailor can turn the cuffs and give the garment several more years of wear, but at some point the cuffs are worn enough that turning them becomes necessary.

The lapel edge

The edge of the lapel is where the fabric takes the most friction against the wearer's chest and arms. A well-finished hand-stitched lapel will survive many years; a machine-finished edge will fray faster. A small fray on the lapel edge is a five-minute repair at FitFix, and we handle it on every suit we make.

The buttons

Buttons loosen over time. This is normal and expected, and it is the cheapest repair on the suit — KSh 300 to KSh 500 per button at FitFix, and no more than that. A button that loosens does not mean the suit is failing; it means the suit is being worn.

What does not break

What does not break, if the suit is well made, is the canvas. A floating horsehair canvas, stitched not glued, will hold its shape through hundreds of wears. The chest will stay structured, the lapels will stay rolled, and the shoulders will stay even. This is the part of the suit you are actually paying for, and it is the part that a fused suit cannot match.

How to Make Any Suit Last Longer

The single biggest factor in suit longevity is not the price. It is rotation. A KES 55,000 suit worn every single day will fail faster than a KSh 15,000 suit worn three times a week. If you are serious about getting maximum life out of any suit, these six habits matter more than the price tag.

Rotate. A suit needs at least a full day to recover between wears — the wool fibres expand with body heat and moisture, and they need time to contract back. Two suits worn alternately will each last twice as long as either suit worn alone.

Brush after wear. A soft clothes brush removes dust and loose fibres before they work their way into the fabric. Thirty seconds after each wear is worth more than an extra dry clean.

Hang properly. A wide wooden hanger, not a wire one, and space in the wardrobe so the suit is not crushed against other garments. Rest the trousers along the crease on the hanger bar, not on a clip.

Dry-clean sparingly. Two to four times a year for a weekly-worn suit is enough. Dry cleaning is chemically harsh. If a suit looks and smells clean and the fabric does not feel stiff, it does not need cleaning.

Steam, don't press. A hand steamer will lift out most wrinkles without exposing the wool to direct heat. If you must press, do it through a pressing cloth.

Repair early. A button that loosens at wear thirty-seven is a five-minute fix. Ignore it, and the button is gone by wear forty. The same applies to a fraying lapel edge, a loose cuff, or a small tear at the pocket. Repair early and the suit carries on. Wait, and the repair costs more.

How many wears can I realistically get out of a made-to-measure suit?

A well-made wool suit worn in rotation with proper care will typically last 200 to 400 wears before the fabric is too worn for professional use. The upper end assumes the suit is dry-cleaned sparingly, hung properly after each wear, and given a brush-down. A cheap fused suit rarely gets past 20 to 30 wears.

What wears out first on a made-to-measure suit?

In our experience on the bench, the trouser knee and seat show shine before the fabric fails, typically between wear 30 and 50 for a suit worn weekly. The lining at the underarm is the second most common wear point. The jacket cuffs and lapel edges come third. Good canvas construction, quality lining and proper rotation spread the wear more evenly than a fused suit does.

Is it cheaper per wear to buy one made-to-measure suit or five cheap suits?

It depends entirely on how often you wear a suit. If you wear a suit three or more times a week, the made-to-measure suit becomes cheaper per wear within the first year. If you wear a suit fewer than twelve times a year, five cheap suits over five years can genuinely cost less per wear — and we are honest about that when we quote a client.

How often should a suit be dry-cleaned?

Far less than most people think. Wool is naturally self-cleaning to a large extent, and dry cleaning is chemically harsh. For a suit worn weekly, we recommend dry cleaning two to four times a year, spot-cleaning small marks in between, and brushing the suit down after each wear.

What is the difference between a fused suit and a canvased suit for wear?

A fused suit has its interlining glued to the outer fabric. Under heat, moisture and movement, that glue breaks down and the fabric bubbles and separates — typically within 20 to 40 wears. A canvas suit has a floating horsehair canvas that is stitched, not glued. It molds to the body and, if cared for, will maintain its shape for hundreds of wears.

The Honest Summary

If you wear a suit once a week, a made-to-measure suit will be cheaper per wear than a rotation of cheap suits, but only if you actually keep the suit for five years. Most people do not — they replace it after three, or they damage it, or they buy a second suit and forget the first. The math assumes a discipline most people do not have.

If you wear a suit three or more times a week, the math flips much faster, and the made-to-measure case is strong. The suit will pay for itself in wear savings alone within twelve to eighteen months, and it will still be a suit you want to be seen in at the end of that period.

If you wear a suit fewer than twelve times a year, buy an off-the-rack suit and have a good tailor alter it. Spend the difference on something else. We will tell you this in the consultation if it is the truth for you, and we would rather lose the sale than sell you something you do not need.
