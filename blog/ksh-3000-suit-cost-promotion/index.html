The KSh 3,000 Suit Problem

By David Ochieng · Master Tailor, FitFix Nairobi · 7 min read

Every week, someone walks into my workshop in Industrial Area holding a suit they paid between KSh 2,500 and KSh 8,000 for. They are usually polite. They are usually a little embarrassed. And they usually say the same thing: "I know it's not great. Can you fix it?"

Sometimes we can. Sometimes the fabric and the construction are too far gone. But the question they are really asking is not "can you fix it?" It is "how did I end up here, and what should I have done differently?"

This post is the answer to that question. It is not a story about one man. It is a pattern I have seen across fourteen years and thousands of suits. If you are about to buy a suit — any suit, at any price — read this first.

The Two Types of Cheap Suit

Not all cheap suits are the same, and I want to be fair to the category before I criticise it. A cheap suit falls into one of two buckets.

The first bucket is a good suit that fits badly. This is a KSh 6,000 to KSh 12,000 off-the-rack suit from a decent retailer, cut from a reasonable wool-blend fabric, but made to a block pattern that does not match your body. The fabric is fine. The construction is fused but acceptable. The shoulders slope unevenly. The jacket is too short. The trousers are too loose. The suit is not bad — it just does not fit you. A good tailor can usually fix this for a few thousand shillings. If you are on a budget, this is the path.

The second bucket is a bad suit that fits badly. This is the KSh 2,500 to KSh 5,000 "custom" suit from a backroom tailor. The fabric is polyester. The interlining is glued. The stitching is uneven. The buttons are plastic. The lining is cheap acetate. This suit will look worse with every wear. It will bubble. It will shine. It cannot be fixed, because the problem is not the fit — it is the entire garment. This is the bucket that gives cheap suits a bad name.

When I talk about "the KSh 3,000 suit problem," I am talking about the second bucket. And I want to be specific about why it is a problem, because most of the arguments you read online about cheap suits are either vague or dishonest.

Why the Second Bucket Is a Problem

Here is what actually happens when you buy a KSh 3,000 "custom" suit from the second bucket.

The fabric is polyester. Real wool breathes, moves with the body, and drapes with weight. Polyester traps heat, glues to the skin in Nairobi humidity, and reflects light in a way that reads as plastic under office lighting. If you have ever walked into a boardroom at 10am and felt the room cool on you specifically, it is because someone noticed the shine.

The construction is fused. The chest and lapels of a real suit have a floating horsehair canvas, hand-stitched into the garment. This is what gives a jacket its shape over time. A cheap suit uses glue — a heat-pressed synthetic interlining — that bubbles and separates within months of regular wear. Once it bubbles, no tailor can fix it. The garment is finished.

The fit is a block pattern. A cheap tailor takes three or four measurements and matches you to a standard size. A real tailor takes twenty measurements and drafts a pattern from scratch. The difference is not visible in the first photo. It is visible in every photo after.

The garment is not built to last. A KSh 3,000 suit will typically survive four to twelve wears before the wear becomes visible enough that you stop wearing it. A well-made made-to-measure suit will typically survive five to ten years of regular wear. The math of "cheap" is only cheaper if you wear the suit fewer than a dozen times, which for most professionals is about one month.

Where I am being honest about the limits of this argument: if you wear a suit fewer than twelve times a year — say for weddings and funerals — a KSh 3,000 suit is genuinely the rational financial choice. You will get four to twelve wears out of it before it needs replacing, and that is enough for the events you actually attend. The made-to-measure argument in this post is not that cheap suits are always wrong. It is that cheap suits are wrong for people who wear suits often, and those are the people most likely to be reading a blog about suits.

The Cost-Per-Wear Math — Honestly

I want to give you the real math, because a lot of the cost-per-wear arguments online are dishonest.

A KSh 3,000 suit, worn four times before the shine and the bubble make it unwearable: KSh 750 per wear.

A KSh 55,000 made-to-measure suit, worn forty times over the first year: KSh 1,375 per wear.

On those numbers, the cheap suit is cheaper per wear. I would rather you see that than have it hidden behind a clever calculation.

The math flips over time. If you wear the made-to-measure suit three times a week for five years — normal for someone in a suit five days a week — that is roughly 780 wears over five years. Cost per wear: KSh 70. The cheap suit is long gone, replaced five or six times over the same period at a total cost of around KSh 18,000 for roughly 24 wears — KSh 750 per wear.

So the made-to-measure suit becomes cheaper — but only if you wear it enough times. For a professional in a suit five days a week, the math flips within the first year. For a professional in a suit twice a month, the math never flips, and the cheap suit genuinely is the rational choice.

I want you to read that again, because it matters: the right decision depends on how often you wear the suit, not on what anyone tells you the suit should cost. If anyone — including me — tells you a made-to-measure suit is always the right choice, they are selling, not advising.

What a Made-to-Measure Suit Does Differently

1. The pattern is drafted for your body

Twenty measurements, not four. Shoulder slope, chest, waist, seat, sleeve length, jacket length, posture. The pattern is drawn from scratch, not matched to a block. This is what separates a garment that fits from a garment that merely fits you better than the last one.

2. The fabric is real wool

Super 120s Italian wool — the standard for Nairobi executives — breathes in the heat, holds its shape through a full working day, and photographs matte under office lights. It is the single biggest determinant of whether a suit reads as "professional" or "cheap" from across a room.

3. The construction is stitched, not glued

A floating horsehair canvas is hand-stitched into the chest and lapels. This is the invisible structure that gives a jacket its shape and allows it to mold to your body over time. A fused suit loses this in the first year. A canvas suit gets better with age.

4. The fittings are real

Two fittings at minimum — a basted fitting to check the balance and drape on your actual body, and a forward fitting to finalize collar, sleeves and pockets. On a suit you will wear every week for years, this is the difference between a garment that looks sharp in year three and one that has to be constantly adjusted.

5. The guarantee is real

Every FitFix suit is covered by a full-year workmanship guarantee on seams, zippers and fabric defects. Details are on the warranty page. A cheap suit offers nothing.

What to Look for When You Buy Any Suit

Whether you choose made-to-measure or off-the-rack, the same six tests will tell you whether a suit is worth your money.

Pinch the chest. A canvased suit has a third layer between the outer fabric and the lining. A fused suit feels flat and papery. If you can feel the floating canvas, the suit is at least partly canvased.

Check the seam allowance. Open the lining at the side seam and feel for extra fabric inside. A good suit has two to three centimetres of seam allowance so it can be let out. A cheap suit has almost none, because the maker was saving fabric.

Look at the buttonholes. Real buttonholes are hand-stitched. Cheap buttonholes are machine-stitched and the stitching pulls away after a few months. Look closely — the difference is obvious.

Check the fabric composition. It should be listed clearly — "100% Wool" or "95% Wool, 5% Cashmere." Anything labelled "Wool Blend" without the actual percentage is usually 20 to 30 percent wool and the rest polyester.

Look at the shoulders. The shoulder line should be smooth and even. If the shoulders slope, or if there is any puckering where the sleeve meets the shoulder, the pattern was cut for a body that is not yours.

Ask who made it. A real tailor will tell you exactly who cut and sewed the suit. A cheap tailor will be vague. The name of the person on the bench is the single best indicator of the garment's quality.

The Bottom Line

If you wear a suit often, a well-made suit is not a luxury. It is the cheapest suit you will ever own, because it is the suit you will wear the most. The math only looks like it favours cheap suits if you wear them rarely.

If you wear a suit rarely, buy an off-the-rack suit and spend KSh 2,000 to KSh 4,000 having a good tailor alter it properly. Do not buy a KSh 3,000 "custom" suit. It is the worst of both worlds — too expensive to be disposable, too cheap to be permanent.

And if you are somewhere in the middle — an executive who wears a suit two or three times a week, a professional who needs to look sharp in front of clients, a graduate stepping into a role where appearance is part of the job — the made-to-measure suit is the right answer, but only if you wear it enough to justify the investment.

That is the honest version of the argument. I hope it helped.
