Why a school uniform is the hardest-working garment in any wardrobe

A school uniform takes more punishment than any adult garment you own. It's worn five days a week, ten hours a day, by a body that is actively growing. It gets sat on, kneeled on, climbed in, eaten in, and washed more often than anything else in the house. And it's the one garment where a badly placed hem is visible from across the classroom. That combination of wear and visibility is why uniform alterations are worth doing properly — and why we approach them differently to adult garments.

The child does not stop growing because the hem was cut

This is the single most important thing about a school uniform alteration. An adult trouser hem is cut to a fixed length and stays there. A child's uniform hem will be one to two centimetres short by the end of the term, because the child has grown — and if you cut a uniform flush to the shoe in January, it will be a capri by October. That's why every school uniform hem we cut carries 3 to 5 centimetres of extra fabric folded inside it — a growth allowance that can be let down twice before the uniform is genuinely outgrown. It costs us five extra minutes on the press. It saves the parent a new uniform halfway through the year. Most Nairobi tailors skip this step. We don't.

The uniform is worn on a body that's still changing shape

Adult bodies settle. A well-fitted suit jacket will still be a well-fitted suit jacket in five years. A child's body does not settle — it changes shape every six months. A uniform that fits perfectly in January is a size too small by the following September. A uniform that fits properly at the start of secondary school needs re-altering by the middle of form one. The right approach is to build the alteration around growth, not around the current size. This means slightly looser seams, hems with allowance, and belts or drawstrings rather than fixed waistbands where possible. Every school uniform alteration we do is designed to give the child room to move inside the garment, not just to fit the child today.

Uniform fabric is built for durability, not drape

School trousers are usually poly-cotton drill — a firm twill weave that resists creasing and survives hard wear, but frays aggressively at a cut edge. Skirts are polyester gabardine, which is smooth and drapey but goes shiny under a hot iron and frays along cut lines. Blazers are wool or poly-wool blends with a canvas structure underneath. Each fabric behaves differently under a needle, and each one has its own failure pattern. Poly-cotton drill frays at the hem; gabardine shines on the press; wool blazers can bubble at the canvas if pressed too aggressively. Knowing which fabric you're working with decides everything about how the alteration is done.

The knees, the seat and the crotch fail first — always

Ask any parent which part of a uniform gives out first. It's the knees. Then the seat. Then the crotch. These are the three points where a growing child puts maximum stress on thin fabric, and they're the three points where cheap uniforms have no reinforcement at all. When we alter a school uniform, we reinforce those three points while the garment is open on the bench. It adds ten minutes to the job. It adds a year of life to the uniform. We do it on every uniform that comes through the workshop, at no extra charge — because we've never seen a school uniform fail anywhere else first.

The crest and the name tag must never move

A school crest is the identity of the uniform. It's often embroidered straight through the fabric of the blazer or jumper, which means it can't be detached and reattached — the alteration has to work around it. A name tag has to be readable for the life of the garment. These are the two things on a uniform that must survive every alteration untouched. We mark the crest position before any cut, work around embroidered crests on the chest panel, and stitch name tags at the inside back collar rather than the hem where they get washed to death. A uniform that comes back with a crooked crest or a faded tag is a uniform that gets sent back to us.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 600 trouser hem with a growth allowance saves a parent buying a new uniform halfway through the year. A KSh 400 elastic replacement on school shorts saves buying new shorts when the elastic goes slack. A KSh 2,000 blazer re-lining on a school blazer extends the blazer's life by two years — compared to KSh 6,000+ for a new one from the school's official supplier. Three school uniform tiers — three completely different alterations

A KSh 1,200 poly-cotton uniform from a Nairobi wholesaler and a KSh 9,000 wool-blend blazer from a private school's official supplier require completely different tailoring approaches. If a tailor treats both the same way, one of them will be damaged. Here's how we categorise school uniforms before deciding what technique to use.

Entry tier

Standard poly-cotton uniforms

The everyday public and low-fee private school uniform — poly-cotton drill trousers, gabardine skirts, cotton-blend shirts and shorts. Fabric weight 160–220 gsm. Usually bought as a set from a wholesaler in town. Colour fades over the first year. Waistbands almost always elasticated on younger children's sizes. Thin seam allowances and single-stitched stress points.

What we do differentlyThese fabrics are thin, so every seam we cut has to be finished properly or it frays within two washes. We overlock every raw edge and reinforce the crotch and seat before returning the uniform — the two places these uniforms fail first. Growth allowance on every hem, standard.

Mid tier

Branded and poly-wool blend uniforms

Mid-fee private schools, academies and international schools with a uniform supplier. Fabric weight 220–280 gsm. Poly-wool blend trousers and skirts, better shirts with proper collars, jumpers with school colours knitted in. Garments are often embroidered with the school crest rather than a sewn badge. Cleaner construction, better pocket bags, visible size labels inside the waistband.

What we do differentlyThe crest is embroidered straight through the fabric, so you cannot detach it and reattach it — you have to work around it. On blazers and jumpers in this tier, we reshape from the side and underarm seams only, never through the chest panel. Done wrong, the embroidery puckers permanently.

Premium tier

Wool-blend blazers and tailored uniform wear

High-fee private schools, boarding schools and formal school wear. Fabric weight 280–380 gsm. Proper wool-blend blazer cloth with a canvas front, lined sleeves, functional cuff buttons and a woven crest or metal badge. Tailored tunics and pinafores with shaped darts. Trousers with a real waistband, curtain waistband and a hook-and-bar closure rather than elastic.

What we do differentlyOn this tier the alteration has to be invisible and the crest has to survive untouched. We hand-finish the sleeve ends, preserve functional buttonholes, and never cut through the canvas front of a blazer. A careless alteration on a KSh 9,000 blazer is a KSh 9,000 mistake — and the school will notice.

If you're not sure which tier your child's uniform falls into, send a photo of the label and the inside of the waistband on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five parts of a uniform that affect the alteration

What a school uniform actually is — piece by piece

School uniforms get described as "the same as adult clothes but smaller," which tells you nothing useful. Structurally, a uniform has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a uniform.

01

The fabric weave — poly-cotton drill, gabardine or wool blend

Most school trousers and shorts are cut from poly-cotton drill, a firm twill weave that resists creasing and survives hard wear. Skirts are usually polyester gabardine — smoother, drapier, and prone to fraying at a cut edge. Blazers use a wool or poly-wool blend, sometimes with a canvas front for structure. Each weave needs a different needle, a different stitch length and a different pressing technique. Press gabardine too hard and it goes shiny permanently; press a wool-blend blazer with too much steam and the canvas front bubbles.

02

The waistband — elastic, fixed or half-elastic

This is the single most important thing to understand about kids' uniforms. Younger children's trousers and skirts use a fully elasticated waistband with a buttonhole for a belt on formal days. Older children's uniforms switch to a fixed waistband with a hook-and-bar. Some uniforms use half-elastic — elasticated at the back, fixed at the front. Each one is adjusted a different way. Replacing elastic is a completely different job from taking in a fixed waistband, and only one of them is what your child actually needs.

03

The knee, the seat and the crotch — where uniforms fail

Ask any parent which part of a uniform gives out first. It's the knees. Then the seat. Then the crotch on boys' trousers. These are the points where a growing child puts maximum stress on thin fabric, and they're the points where cheap uniforms have no reinforcement at all. When we alter a school uniform, we add reinforcement stitching at the crotch, seat and knee while the garment is open on the table. It costs us ten minutes and saves the uniform another year.

04

The hem and the growth allowance

This is what separates a school uniform alteration from any other hemming job. A child does not stop growing because the hem was cut. If you hem a school trouser flush to the shoe today, it will be a capri by the second term. Every school uniform hem we cut includes a growth allowance — 3 to 5 centimetres of extra fabric folded inside the hem so it can be let down twice before the garment is outgrown. It is the reason our school uniform hemming costs the same as a normal hem but lasts three times longer.

05

The crest, the badge and the name tag — what must never move

A school crest is the identity of the uniform, and some are embroidered straight through the fabric while others are woven patches stitched on. A name tag has to be readable for the life of the garment. These are the two things that must survive every alteration untouched. We mark the crest position before any cut, work around embroidered crests on the chest panel, and stitch name tags at the inside back collar rather than the hem where they get washed to death. A uniform that comes back with a crooked crest is a uniform that gets sent back to us.

If a tailor quotes a school uniform alteration without asking about the child's age, the fabric, or the growth allowance, they're guessing — and the uniform will show it by mid-term.

Fabric decides the technique

How the school uniform fabric changes the job

Two uniforms can look identical hanging on the same rail and behave completely differently under a needle. Here's my honest breakdown by fabric type — the same one I'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Poly-cotton drill

The workhorse of Kenyan school trousers and shorts. It resists creasing, survives repeated washing and holds its colour longer than pure cotton. The trade-off is that it frays aggressively at a cut edge and it can scorch under a hot iron. Every raw edge we cut on poly-cotton drill gets overlocked before it's folded into a hem — skip that step and the hem develops a fringe of loose threads within a month. We also press through a cloth at medium heat, never directly.

Polyester gabardine

The standard skirt fabric — smooth, drapey and quick-drying. Gabardine has a fine twill face that goes shiny the moment it meets a hot iron, and once that shine appears it never comes out. That's why skirt hems and pleats have to be pressed through a protective cloth on low heat, usually from the inside. Gabardine also holds a pleat beautifully if it's pressed properly, which is the reason a re-pleated skirt looks better than a new one when it's done right.

Wool and poly-wool blend blazer cloth

Blazer cloth is the most technical fabric in a school uniform. It has a face side and a nap direction, a canvas front that gives the jacket its shape, and a lining that has to be re-set whenever the jacket is altered. Sleeve shortening on a blazer with functional cuff buttons means moving the buttons and reworking the buttonholes by hand — that's why blazer sleeves cost more than trouser hems. On the plus side, wool-blend responds to steam beautifully, so a well-pressed blazer alteration is almost invisible.

Cotton tunic and pinafore cloth

Girls' tunics and pinafores are usually cut from a cotton or cotton-poly poplin with shaped darts at the waist and bust. The darts are what give the garment its shape, so any resizing has to reshape the darts rather than simply take in the side seam. Take in the sides only on a fitted tunic and it will pull across the chest. We re-draft the darts, then reshape the side seams to match. Slower, but the tunic sits properly.

PE kit jersey and interlock knit

PE shorts and shirts are knitted, not woven — which changes everything. A knit stretches in every direction, so a hem stitched on a normal machine will pop the moment the child runs. We use a stretch stitch or a cover-stitch on all PE kit hems, and we use ballpoint needles so the needle pushes between the knit loops rather than cutting them. Cutting a knit thread with a sharp needle is how a PE shirt develops a run.

Knitted jumpers, cardigans and school socks

School jumpers are usually a fine-gauge acrylic or acrylic-wool knit with a ribbed hem and cuffs. Sleeve shortening on a jumper means re-forming the ribbed cuff so it still grips — you can't just cut and hem a rib, or it goes slack. We remove the cuff, shorten the sleeve from the top, and reattach the original cuff. On cardigans we also check the button placket for stretch before returning it, because that's the second thing to give out.

If you don't know what your child's uniform is made of, send a close-up photo of the fabric on WhatsApp. We'll identify the weave and tell you straight what's possible.

A short note from the bench

The one thing every school uniform hem must have

If you take one thing from this page, let it be this. Every school uniform hem I cut has extra fabric folded inside it — three to five centimetres of it, hidden inside the hem, invisible when the child is wearing the uniform. It's called a growth allowance, and it's what separates a school uniform alteration that lasts one term from one that lasts the whole school year. Almost no Nairobi tailor does this. I do it on every uniform that comes through the workshop, at no extra charge, because I've seen what happens when it isn't done.

Here's what a hem without a growth allowance looks like. Parent brings uniform in January. Child is, say, one metre twenty. Tailor hems the trouser flush to the shoe — perfect fit on collection day. Parent collects it, child wears it to school, everyone is happy. Come May, the trouser is one centimetre too short — because the child has grown. Come September, it's a capri. Parent comes back to the tailor in September asking what can be done. The answer is usually "nothing — there's no fabric left in the hem." The uniform is dead, and it's dead not because it wore out, but because the hem was cut too tight in the first place. A new pair costs KSh 2,500. The growth allowance would have cost the parent nothing.

With a growth allowance, the same uniform works for the whole year. Come May, we let the hem down one centimetre. Come September, we let it down another. By the following January, the growth allowance is used up — but the uniform has lasted twelve months instead of six. That's the whole point. A child's body is not fixed. A child's uniform hem shouldn't be either. Every parent who's had a uniform die mid-year knows the frustration of it. Every parent who hasn't yet will, eventually.

There's a second thing about the growth allowance that matters — it changes how I cut the uniform in the first place. A hem with a growth allowance sits a fraction of a centimetre longer than a flush hem, because the extra fabric has to be folded inside cleanly. On a plain trouser, that difference is invisible. On a pleated skirt, it means the pleats have to be re-set with a slightly different spacing at the hem to account for the extra fold. On a blazer, the lining hem swing has to be adjusted — the lining stops 2 to 4 cm above the outer hem, and that swing has to account for the growth allowance at the bottom. This is technical, and it takes longer than a plain hem. It's also why we charge the same as a normal hem for it — because we'd rather every parent in Nairobi had a uniform that lasted the year than squeeze an extra hundred shillings out of them for a proper job.

The last thing I'll say about school uniforms — and I've been at this fourteen years — is that parents feel the cost of uniforms in a way they don't feel the cost of adult clothing. A KSh 2,500 trouser for an adult is a choice. A KSh 2,500 trouser for a child in standard four is a necessity, and it's a necessity that repeats every year. The alterations that help parents the most are the ones that stretch a uniform one more term. Growth allowances stretch it. Reinforcement at the knee and seat stretches it. Waistbands that can be let out stretch it. Blazers with re-lining instead of replacement stretch it. That's why we keep our school uniform prices lower than our adult prices, and why bulk rates start from as few as three uniforms — because parents shouldn't be charged adult-tailoring prices to keep their children in uniforms that fit.

Make the uniform last the school year

Eight habits that keep school uniforms out of the workshop

A school uniform is the hardest-working garment in any household. Five days a week, ten hours a day, on a child who runs, kneels, climbs and sits on the ground. Most of the damage we see in the workshop isn't from the alteration — it's from how the uniform is washed, dried, rotated and stored between wears. Here are the eight habits we teach every parent when they collect an altered uniform.

1. Two sets per child, minimum

One uniform worn five days a week is worn out within a year. Two sets rotated gives each one a full day to recover its shape, halves the washing cycles on each, and means a mid-week accident doesn't leave you scrambling on a Sunday night. If budget allows three, even better. This single habit extends the life of a uniform more than any alteration can — and it's the reason a growth allowance is worth having even on the second set.

2. Wash cold, inside out, separate darks

Poly-cotton drill and gabardine hold their colour far better in cold water. Turning uniforms inside out protects the visible surface from friction in the drum. Wash whites and darks separately — poly-cotton picks up colour transfer more aggressively than pure cotton, and once grey, it stays grey. Skip the fabric softener; it leaves a residue that dulls uniform colours faster.

3. Never tumble dry a uniform

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shrink a poly-cotton uniform by half a size and to weaken the elastic in a kids' waistband. Both of those problems cost you a trip to the tailor. Hang uniforms on a line or dry them flat on a rack. If you're washing on a Sunday night and need them by Monday, point a fan at them. It takes longer but it saves the uniform — and the waistband elastic.

4. Iron pleats while damp, from the inside

Pleated skirts hold their shape beautifully if pressed while slightly damp, and they lose it permanently if pressed dry with too much heat. Press from the inside, or through a thin cloth on the outside. Gabardine goes shiny the moment a hot iron touches it directly, and that shine never comes out. Steam is your friend on uniforms — direct heat is not.

5. Check the hem at mid-term, not at year end

A uniform hem that's an inch above the shoe in June will be a capri by October. Check the hem every mid-term — if there's a clear gap between the hem and the shoe, bring it in. We'll let it down from the growth allowance for KSh 600, and the uniform lasts the rest of the year. Wait until December and you'll be buying a new pair.

6. Put name tags at the collar, not the hem

Name tags stitched at the hem get washed, dragged and rubbed against shoes — they wash out within a term. Tags stitched at the inside back collar survive the life of the garment and stay readable. If you're stitching tags yourself, that's the position to use. If you'd rather we did it, it's KSh 100 per tag and we'll do it during any other alteration at no extra pickup cost.

7. Repair the first hole before it becomes a knee

A worn knee is a KSh 500 reinforcement job today. Left for another term, it becomes a KSh 800 darning job, then a KSh 1,200 patched knee — or a new pair of trousers entirely. Same for a small tear at the crotch seam: KSh 600 today, a full rebuild if you wait. Message us the moment you first notice something small. Most small repairs are same-day and come with free pickup.

8. Rotate PE kit separately from formal uniform

PE kit gets sweat, mud and grass stains that formal uniform never sees. Wash it separately, and don't put PE shorts in with school trousers — the knit fibres shed onto the woven fabric and the trousers come out looking fuzzy. Rotating PE kit separately also means the formal uniform stays crisp for the days that matter: assembly, exams, visiting days and school photos.

Follow those eight habits and your child's uniform will hold its fit for the full school year. When it eventually does need an alteration, we're one WhatsApp message away. See our elastic replacement page and darning & invisible mending page for the two most common uniform rescues.
