First, what do you actually own?

Most people don't know the difference between a bomber and a varsity. And it changes the alteration entirely.

Before I quote a jacket job, I need to know what kind of jacket it is. Not because I'm being difficult — because the construction is different. A bomber has ribbed cuffs that need different handling from a utility jacket with drawstrings. A denim jacket has flat-felled seams that must be preserved. A varsity jacket has leather sleeves that most Nairobi tailors won't touch. Here's the identification test.

01

Bomber jacket

Look for: ribbed cuffs and hem

Short, boxy, hits at the hip. Usually nylon, polyester or a poly-cotton blend with a padded lining. The giveaway is the elasticised ribbed knit at the wrists and waistband, and a zip front running from bottom to a stand-up collar. The most common Nairobi bomber is a plain black nylon zip-up.

What we usually do: Shorten sleeves at the cuff (keeping the ribbing), take in the body slightly, replace the front zip with a heavy-duty YKK. The ribbed hem is the tricky part — if the elastic has stretched, we replace it with matching ribbing.
02

Denim jacket (trucker style)

Look for: flat-felled seams, button front

Structured, boxy, sits at the hip or slightly above. Blue or black, sometimes washed, sometimes raw. Button front (not zip), two chest pockets with flaps, and two vertical side pockets that begin at the bottom hem. Every seam on a denim jacket is flat-felled — a two-needle folded seam that's the hardest type to reproduce if you shorten carelessly.

What we usually do: Slim the body between the chest and the hem, shorten sleeves with a flat-felled finish, replace buttons with metal shank buttons. Never machine-hem a denim jacket — the seam has to look factory.
03

Utility / field jacket

Look for: four patch pockets, drawstring waist

Longer than a bomber — usually hits at the top of the hip or just below. Four patch pockets (two chest, two hip), often with button flaps. Waist and hem usually have internal drawstrings or elasticated cords. Fabric is heavier — canvas, ripstop or a heavy cotton twill. Think military-inspired M-65 or safari jacket cut.

What we usually do: Take in the waist using the existing side seams, shorten the body if it sits too low, replace the drawstring if it's frayed or lost. Harder alteration than a bomber because the pockets have to stay level after slimming.
04

Varsity jacket (letterman)

Look for: leather or wool sleeves, contrasting body

The tell is the contrast: two-tone with leather or wool sleeves and a different fabric body (wool, fleece or knit). Ribbed cuffs, hem and collar. Snap-button front, not zip. Often has an embroidered letter or emblem on the chest. Common at Kenyan schools, colleges and university sports teams.

What we usually do: Shorten leather sleeves from the shoulder (not the cuff — you can't re-stitch leather where old holes exist), take in the wool body, replace worn snaps. Requires specialist leather handling. We do it, but it's a slower job than the others.
05

Windbreaker / lightweight shell

Look for: paper-thin nylon, packable

Almost weightless — usually 40 to 80 gsm nylon or polyester. Zips all the way up to the chin, often has a small packable pouch. The fabric is so light that it frays if you cut it wrong. Common in Kenya for hiking, safari wear and light rain.

What we usually do: Shorten sleeves and hem with a narrow rolled hem — heavy seams would show through the thin fabric. Light alterations only; a windbreaker is not the garment to try a full restyle on.
06

Puffer / quilted jacket

Look for: horizontal quilt lines, puffy panels

Horizontal stitching across the body and sleeves forming baffles — the panels filled with down or synthetic insulation. Usually a zip front, sometimes a hood. Nylon or polyester shell with a membrane backing. Common for Nairobi cold mornings and travel.

What we usually do: Take in the side seams so the baffles stay level, shorten sleeves with a hidden finish, repair damaged baffle seams. We never cut through a baffle — the insulation escapes and the panel collapses. Anything involving baffles has to be handled differently.

If you're not sure which of those describes your jacket, send a photo on WhatsApp. I'll identify it from the construction and give you a firm quote.

What a jacket actually is, layer by layer

Five layers, and why each one matters to your alteration

A jacket is not a shirt with a longer body. It has five layers, and if your tailor doesn't understand all five, the alteration will look wrong within six months. Here's what I look at before I touch a jacket with scissors.

01

The shell (outer fabric)

Nylon, polyester, denim, canvas, wool, leather — every jacket starts with a shell. What matters to the alteration is the weave, weight, and stretch. A ripstop shell is grid-locked and can't be pressed flat. A wool shell can be shaped with steam. A denim shell takes a flat-felled seam perfectly. A leather shell leaves permanent needle marks, which means every stitch has to be right the first time.

Affects: seam finish, pressing, needle choice

02

The lining (inner fabric)

Most jackets have a separate lining — satin, taffeta, cupro, polyester mesh or quilted. The lining isn't just cosmetic. It's what stops the shell from stretching out of shape, and it hides the raw seams of the alteration. When we take in a waist, we remove the lining at the bottom hem, alter the shell, then re-stitch the lining so it fits the new shape. Skipping this step is the number one reason alterations look homemade.

Affects: any alteration that changes the outer silhouette

03

The wadding or batting (insulation)

On insulated jackets — puffers, quilted jackets, some bombers — there's a middle layer of down, synthetic fill or wool batting. Wadding cannot be cut without destroying the panel. It has to be removed, altered around, and re-stitched back in place through baffles or channels. This is why puffer jackets are more expensive to alter than plain shells — the labour is double.

Affects: puffers, quilted jackets, insulated bombers

04

The interfacing (structural canvas)

Not every casual jacket has interfacing — but the better ones do. It's a hidden layer of stiffening material (canvas, horsehair, or fusible webbing) that gives structure to collars, front plackets, and sometimes shoulders. On a varsity jacket, interfacing in the collar is what keeps it standing up. Alterations have to account for this — we can't just sew through interfacing without pulling the collar out of shape.

Affects: collar, front placket, shoulder shape

05

The hardware (zips, buttons, snaps, drawstrings)

The visible working parts. Zips take the most punishment on casual jackets because the fabric is usually heavier than on a shirt or blouse. A standard lightweight zip forced into a denim or canvas jacket will break within a couple of months — the teeth bend under the weight of the fabric. We replace with heavy-duty YKK only. Same for snaps on varsity jackets (metal only, never plastic) and buttons on denim (shank-style, never flat).

Affects: durability, look, and how the jacket feels to use

If your tailor isn't asking which of those layers your jacket has, they're guessing. We don't guess on a jacket — the job is too expensive to get wrong.

How the fabric changes the alteration

The same alteration is three different jobs on three different jackets

This is why jacket alterations can't be quoted per garment type alone. A sleeve shortening on a nylon bomber takes forty minutes. On a leather varsity jacket it takes three hours. On a puffer it takes two hours, but the baffle work adds another hour. Here's the honest breakdown.

Nylon and polyester shells

The easiest fabrics to alter, with one major caveat: heat. Nylon and polyesters melt before they press. Every seam has to be pressed through a protective cloth at low temperature, or the fabric turns shiny and hardens. We work on nylon shells daily — they're the bulk of Nairobi's casual jacket market. Sleeve shortening on a nylon bomber takes roughly forty minutes. Body slimming takes an hour and a half. Front zip replacement is thirty minutes.

Denim and heavy cotton twill

Denim has two things going for it — it's strong, and it takes a beautiful flat-felled seam. The problem is the original seams on a denim jacket are usually triple-stitched with a heavier thread than a domestic machine can handle. We use a proper walking-foot industrial machine for denim work. Sleeve shortening on a denim jacket requires removing the original hem, cutting to new length, and re-stitching with a twin needle to match the factory look. It's slower but the finish is right.

Canvas and ripstop utility shells

Canvas is the hardest shell to hem cleanly because the needle struggles through the fabric layers. On a heavy canvas utility jacket, we do most of the hem work by hand with a saddler's needle and waxed thread. Ripstop is different — it's lighter, but the grid structure means you can't press it flat. We use a lighter needle and a slightly looser stitch tension. This is the fabric range where experience shows the most.

Wool and wool-blend varsity bodies

Wool varsity jackets take a press beautifully, but they also shrink if you use steam too aggressively, and the fibres matte together if you over-handle. We work on wool bodies with a muslin press cloth and moderate heat. Slimming a wool varsity body is one of the more satisfying jobs — the jacket responds to shaping and holds its new silhouette permanently. Same for wool-blend utility jackets — the fabric forgives more than people expect.

Leather sleeves on varsity jackets

Leather is the hardest shell to alter on any jacket. Every needle leaves a permanent hole — you can't stitch twice in the same place. Varsity jacket sleeves are almost always leather, which means standard shortening-from-the-cuff is off the table. Instead we shorten from the shoulder: remove the sleeve at the shoulder seam, shorten at the top, re-stitch through the original stitch holes so no new damage appears. A leather sleeve shortening takes three hours per sleeve. Some Nairobi tailors refuse the job entirely. We do it weekly.

Quilted and insulated shells

Quilted jackets and puffers add the wadding layer — which means the alteration has to work around the baffles. If you take in the sides, the baffles must be re-stitched level with the original rows, or the jacket will look collapsed on one side. Baffle alignment is the whole job. It's slow, it's precise, and there's no shortcut. If your puffer jacket's baffles are already misaligned from a prior alteration, message me first — sometimes it can be fixed, sometimes the jacket has to be lived with.

Delicate windbreaker and packable shells

The lightest fabrics need the lightest handling. Nylon windbreakers in the 40 to 60 gsm range fray at the cut line within seconds if you don't have the right tool. We use a hot-knife cutter that seals the cut edge as it slices, then a rolled hem finish. A windbreaker sleeve shortening is a fifteen-minute job, but it has to be done with the correct equipment. Most general tailors in Nairobi don't have a hot-knife — the windbreaker ends up fraying at the cuff within weeks.

If you don't know the fabric of your jacket, send a close-up photo on WhatsApp. I'll identify it from the weave and confirm what's possible before you commit.

Before you trust anyone with your jacket

Five questions to ask any jacket tailor in Nairobi — including me

If you're going to hand over a jacket that matters to you, you deserve to know who's working on it and how they think. These are the five questions I'd ask if I were the customer. I've answered each one honestly.

1. "Who actually does the work — you, or someone I've never met?"

Every jacket that comes through FitFixClothesAlterations.co.ke is handled by me personally. Not delegated to an apprentice, not handed off to a machine operator. If a jacket needs a shoulder reshape or a leather sleeve shortening, I do it. If it's a simple hem, I might hand it to my team — but I still check the finish before it goes back to you. I'm not a shop that farms work out to subcontractors. Everything happens in Industrial Area, under my eye.

2. "What happens if the fit is wrong when you hand it back?"

I collect the jacket again and fix it — free. No arguments. No "you must have measured it wrong." Our 100% fit guarantee applies to every jacket, whether it's a KSh 500 button replacement or a KSh 12,000 full restyle. In four years of running this workshop, I've never charged a client for a re-do. If a jacket comes back twice because I got it wrong, that's my cost to absorb — not yours.

3. "Will you tell me honestly if a jacket isn't worth altering?"

Yes. Sometimes the right answer is don't. If a jacket's shoulders are too narrow to expand, if the fabric is fatigued at the seams, if the leather is cracked in a way that any stitch will make worse — I'll say so. I've turned down jobs where the alteration would have cost more than the jacket is worth, and I've told clients to save their money for something better. That costs me a job in the short term. It earns trust in the long run, and trust is the only reason I have clients who bring me a jacket every year.

4. "What specifically are you going to do differently on my jacket?"

Every quote I send includes the specific work I'm going to do — not just "sleeve shortening" but "sleeve shortening at the cuff with a rolled hem finish to preserve your zip cuffs" or "waist take-in from the side seams only, with the existing belt loops preserved and re-spaced." If the tailor's quote is just a price with no description of method, that's a red flag. You should know exactly what's going to happen to your jacket before you hand it over.

5. "How long have you actually been doing this — and what did you do before?"

Fourteen years. I trained at Manix Clothing Store — Italian Men's Wear, then worked as Senior Tailor at Italian Men's Wear, then spent two years as Head Tailor at City Walk Ltd before founding this workshop. Those are real places, with real names, that you can verify. I'm not a "tailor" who learned from YouTube last year. This is the only trade I've ever had, and I've spent almost half my life at it. That's the answer to question five.

If you're interviewing another tailor and they can't answer all five of those questions clearly, keep looking. There are good tailors in Nairobi. You just have to ask the right things.

A jacket's five-year plan

How to make one jacket last five years in Nairobi

Jackets don't wear out from being worn. They wear out from being worn wrong. Over fourteen years I've watched thousands of jackets come and go, and the ones that last five or ten years always share the same habits. Here's the plan I'd follow with my own.

Y1

Year one — the fitting

If the jacket doesn't fit properly in the first month, get it altered immediately. Don't wait for "a special occasion." A jacket that fits wrong gets worn less, sits in the wardrobe longer, and the fabric takes on storage creases that are hard to press out. Alter it in year one, wear it well, and the jacket gives you four more years of service before it needs anything else.

Action: one session with a tailor in the first month. Nothing after that unless something breaks.

Y2

Year two — the maintenance

By the second year, small things start to go. A button loosens. A pocket corner frays. A cuff stitch pulls. These are all fifteen-minute fixes if you catch them early, and KSh 1,500+ repairs if you ignore them for six months. The rule I give every client: if you notice something small in the mirror, message me that week. Small fixes are cheap. Big fixes are not.

Action: keep an eye out every couple of months. Fix small issues the week you spot them.

Y3

Year three — the lining

The lining is the first thing to wear out on a well-used jacket. It's also the most hidden, which is why people ignore it until it tears through. By year three, the lining on a daily-wear jacket usually needs repair — a few torn seams, a worn patch under the arms, a sagging hem. A partial lining repair at this stage costs around KSh 1,500. If you wait until year five, the whole lining needs replacing at KSh 3,500+.

Action: check the lining in year three. Repair, don't replace.

Y4

Year four — the hardware

Zips, snaps and buttons take a beating on casual jackets. The front zip on a daily-worn jacket will usually need replacing by year four — not because it's broken, but because it's stretched and stops sliding smoothly. Same for snaps on a varsity jacket. Replace while it's a KSh 1,200 job, not after it fails at the worst moment and you're looking at a KSh 2,000+ emergency repair with a rush fee on top.

Action: replace the front zip proactively. Keep a spare button taped inside the jacket.

Y5

Year five — the decision point

By year five, a jacket is either going to last another five years or it isn't. This is the year to assess honestly. If the shell is intact, the seams are holding, and the only issues are lining and hardware — a full tune-up (KSh 5,000–KSh 7,000) gives you another five years. If the shell has gone thin at the elbows and shoulders, or the leather sleeves are cracked, it's probably time to retire it. A good tailor will tell you which one you're in. We always do.

Action: bring the jacket in for a full assessment. Then decide.

Can you alter a denim jacket without ruining the flat-felled seams?

That's the whole job on a denim jacket. Flat-felled seams — the two-needle folded seam that runs down the sides and around the hem — are the reason a denim jacket looks factory-made even after alteration. We use a twin-needle industrial machine and match the original seam width, stitch length and thread colour. If the alteration is done badly, the seam looks home-sewn and the jacket is ruined. Done right — which is how we do it — you can't find the change.

Can you alter the leather sleeves on my varsity jacket?

Yes, but this is specialist work. Leather varsity jacket sleeves are shortened from the shoulder, not the cuff — because leather leaves permanent needle holes, and re-stitching at the cuff would show. We remove the sleeve at the shoulder seam, shorten at the top, and re-stitch through the original stitch holes so no new damage appears. Takes about three hours per sleeve. Most Nairobi tailors refuse leather varsity work — it's one of the jobs we do that others won't.

Do you replace jacket zips with heavy-duty ones?

Can you alter a puffer jacket or quilted jacket?

With a caveat, yes. Puffer and quilted jackets have a wadding layer inside baffles — those horizontal stitched channels. Wadding can't be cut without destroying the panel. What is the difference between a bomber and a varsity jacket?

A bomber is usually a single-fabric jacket — nylon, polyester or poly-cotton — with a zip front and elasticated ribbed cuffs and hem. A varsity jacket is a two-tone jacket with leather or wool sleeves and a contrasting body fabric, a snap-button front, and ribbed cuffs, hem and collar. The varsity is harder to alter because leather behaves differently from wool, and the contrasting materials require different stitching techniques on the same garment.
