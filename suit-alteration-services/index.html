The garment with the highest stakes

Why a suit alteration is the most consequential job in a tailor's workshop

A suit is not just a jacket and a pair of trousers. It's a single system — three pieces (or two) that have to work together as one garment on a body that is standing, sitting, walking, reaching and being photographed. The jacket has to sit on the shoulders, the sleeves have to break at the wrist, the jacket hem has to fall below the seat, the trousers have to sit at the natural waist and break on the shoe. Get any one of those wrong and the whole suit looks wrong, even if the other two pieces fit perfectly. That's what makes a suit alteration different from any other garment on this bench.

The jacket has a canvas — and the canvas has to move

Every well-made suit jacket has a floating horsehair canvas inside the chest and shoulders. This canvas is what gives the jacket its shape, its roll of the lapel, and the way it drapes on the wearer. It's not stitched down everywhere — it floats inside the jacket, attached at the shoulder seam, the lapel edge and a few tacks in the chest. When you slim the body of a jacket, you can't just re-cut the side seam. The canvas inside has to move with it — by hand. We hand-stitch the canvas to its new position at every body slimming, shoulder adjustment or lapel roll correction. It takes an extra forty minutes per jacket. Most Nairobi tailors skip it. Skipping it is why a slimmed jacket starts to lose shape within a few months.

The sleeve pitch — the most invisible detail on a suit

Sleeve pitch is the angle at which the sleeve hangs from the shoulder. On a well-fitted suit, the sleeve should hang slightly forward — following the natural forward curve of the arm — so that the jacket sleeve moves with the wearer when they walk and sit. If the sleeve pitch is wrong, the sleeve will twist as the wearer moves, and the front of the sleeve will pull away from the wrist while the back sits too low. This is a problem most people don't know the name of, but they can feel it. When we shorten a sleeve, we always check the pitch first. If it needs correcting (a specialist adjustment that re-sets the sleeve at the shoulder), we tell the client. It's a KSh 2,500 job on its own, but it's the difference between a sleeve that hangs right and one that always looks slightly off.

The shoulder is sacred — and it's the first thing to measure

On a suit jacket, the shoulder is the single most important measurement. If the shoulders fit, almost everything else can be adjusted. If the shoulders don't fit, the suit will never look right no matter what you do to the rest of it. This is why we always measure shoulders first, then work outward to the sleeve, the chest, the body and the hem. A shoulder adjustment on a suit jacket — reducing width, adding width, correcting slope — is a specialist job that most tailors avoid. We do it regularly, but only when it's genuinely needed. If the shoulders on a client's jacket are within half a centimetre of correct, we don't touch them — because any shoulder work is risky and the payoff rarely justifies it.

The trouser break — a two-millimetre decision

The break on a suit trouser is the point where the hem meets the shoe. There are three classic options: no break (hem touches the shoe with no fold), single break (one slight fold), and double break (two folds). No break reads as modern and Italian. Single break is the traditional all-purpose look. Double break reads as slightly old-fashioned or American. The difference between them is often as little as two millimetres of hem length — and the difference it makes to the whole outfit is disproportionate. This is why we never hem a suit trouser without knowing the wearer's shoe and their preferred break. A suit hemmed for shoes without socks, or without the client being clear on break preference, will look wrong when they wear it with real shoes to real occasions.

The three-piece system — jacket, waistcoat and trousers have to agree

On a three-piece suit, the jacket, waistcoat and trousers have to work together as one garment. The waistcoat has to sit low enough that the jacket buttons over it, but high enough that the trousers' waistband is covered. The waistcoat's back strap has to be at the same height as the jacket's side vent. The shirt collar has to show above the waistcoat's V. This is a system, not three separate garments. When we alter a three-piece suit, we do the trousers first, then the waistcoat, then the jacket — because the trousers set the vertical reference that everything else measures against. Altering a three-piece in the wrong order is how a suit ends up with a jacket that sits right but a waistcoat that doesn't.

Why this matters to your wallet

A well-altered KSh 30,000 suit looks like a KSh 90,000 suit. A badly-altered one looks worse than it did before the alteration. We turn down suit alterations every month because the honest answer is that the suit's structure has failed — the canvas has separated, or the shoulders have been compromised by a previous alteration, or the fabric has dry-cleaned thin. When we can't improve a suit, we tell the client. When we can, the alteration pays for itself many times over. It's the same standard we hold on blazer alterations and overcoat alterations.

Not all suits are built the same

Three suit tiers — three completely different alterations

A KSh 12,000 fused suit from a market supplier and a KSh 90,000 canvased suit from an Italian maker require completely different handling. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be ruined. Here's how we categorise suits before deciding what technique to use.

Entry tier

Fused suits — mass-market and mid-tier retail

The everyday suit — from market suppliers, fast-fashion and mid-tier retail. Fabric weight 250–320 gsm, usually a polyester-wool blend or a wool-poly blend. The front of the jacket is fused — a layer of heat-activated adhesive holds the interlining in place rather than a canvas floating inside. No horsehair, no hand-work at the shoulder. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 8,000 and KSh 20,000. The starting suit for most men.

What we do differentlyFused suits have no canvas to work around, so slimming the body is quicker — but the fused interlining can delaminate if the jacket is pressed too hot or steamed too aggressively. We press fused jackets through a muslin cloth at medium heat, never with direct steam on the fused area, and we avoid opening the front panel more than necessary because every re-fuse is one more chance for the adhesive to fail.

Everyday tier

Half-canvased suits — Italian and regional mid-tier

The classic better-made suit — SuitSupply, Boggi, mid-range Italian, better regional makers. Fabric weight 260–340 gsm, mostly pure wool or a wool-silk blend. The chest is canvased (a floating horsehair layer), the shoulders and sleeves are half-canvased. Properly constructed with a hand-set sleeve head, a working buttonhole cuff on the sleeve, sometimes a half-lining at the front. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 25,000 and KSh 60,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier we hand-move the canvas at every body alteration — no shortcuts. We preserve hand-set sleeve heads and never machine-stitch through a canvased chest panel. On sleeves with working buttonholes, we hand-rework the buttonholes when shortening the sleeve — machine-stitching buttonholes on a canvased suit is a sign the tailor has taken a shortcut the client will notice.

Premium tier

Full-canvased and bespoke suits

The suit you only alter once. Full-canvased jackets from Italian, British or Japanese makers — Zegna, Canali, Brioni, Huntsman, Ring Jacket. Fabric weight 280–400 gsm, pure wool, wool-silk blends, sometimes cashmere. Hand-padded lapels, hand-set sleeve heads, hand-finished buttonholes, hand-stitched linings visible at the inside edges. Precise construction to within a millimetre. Common above KSh 90,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier the alteration has to be invisible and the construction has to be preserved exactly. We hand-finish every seam. We re-set the sleeve head by hand if the sleeve is being shortened from the shoulder, and we never machine-stitch through the canvas, the lapel roll or the visible hand-stitching. On suits with hand-finished buttonholes at the cuff, we replicate the hand-finish precisely — the buttonhole has a specific look that machine work cannot reproduce. This takes a full extra day and is priced accordingly.

If you're not sure which tier your suit falls into, send a photo of the inside front panel of the jacket (canvas is often visible behind the lining) and the inside label on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five parts of a suit that affect the alteration

What a suit actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a suit as "a jacket and trousers in the same fabric." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a suit has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a suit.

01

The shoulder — the anchor of the whole garment

The shoulder of a suit jacket is where the sleeve joins the body, and it's the anchor for everything else. On a canvased jacket, the sleeve head is set by hand with a small amount of "ease" — extra fullness in the sleeve head that lets the shoulder roll naturally. When the shoulder fits, the jacket sits on the wearer the way it was designed to. When the shoulder doesn't fit, the jacket pulls across the chest, the collar gapes at the neck, the sleeve twists and the hem rides up. Shoulder adjustment is the highest-skill suit alteration we do. We measure shoulders first, always. And if they're within half a centimetre of correct, we leave them alone — because shoulder work is the one adjustment on a suit that can make things worse if it's done without a clear reason.

02

The chest canvas and lapel roll

On any suit jacket that isn't fused, there's a floating canvas inside the chest — a woven cloth of horsehair and wool that gives the jacket its shape and the roll of the lapel. The canvas is attached at the shoulder seam, the lapel edge and a few tacks in the chest, but floats freely elsewhere. When we alter the body of the jacket, we hand-move the canvas to its new position — never cut it. When we adjust the lapel roll, we reshape the canvas by hand over a ham and let it settle into the new curve over 24 hours. This is slow work and most Nairobi tailors skip it. Skipping it is why a slimmed jacket starts to lose shape within a few months.

03

The sleeve and the sleeve head

The sleeve is the part of the jacket the wearer sees most often — every time they raise an arm, or rest their hands on a table, or button the jacket. On a well-made suit, the sleeve head (the point where the sleeve joins the shoulder) is set by hand, and the pitch of the sleeve (the angle at which it hangs) is tuned to the wearer's body. When we shorten a sleeve, we shorten at the cuff end — never at the sleeve head — because re-setting a sleeve head by hand is a shoulder job, not a sleeve job. On suits with working cuff buttons (the buttons that actually unbutton), we hand-move the buttons and hand-rework the buttonholes so the jacket still looks hand-finished.

04

The jacket body and vent

The body of the jacket has a front panel, a back panel and side seams. Venting — a single centre vent, double side vents, or no vent — is what allows the jacket to move with the wearer when they sit and walk. A suit jacket with no vent sits rigidly on the wearer; a single-vent jacket opens slightly at the centre back; a double-vent jacket opens on both sides. When we slim the jacket body, we preserve the vent structure exactly. When we lengthen or shorten a jacket, we re-set the vent opening at its original height. On a jacket whose vent has been compromised by a previous bad alteration, we tell the client upfront — because re-setting a vent that has been cut too short is a specialist job that sometimes isn't worth doing.

05

The trousers — waistband, rise, break and seat

On a suit trouser, four things have to line up with the jacket and with each other. The waistband sits at the natural waist (usually just below the navel on a classic fit, higher on a modern slim fit). The rise determines how high the waistband sits relative to the crotch. The break determines where the hem meets the shoe. The seat determines whether the rear of the trouser pulls or drapes. All four have to be correct for the trouser to look right and for the jacket to sit properly over the waistband. When we alter a suit trouser, we work top-down — waist and rise first, then seat, then hem — because each adjustment changes the vertical reference for the one below.

If a tailor quotes a suit alteration without asking about the shoulders, the sleeve pitch, or the trouser break, they're about to alter a suit into something that will bother you for years. We'd rather ask five questions upfront than see you back with a suit that doesn't get worn.

The cloth decides the technique

The five suit cloths we work with most often — and how each one behaves

The cloth decides how the alteration behaves under a needle, how it presses, and how the suit ages after the first few wears. Here's our honest breakdown by cloth type, the same one we'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Worsted wool — the classic business suit

The standard for a business suit. Worsted wool is smooth, tightly woven and holds a crisp line — it's what most city suits are cut from. Worsted presses beautifully, ages well, and takes alteration without complaint. The trade-off is that worsted wool can look shiny if over-pressed (usually a problem with cheap worsted, less so with better cloth). We press worsted through a muslin cloth at medium heat and never on a fused jacket without checking the fusing first. Worsted is our default recommendation for a working suit that will be worn two or three times a week.

Fresco and hopsack — summer suit cloths

Open-weave wool cloths designed to breathe. Fresco is a tightly twisted worsted that resists wrinkling and creates a crisp, textured surface. Hopsack is a looser basket-weave cloth that's more casual and more breathable. Both are ideal for Nairobi's warm months and for travelling. Fresco and hopsack hold shape well but crease visibly at the elbows and knees if the wearer sits for long — a natural property of the open weave. We cut with a small allowance on both cloths so the fabric can recover after a long day. Fresco and hopsack both take alteration well, but they take a press less crisply than worsted.

Flannel — winter and country suit cloth

Soft, brushed wool with a matte finish. Flannel is warmer than worsted, drapes beautifully, and reads as slightly more casual. It's the cloth of choice for country suits, wedding suits in cool climates, and any suit worn between May and September in Nairobi. Flannel takes alteration well but shows wear at the elbows, the knee and the seat faster than worsted — the brushed surface pills over time. We take extra care on flannel alterations to press gently (the brushed surface can flatten if you press it too hard) and to reinforce stress points while the garment is open.

Linen and linen blends — hot-weather suits

Pure linen suitings are the iconic summer cloth — light, breathable, comfortable. The trade-off is that pure linen wrinkles constantly and has almost no natural recovery. Most modern linen suits are linen-wool or linen-silk blends that soften the wrinkling without losing the cool feel. Linen frays aggressively at a cut edge, so every raw cut on a linen suit gets overlocked before the seam is closed. Linen also doesn't press as crisply as wool, and it can develop a shine if pressed too hot. On a linen suit alteration, we work with the natural rumple rather than trying to press it out.

Wool-silk and wool-cashmere blends — the luxury tier

The cloth of premium suits. Wool-silk blends give a slight sheen and a beautiful drape — very common in Italian tailoring. Wool-cashmere blends add softness and warmth without much weight gain. Both are more delicate than pure wool, more expensive, and require more care during alteration. Silk content means the fabric is fragile under a hot needle; cashmere content means it responds beautifully to steam but hates direct heat. On a wool-silk or wool-cashmere suit, we hand-finish every seam and never machine-stitch through the canvas. A careless alteration on a KSh 90,000 wool-silk suit is a KSh 90,000 mistake.

Polyester and poly-wool blends

Common on entry-tier and mid-tier suits. The polyester content gives the suit better shape retention and makes it more resistant to wrinkling, but it also makes the fabric more sensitive to heat — hot ironing will leave a permanent shine on the surface. Poly-wool also doesn't press with the softness of pure wool, so the shaping we can achieve on a canvased suit is more limited. We press poly-wool blends through a muslin cloth at low temperature and never with direct steam on the fused interlining. On poly-wool suits, we also avoid heavy stitching because the fabric can pucker around dense seam lines.

If you don't know what your suit is made of, send a close-up photo of the fabric and the inside label on WhatsApp. We'll identify the cloth and tell you straight what's possible.

How Nairobi actually asks about suit alterations — in the words people use

Half our WhatsApp inbox arrives in Kiswahili, a third in English, and the rest in the mix that only Nairobi speaks. These are the suit questions we see most often, answered the way we'd answer over the counter.

A short note from the bench

Sleeve pitch — the one thing about a suit most people can feel but can't name

When a client brings me a suit jacket and says "something feels off but I can't tell what," nine times out of ten it's the sleeve pitch. Not the sleeve length. Not the shoulder width. The pitch — the angle at which the sleeve hangs from the shoulder. On a well-made suit jacket, the sleeve should hang slightly forward, following the natural forward curve of the human arm. When the pitch is correct, the jacket sleeve moves with you when you walk, sit, or reach for something. When it's wrong, the sleeve twists slightly every time you move, and the front of the sleeve pulls away from the wrist while the back sits too low. The client can feel it every time they put their hands in their pockets. Most tailors never mention it because most clients don't know the term. But I do, and I check it on every jacket that comes into the workshop.

Here's how I think about sleeve pitch. An arm is not a straight stick — it's a cylinder that hangs slightly forward of the torso when relaxed, and moves backward and forward constantly through the day. When a suit jacket sleeve is cut at the correct pitch, the sleeve fabric rolls with the arm and always sits in the correct position. When a suit jacket sleeve is cut straight down (or set at the wrong angle), the sleeve fights the arm every time it moves. On a fused jacket, this isn't fixable without re-cutting — the interlining holds the sleeve in whatever direction it was pressed. On a canvased jacket, the sleeve head is hand-set, which means we can re-set it — hand-stitching the sleeve head at a slightly different angle so the pitch is corrected. That's a KSh 2,500 job on its own, and on a good suit it's the single most transformative alteration you can do.

The other thing clients don't always think about is the order in which we alter a suit. Most tailors alter a suit in the order the client asks for it — "shorten the sleeves, then take in the waist, then hem the trousers." I don't work that way. I always start with the shoulders, because the shoulders are the anchor. Then I move to the chest and the body — the canvas has to move here, which is the slowest part. Then the sleeves, where I check the pitch before I shorten. Then the trouser waist and rise. Then the trouser seat. Then the trouser hem and break. Each of those steps changes the vertical reference for the next one. If you alter a suit in the wrong order, you're constantly re-measuring — and every re-measurement is a chance for a small error to compound into a visible one.

I want to also say something about the canvas, because clients don't always understand why a canvased suit costs more to alter than a fused one. The canvas is what gives a good suit its shape. It's a floating layer of horsehair and wool inside the chest that moves with the wearer, gives the jacket its roll at the lapel, and lets the jacket breathe on hot days. When I slim a canvased jacket, I don't just re-cut the side seam — I hand-move the canvas to its new position, stitch by stitch. That takes an extra forty minutes per jacket. On a fused jacket, there's no canvas to move, so the alteration is quicker and cheaper. But the trade-off is that a fused jacket has no memory — it will not develop the character and drape that a canvased jacket develops over years. That's the difference between altering a KSh 15,000 suit and altering a KSh 90,000 suit. Both can fit you well. Only one will age beautifully.

The last thing I'll say about suits is this. A suit is the one garment where the alteration budget rarely feels like a budget — because a well-altered suit is the difference between looking like you bought the suit and looking like the suit was made for you. On a KSh 40,000 suit, spending KSh 7,500 on a full package — sleeves, length, body, trousers, hem — is not an expense. It's what turns the suit into the suit you actually want to wear. Fourteen years of altering suits has taught me that the clients who get their suits altered properly are the ones who look forward to wearing them. The clients who skip it are the ones whose suits sit in the wardrobe.

Make your suit last between alterations

Eight habits that keep a suit out of the workshop

A suit is the most expensive garment in most wardrobes, and the one that spends the most time hanging in a cupboard. How you store it, how often you dry clean it, and how you rotate it decide how long the suit stays worth altering. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect an altered suit.

1. Rotate between at least two suits

A wool suit needs 24 to 48 hours to recover its shape after being worn. The fibres relax, the moisture evaporates, and the fabric returns to its original dimensions. Wear the same suit three days in a row and it never gets that recovery — the elbows stay creased, the seat stays stretched, and the canvas slowly loses its shape. If you own two suits and rotate them, each one gets worn half as often and lasts roughly twice as long. On a KSh 40,000 suit, that rotation is worth KSh 40,000 of extra wear.

2. Brush after every wear — before it goes back in the cupboard

The single most important habit for wool suits. A soft clothes brush, used before the suit goes back on the hanger, removes dust, pollen and food particles that moths feed on. Brush the wool with the nap, then against it, then with it again. Two minutes per suit. If you brush before storage, you remove most of the risk of moth damage before it starts. Bring the suit to us for a moth hole repair and we'll always ask you whether you brush — the answer is almost always no, and it's almost always why the hole is there.

3. Hang on a wide wooden or padded hanger

Suit hangers are not a place to economise. A thin wire hanger concentrates the weight of the jacket at a single narrow point on the shoulders and, over months, stretches that point out of shape. A wide wooden hanger with a shaped shoulder distributes the weight evenly across the jacket's shoulders so the jacket hangs the way it was designed to. Same for suit trousers — hang them by the hem or on a trouser bar, never folded over the hanger bar, which leaves a permanent crease at the knee. A good hanger costs KSh 500. It saves a suit worth 100 times that.

4. Air the suit for an hour before hanging it up

A suit that's been worn all day has picked up moisture from your body, your breath and the outside air. If you hang it directly in a closed cupboard, that moisture stays in the wool and slowly degrades the fibres — which is why suits that are stored badly always smell slightly stale. Hang the jacket on a hook or a chair back for an hour before it goes into the cupboard. Long enough for the moisture to evaporate and the jacket to recover its shape. This one habit roughly doubles the number of years a suit stays in good condition.

5. Dry clean sparingly — twice a year is plenty

Dry cleaning is hard on wool. The solvents strip natural oils from the fibres, and over time, a suit that's been cleaned four or five times a year will feel thinner and look flatter than the same suit cleaned once a year. On a wool suit worn twice a week, one dry clean at the end of the season is usually enough. Between dry cleans, spot clean with a damp cloth and brush the suit down. Air it after every wear. Every unnecessary dry clean shortens the life of the suit by one cycle.

6. Steam instead of pressing

A suit jacket should never be pressed flat with an iron at home. The canvas, the lapel roll and the sleeve head all have shape that a flat press destroys. If the suit has wrinkles, hang it in a steamy bathroom for ten minutes and let the steam relax the fibres — no iron required. If you must iron, press only the trousers through a muslin cloth, and only the trouser — never the jacket. Any wrinkles in the jacket that steam won't fix are probably there because the jacket needs re-shaping, which is a workshop job, not a household one.

7. Store the suit in a breathable garment bag

Never store a wool suit in a plastic dry-cleaner's bag — plastic traps moisture against the wool and creates exactly the warm, humid environment that moths and mildew love. Use a cotton or canvas garment bag, or a clean cotton pillowcase if you don't have one. If you must store the suit long-term (say, between seasons), use a breathable bag, put a cedar block or lavender sachet in each pocket, and check the suit once a year. Cedar blocks should be lightly sanded once a year to refresh the scent.

8. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose stitch at the shoulder seam is a KSh 500 reinforcement job today. Left for three months, it becomes a KSh 2,500 shoulder rebuild. A small lining tear at the armpit is a KSh 1,000 patch today; left for a season, it becomes a KSh 2,500 full lining replacement. A waistband button that's come loose is KSh 300 today; left alone, it's a KSh 1,500 waist reconstruction when the suit falls apart at the front. The clients who spend the least on suit repairs are the ones who message us the first time they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less.

Follow those eight habits and your suit will hold its shape and its character for a decade. When it does eventually need work — a sleeve shortened, a lining rebuilt, a shoulder corrected — we're one WhatsApp message away.
