The fabric problem nobody talks about

Why a polo shirt is the hardest top in your wardrobe to alter

Most tailors will tell you a polo shirt is easy. Shorten the sleeves, taper the body, done in an hour. That's the answer you get from someone who has only worked on woven shirts. It's the wrong answer, and it's why so many altered polo shirts end up hanging in wardrobes unused.

Knit fabric doesn't behave like woven fabric

A dress shirt is woven — two sets of threads crossing each other at right angles. A polo is knit — one continuous loop of yarn, knitted into shape. This is a fundamental difference. When you cut woven fabric and stitch it, the seams stay where you put them. When you cut knit fabric, the loops near the cut edge start to unravel, and the fabric pulls back toward its natural shape. If you don't account for this, the polo looks fine when you collect it and starts to pucker after two washes.

Every polo alteration we do starts with identifying whether the knit is single or double. Single knits (most cheap polos) pull back harder. Double knits (piqué, interlock) hold their shape. Both are workable — but the technique differs by about 20% in seam allowance and stitch tension.

The collar is a whole separate craft

Polo collars are usually knit ribbing stitched onto the neckline, sometimes with a placket running down the front and two or three buttons. Collar problems are the number one reason polos end up in the alteration pile — stretched out of shape, drooping at the back, or curling at the points. Fixing a collar is not the same job as fixing a dress shirt collar, where you simply remove and reattach. On knit polos, the collar has to be re-knit to a specific tension or replaced with matching ribbing, which is why collar work takes longer than a sleeve shortening even though it looks smaller.

Sleeves hang differently on polos

Polo sleeves are usually short, with a ribbed band at the cuff. Shortening a dress shirt sleeve means folding the fabric and stitching a straight seam. Shortening a polo sleeve means removing the ribbing, shortening the sleeve body, and then reattaching the ribbing at exactly the original tension — otherwise the cuff sits wrong and the sleeve twists. On a poorly altered polo, this is the mistake that shows first: the sleeve points slightly off-centre instead of straight down.

Body slimming has to respect the side vents

Most good polos have side vents — small slits at the bottom hem that let the shirt move with you. When you slim a polo, you have to taper from the armpit down to the vent and stop, because if you taper through the vent, the vent no longer opens and the polo looks like a fitted shirt instead of a polo. We taper to the top of the vent, then leave the vent intact. Small detail. Changes everything about how the polo wears.

Why this matters to your wallet

We charge KSh 1,200 to slim a polo body. A tailor who doesn't know about side vents, rib cuffs and knit pull-back might charge KSh 700. Six weeks later, when the polo puckers at the sleeve or the vent has disappeared, you'll spend KSh 1,200 getting it fixed by someone who does know. Doing it right the first time is cheaper than doing it twice.

Read the label before you book

Three tiers of polo shirt — three different alteration jobs

A KSh 900 cotton polo from a market stall and a KSh 9,000 performance polo from a golf shop require completely different tailoring approaches. If your tailor treats both the same way, one of them is going to end up looking wrong. Here's how I categorise polos before I decide what technique to use.

Entry tier

Everyday cotton polos

Mass-produced cotton polos — from market stalls, mid-tier retailers and fast fashion. Fabric weight 180–220 gsm, usually single-jersey knit. Colour tends to fade over the first year. Standard ribbed collar and cuffs, sometimes a synthetic placket. The most common polo in Nairobi.

What we do differentlyWe cut slightly larger seam allowances on hem and slimming work because these knits pull back harder than premium ones. A sleeve shortened too tight will buckle after the second wash.

Mid tier

Piqué and interlock polos

Uniqlo, Muji, Massimo Dutti, mid-range Italian brands, and the classic Lacoste-style piqué. Double-knit construction, 220–280 gsm, better colour depth, cleaner ribbing at collar and cuffs. Often with 2–5% elastane for stretch. The standard polo for Nairobi professionals.

What we do differentlyThe double-knit structure means the fabric pulls back evenly, so we can taper more precisely. Elastane blends need a slightly looser stitch so the fabric still moves — done wrong, the seam buckles after one wash.

Premium tier

Performance, golf and designer polos

Technical golf polos, moisture-wicking athletic polos, and designer knits from Sunspel, Paul Smith, Peter Millar and similar. Fabric 150–250 gsm with synthetic blends, sometimes with mesh panels or laser-cut ventilation. Precise seam tolerances to within a millimetre.

What we do differentlyOn this tier, the alteration has to be invisible. We match thread colour, stitch length and ribbing tension precisely, and never cut through mesh panels. A careless alteration on a KSh 12,000 performance polo is a KSh 12,000 mistake.

If you're not sure which tier your polo falls into, send a photo of the label and the inside of the collar on WhatsApp. I'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five parts of a polo that affect the alteration

What a polo shirt actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a polo as "a t-shirt with a collar and buttons." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a polo has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what I look at before I take scissors to a polo.

01

The knit structure — single jersey, piqué or interlock

Almost every polo is knitted from one of three structures. Single jersey is the cheapest and lightest — it pulls back hardest after cutting. Piqué is the classic textured polo weave, with a slightly rougher surface and much more dimensional stability. Interlock is the smoothest and most structured, used in premium polos. The knit determines what stitch tension and needle size to use, and how much seam allowance to leave. This is the first thing I check, before anything else.

02

The collar and its attachment method

Polo collars are usually attached in one of two ways. The "set-in" method stitches the collar to a finished neckline — this is the modern approach, used on most polos. The "linked" method knits the collar as a continuous piece with the body — this is the traditional approach, used on higher-end polos. When we repair or replace a collar, the attachment method determines the technique. On set-in collars we can replace the whole collar. On linked collars, we have to re-work the join itself.

03

The placket — the button strip at the front

Every polo has a placket: the vertical strip of fabric running down the chest, holding two or three buttons. Placket construction varies widely. On cheap polos, it's a single strip of knit fabric. On better polos, it's a two-piece placket with an inner facing. When the placket is worn, frayed or has lost buttons, we rebuild it. This is one of the fiddliest jobs on a polo because the placket has to lie flat while still moving with the fabric around it.

04

The sleeve ribbing — a knit band, not a hem

Polo sleeves end with a ribbed knit band, not a folded hem. This ribbing is elastic and slightly denser than the sleeve body, which is what makes it grip the arm. When we shorten a polo sleeve, we detach the ribbing, shorten the sleeve body, and reattach the ribbing at the original tension. Getting the tension wrong is the most common mistake — the sleeve will twist or the ribbing will bunch after washing.

05

The side vents — the small slits at the hem

Good polos have side vents: short vertical slits at the bottom hem, usually 4–6 cm long, stitched at the top. The vents let the shirt move with you without pulling. When we slim a polo body, we have to taper down to the top of the vent and stop. If we taper through the vent, the vent closes and the polo looks like a fitted dress shirt. Small detail, big difference.

If a tailor quotes you a polo alteration without asking about any of those five things, they're guessing — and the result will show up after the second wash.

Before you book — does your polo actually fit?

Seven points on a polo that tell you it needs altering

Most people message me saying "my polo doesn't feel right." That's not enough information for me to quote accurately, and it's not enough for you to decide what to ask for. Here's the checklist I use when I look at a polo in the workshop. Run through it with your own polo on. If three or more of these are wrong, an alteration is worth it. If one or two, you may be able to live with it.

01

The shoulder seam sits at your shoulder point

Put the polo on and look in a mirror from the side. The shoulder seam — the line where the sleeve meets the body — should end exactly at the widest part of your shoulder bone. If it hangs past it, the polo is too big in the chest or shoulders. If it sits inside the shoulder line, the polo is too small and probably can't be fixed. Most polos that "feel too big" have this problem, and the fix is to take in the shoulder seam and armhole — a technical alteration that costs more than a body slimming.

02

The sleeve ends at mid-bicep, not the elbow

Polo sleeves should end around mid-bicep — roughly 15 to 18 cm above the elbow crease. If your sleeves reach your elbow, they're too long. If they end within 8 cm of the shoulder, they're too short and can't be lengthened. Sleeve shortening is one of our most common jobs and one of the fastest, but the ribbing has to be preserved and re-attached at the right tension, which is why a KSh 400 market job looks wrong after three washes and ours doesn't.

03

The chest lies flat with the placket closed

Button the placket all the way up, then run your hand across your chest. The fabric should lie smooth with no X-shaped wrinkle at the top button. If you see an X, the chest is too tight and the polo will pull at the buttons all day. If you see loose fabric pooling at the sides, the chest is too wide. Chest alterations on knitwear are done from the side seams — never the front, which would distort the placket.

04

The waist doesn't billow when you raise your arms

Raise both arms overhead. If the polo pulls up to reveal your stomach, the polo is too short or too narrow in the chest. If the sides flare out and stay flared when you lower your arms, the waist is too wide and needs slimming. This is the classic "baggy middle" complaint, and it's fixed with a taper from the armpit down to the side vents — never through them.

05

The hem falls to the middle of your fly

With the polo on and untucked, the front hem should fall to roughly the middle of your trouser fly — around 20 to 25 cm below your waistband. If it falls lower and covers your pockets, it's too long (common on polos bought in a size up for the chest). If it sits above the fly, it's too short and will ride up when you sit. Hem shortening is straightforward, but the side vents have to be preserved.

06

The collar sits flat with no curl at the points

Look in the mirror from the front. The collar points should lie flat against the chest, not curl up or flip. If the collar points curl, the ribbing has lost its memory and the collar needs re-tensioning or replacement. This is the number one reason polos end up in the alteration pile, and it's the hardest single job on a polo — the collar has to be re-knit to the correct tension or replaced entirely with matching ribbing.

07

The side vents open cleanly when you sit

Sit down and lean forward. The side vents at the hem — the small slits on the sides — should open slightly to give the polo room to move. If they stay closed and the fabric strains, the polo is too tight at the hips and needs slimming to be reversed (let out, not taken in). If they gape open even when you stand still, the polo is too loose and needs taking in. Either way, this is the detail that most tailors miss.

Run through all seven points. If three or more feel wrong, send me a photo on WhatsApp and I'll tell you honestly which alterations will make the biggest difference. You don't have to fix everything at once — sometimes the right answer is to fix one thing and live with the rest.

An honest decision — modify, or replace?

When a polo is worth altering, and when you should just buy a new one

I turn down jobs every month. Not because I can't do them — because the honest answer is that a KSh 1,500 alteration on a KSh 1,200 polo doesn't make sense. Here's the test I use before I quote you. If your polo fails more than two of these tests, buy a new one.

✓

Was the polo good quality to begin with?If it was a KSh 900 market polo, the fabric has probably faded or thinned after a year of washing. Altering it will fit better for a few months, then the fibres will fatigue and the alteration will start to look strained. A KSh 3,000+ piqué or performance polo is worth the alteration investment.

✓

Is the fabric still in good condition?Run your thumb over the fabric. Does it feel supple, or has it gone stiff, thin and papery? Supple fabric takes alteration well. Fatigued fabric will buckle along the seams after a few wears. If the fabric has gone soft and dusty-feeling in places, alteration will accelerate the damage rather than fix the fit.

✓

Is the collar still holding its shape?If the collar ribbing has gone floppy and stretched, replacing it costs almost as much as the polo did originally. If the collar is still crisp but everything else needs work, alteration makes sense. If the collar has gone, the alteration investment rarely pays back.

✓

Will the alteration cost less than 40% of a new one?If a mid-tier piqué polo costs KSh 3,500 new, an alteration should cost no more than KSh 1,400 to be worth doing. If it costs more, the numbers usually favour buying a new polo — unless the polo has sentimental value, which changes the calculation entirely.

✓

Is the fit fixable at all?Too big can be altered. Too small cannot — you can't create fabric where none exists. If the polo is even one size too small in the chest or shoulders, no tailor can save it. If it's one size too big, an alteration can make it fit perfectly. This is the single biggest factor in whether alteration makes sense.

✓

Does the polo have meaning beyond its price?A polo your father wore, a travel polo from a specific trip, a team polo from a specific year — the calculation changes entirely. On sentimental garments, we do the alteration no matter what the numbers say, because the value isn't in the fabric. Tell us when you message, and the quote is built around the meaning, not the price tag.

You won't get a hard sell when you message me. If the answer is "buy a new one," I'll tell you that straight. Better that than charging you for an alteration that stops looking right in six months.

A short note from the bench

Why collars are the hardest part of a polo

When clients ask me which polo alteration is the hardest, they expect the answer to be sleeve shortening or body slimming. It's not. The hardest single job on a polo is the collar — and specifically, the collar that has lost its memory.

A polo collar is a piece of ribbing — knit at a slightly tighter gauge than the body of the shirt, with elastic memory. It's designed to grip the neck, sit flat against the chest and return to shape after every wear. When the ribbing loses its memory (usually from repeated washing or from being left in a hot car), the collar points curl and the collar stops lying flat. You can't iron the memory back into ribbing — the fibres have permanently changed shape.

Fixing it requires one of two approaches. The first is to re-tension the existing ribbing — a slow, careful hand-stitch job that pulls the collar back toward its original shape without over-stretching it. This works on 40% of collars. It costs KSh 600 and takes two hours. The second approach, for the other 60%, is a full collar replacement — old collar removed, new ribbing sourced to match the original colour and gauge, cut and attached at the correct tension. This costs from KSh 1,200 and takes two to three days.

The reason this matters is that most Nairobi tailors quote a polo collar job at KSh 300 to KSh 500 and then either over-tension the collar (which makes it stand up) or replace it with wrong-colour ribbing (which shows from ten metres away). By the time you bring it to us to fix, we're doing a repair on a repair. It's cheaper to have it done once, properly.

If you own a polo where the collar has gone floppy and you're not sure whether it's worth fixing — send me a photo. I'll tell you straight whether re-tensioning will work, whether a full replacement makes sense, or whether the polo should be retired. That's the honest answer you deserve.

What's the difference between a piqué polo and a jersey polo for alterations?

Piqué is a double knit, which means it holds shape better than single jersey. That's good news for alteration — we can taper more precisely and the seams hold longer. Jersey polos (the cheaper, lighter category) pull back harder after cutting, so we cut with slightly larger seam allowances. Cost is the same for both tiers, but the finished fit on a piqué tends to look sharper because the fabric has more structure. If you're unsure which one you own, look at the surface — piqué has a textured, slightly waffled look; jersey is smooth.

Make them last between alterations

Eight habits that keep polo shirts out of the alteration pile

Polos take a beating. They're the shirt you wear most often, and the one you wash most often — and that cycle is what ages them fastest. Here are the eight habits I teach every client when they collect an altered polo. The same ones I follow with my own.

1. Wash cold, wash inside out

Polos fade from the outside in, so turning them inside out protects the visible surface. Cold water preserves the colour depth and prevents the knit from shrinking harder than it should. Skip the fabric softener — it leaves a residue that shortens the life of the fibre and dulls the colour over the first year. Wash with similar colours.

2. Air dry flat, never tumble

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shrink a polo by half a size and to weaken the ribbing in the collar and cuffs. Hang polos on a line or dry them flat on a rack. If you're in a hurry, point a fan at them. Takes longer, saves the shirt. Never hang them by the collar with a clip — the collar ribbing will stretch out of shape.

3. Iron inside out, collar last

Iron polos inside out or through a thin cloth. Direct high heat fades the surface and can shine the knit — especially on piqué. Iron the body first, then the sleeves, then finally the collar. The collar should be ironed last and gently, so you don't re-stretch the ribbing you just pressed flat.

4. Give the fabric 24 hours between wears

Knit fabric needs 24 hours to recover its shape and to dry out fully from body moisture. Wear the same polo two days in a row and the collar and cuffs stay stretched. Own three or four polos and rotate them — the fabric breathes better and the shirt looks fresher for years longer. This single habit alone halves the number of collar repairs we do on a given client.

5. Button the placket before washing

Polos with unbuttoned plackets twist in the wash and put stress on the buttonholes. Button all the way up before washing — the placket stays flat, the buttonholes stay intact, and you won't find yourself needing a placket rebuild six months early. Same reasoning for zip-front polos — close the zip before washing.

6. Deal with small issues the week you notice them

A loose button, a fraying placket stitch, a small pull in the collar ribbing — these are cheap and easy to fix when fresh. Left for months, they become structural problems. A loose button is KSh 300 today. A rebuilt placket is KSh 800 tomorrow. The clients who spend the least on alterations are the ones who message us first when they notice something.

7. Fold, don't hang, when storing

Hanging a polo stretches the shoulders and pulls the knit out of shape over a season. Fold polos flat and stack them in a drawer or on a shelf. If you must hang, use a padded hanger or a hanger with a wide bar — never a wire hanger, which distorts the shoulder seam. A folded polo looks fresher for years longer.

8. Keep one polo out of rotation

If you have a polo you wear only for special occasions — a wedding guest polo, a travel polo — keep it out of the daily cycle. Wash it less often, air it more. A polo that's washed once a month instead of once a week lasts five times longer. The fabric stays supple and the colour stays deep.

Follow those eight habits and your polos will hold their fit for years. And when they do eventually need an alteration, we're one WhatsApp message away.
