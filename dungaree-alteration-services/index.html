The structural reality nobody explains

Why dungarees are the most structurally complicated garment we alter

Most people think of dungarees as "heavy trousers with straps." That description misses the entire point. A dungaree is a garment suspended from your shoulders — every part of it depends on the shoulder straps being right. Change one measurement and the whole fit shifts. Here's what that means in practice.

Dungarees hang — they aren't worn on the waist

Ordinary trousers sit on your waist and stay there. Dungarees are suspended from your shoulders by straps. That changes everything about how the garment fits and how it's altered. If the straps are too long, the whole dungaree slides down. If the straps are too short, the bib pulls up into your chest and the crotch rides up. If the waist is taken in without adjusting the straps, the shoulder tension changes and the bib sits differently against your chest.

The bib is a structural panel, not decoration

The bib is the front panel of fabric that sits across your chest, held in place by the straps. It's usually lined or faced internally, and it has to lie flat against your chest without buckling. When we slim or reshape a dungaree, the bib has to be reshaped too — otherwise it flares away from your chest or sits crooked. Reshaping a bib properly requires opening it at the top seam, adjusting the internal facing, and re-closing it so the outer surface looks untouched. That's a two-hour job on its own.

Straps are a whole subsystem

Dungaree straps are a subassembly of their own. They have buckles or buttons, they attach to the bib at the top and to the back waistband at the bottom, and they often carry a cross-back (the X-shaped strap configuration on the back). Every adjustment to the strap length or buckle position changes how the whole garment hangs. When we adjust a strap, we check the tension at the top, the mid-section and the back anchor point — because if any one of those is off, the bib sits wrong within a week.

Waist and seat adjustments are unusual on dungarees

On ordinary trousers, waist and seat adjustments are done from the side seams and back seam. On dungarees, the side seams are also where the bib attaches and where the straps anchor at the back. So a waist adjustment on a dungaree has to be planned around three other features, not just tightened at the sides. Most alterations shops treat dungarees like trousers — they take in the sides, and the straps end up misaligned. Done properly, the waist adjustment on a dungaree starts at the side seams, coordinates with the strap anchors, and finishes with the bib reshaped to sit flat again.

Buckles, buttons and rivets fail on dungarees more than on any other garment

Dungaree hardware takes stress every single day. The buckles at the straps hold the entire weight of the garment. The buttons on the bib and the rivets at stress points are all under tension. Standard plastic buckles and light-gauge buttons fail on dungarees within a year of daily wear. We replace with metal-only hardware — brass or steel buckles, metal shank buttons, reinforced rivets. It costs more in materials. It's the only hardware that lasts.

Why this matters to your wallet

We charge KSh 1,500 to slim a dungaree body. A general alterations shop might charge KSh 800 — and they'll take in the side seams, distort the bib, misalign the strap anchors, and the dungaree will fit worse after the alteration than before. Doing it right the first time is cheaper than doing it twice. That's the same standard we hold on cargo pant alterations and jeans alterations.

Read the fabric before you book

Three tiers of dungaree fabric — three different alteration jobs

A KSh 2,000 cotton dungaree from a market stall and a KSh 20,000 heavy canvas workwear overall require completely different alteration approaches. If your tailor treats both the same way, one of them is going to end up looking wrong. Here's how I categorise dungarees before I decide what technique to use.

Lightweight

Cotton twill and fashion dungarees

Lightweight cotton twill, sometimes with elastane for stretch. Fabric weight 200–280 gsm. Used in fashion dungarees from mid-tier retailers, fast fashion, and some casual weekend overalls. Often unlined or half-lined. Buckles are usually plastic or light plastic-coated metal. The most common lightweight category in Nairobi.

What we do differentlyCotton twill with elastane pulls back after cutting, so we cut with a small allowance. On lighter dungarees, we reinforce every strap anchor point with an internal patch — the strap pull is where these fail fastest. Standard alteration time: two to three days.

Mid-weight

Denim dungarees and classic overalls

Denim at 10–14 oz or heavy cotton twill at 300–360 gsm. The classic dungaree fabric, worn for work, weekends and casual Fridays. Front bib with internal pocket, side pockets, sometimes tool loops. Metal buckles preferred. Full seam construction with flat-felled seams throughout. The most common dungaree type among Nairobi clients.

What we do differentlyFlat-felled seams take a heavier needle and thread than standard garment work. We use an industrial walking-foot machine and triple-stitch at stress points. On denim dungarees, matching the original thread colour and stitch length matters more than on any other dungaree — the flat-felled seams are visible from metres away.

Heavy-duty

Canvas, duck cloth and workwear overalls

Heavy canvas and duck cloth at 12–16 oz, or a canvas-denim hybrid. Used in proper workwear — mechanics, painters, welders, factory staff, security uniforms. Sometimes waterproof-coated. Multi-pocket construction with tool loops, knee reinforcements, and heavy buckles. Buckles are usually metal and can weigh over 100 grams each. Irreplaceable for the client who wears them every day.

What we do differentlyCanvas is difficult to hem cleanly because the needle struggles through three layers. We do most of the work by hand with a saddler's needle. On coated canvas, pressing is done through a muslin cloth at low temperature to avoid burning the coating. On workwear, every seam we alter is reinforced twice — workwear takes far more stress than fashion wear.

If you're not sure which tier your dungarees fall into, send a photo of the label and the inside of the bib on WhatsApp. I'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The six parts of a dungaree that affect the alteration

What a dungaree actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe dungarees as "overalls." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a dungaree has six parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what I look at before I take scissors to a pair of overalls.

01

The bib — the front chest panel

The bib is the flat panel of fabric sitting across your chest, held in place by the straps. It's usually lined or faced internally with the same fabric as the body, and it carries the top buttons or buckles where the straps attach. When we reshape the body, the bib has to be reshaped too — otherwise it flares away from your chest or pulls tight across the shoulders. We reshape bibs by opening the top seam, adjusting the internal facing, and re-closing the seam so the outer surface looks untouched.

02

The straps — length, buckle position and cross-back

Straps run from the top of the bib, over your shoulders, and attach at the back of the waistband. On classic dungarees, straps cross at the back in an X-configuration; on some modern dungarees they run straight over the shoulder. Every strap has a buckle or a button that adjusts the length. When we adjust a strap, we check tension at the top (bib anchor), the mid-section (over the shoulder) and the bottom (back waistband anchor) — because all three have to work together or the bib sits wrong after a week of wearing.

03

The waistband — where straps anchor and where the seat attaches

The dungaree waistband is often wider than a trouser waistband — 5 cm or more — and it carries the back anchor buttons or loops where the straps attach. When we take in a waist, the strap anchor positions have to be re-checked, because if we simply tighten the sides the anchor points shift inward and the straps sit at a different angle across the back. That change is visible through the bib within a few wears.

04

The seat and crotch — V-joint or flat seam construction

Dungarees are cut with a slightly deeper seat than trousers because they hang from the shoulders rather than sitting on the waist. The crotch construction on quality dungarees uses a reinforced V-joint — the same reinforcement we see on cargo pants. When we adjust the seat or rise, we coordinate with the bib length so the whole front panel sits correctly. On a dungaree, the seat and bib are structurally linked — you can't adjust one without considering the other.

05

The legs — hem, knee reinforcement, and tool loops

The legs on dungarees are usually cut slightly wider than trouser legs to allow for movement and to sit over boots or heavy shoes. Many workwear dungarees have knee reinforcements (either internal or visible panels) and tool loops. When we shorten the hem or slim a leg, we work around the knee panel and preserve the tool loops at their original position. Hem finishes on dungarees are usually a heavier flat-felled hem than on trousers — we reproduce that finish at the new length.

06

The hardware — buckles, buttons, rivets, and zip

Dungaree hardware takes more stress than any other garment's hardware. The buckles at the straps carry the entire weight of the garment. The buttons on the bib and the rivets at the crotch and pocket corners are under tension every time you move. Standard plastic buckles fail on dungarees within a year of daily wear. We replace with metal-only — brass or steel buckles, metal shank buttons, reinforced rivets. On workwear, hardware is the difference between a pair that lasts two years and a pair that lasts ten.

If a tailor quotes you a dungaree alteration without asking about the straps, the bib, or the hardware, they're guessing — and the result will show up within the first week of wearing.

The subsystem that makes or breaks a dungaree

How strap construction actually works — and why it matters

Every complaint I hear about dungarees that "don't fit right" traces back to the strap system. Understanding how the straps are built and how they interact with the bib, the waist and the seat is the difference between a comfortable fit and a garment that lives in a drawer. Here's the short version of what fourteen years of altering dungarees has taught me.

Strap length is not a preference — it's a system

Strap length determines where the bib sits on your chest, how much room there is under your arms, and how the waistband sits. When we shorten or lengthen a strap, we're not adjusting one measurement — we're adjusting the whole fit. Adding 2 cm of strap length lowers the bib by 2 cm, which drops the waistband by roughly 1 cm, which in turn changes where the hem sits. There's no single adjustment; every change has to be planned as a chain.

The X-back (cross-back) is what stops the straps slipping

On classic dungarees, the straps cross at the back in an X. This cross is not a stylistic choice — it's a functional one. The X-back keeps the straps centred on your shoulders, stops them sliding off, and distributes the garment's weight evenly across your back. When we adjust strap length on an X-back dungaree, we have to preserve the crossing point — moving it up or down even 1 cm changes where the straps sit on your shoulders. Repositioning an X-back to fit a client who's lost or gained weight is one of the most technical jobs on a dungaree.

Buckles take 100% of the strap load

The buckle on each strap is the single point that carries the entire weight of the dungaree through that strap. On a heavy workwear pair that weighs 800 g, each buckle is holding roughly 400 g of fabric against gravity all day. Standard plastic buckles fail within a year of daily wear. We replace with brass or steel buckles — they cost three to four times as much as plastic, but they last five years instead of one. On workwear, this is non-negotiable.

Strap width affects how the load is carried

Wider straps spread the load over a larger surface area of your shoulder, which reduces pressure points and makes the dungaree comfortable to wear all day. On lighter fashion dungarees, straps can be as narrow as 25 mm. On workwear, they're often 40–50 mm. When we replace a strap, we match the original width unless the client asks for a wider replacement — which we'll do happily, at the cost of a longer strap and thicker buckles.

The bib anchor point is where most dungarees fail

Where the strap attaches to the top of the bib is where the tension is highest. The fabric around the button or buckle carries the load from the strap, and if the anchor isn't reinforced, the bib tears here first. When we take in or let out a dungaree, we add an internal reinforcement patch behind the anchor point as standard. It's a fifteen-minute step that costs us a bit of material and roughly doubles the life of the bib anchor.

Adjusting the back anchor points is the last step, not the first

Most tailors adjust the back anchor points first when resizing a dungaree, because they're the easiest to reach. That's the wrong order. The correct sequence is: adjust the bib, then the straps, then the waistband, and finally the back anchor points. If you move the anchors first, you end up repositioning them twice. We work in that sequence on every dungaree, and it's the reason our alterations look factory-standard on the second wearing.

Before you book — do your dungarees actually fit?

Seven checkpoints that tell you if dungarees need altering

Most clients message saying "my overalls don't fit right." That's not enough for me to quote accurately, and it's not enough for you to decide what to ask for. Here's the checklist I use on every pair in the workshop. Run through it with the dungarees on. If three or more are off, the pair is worth altering. If one or two, you may be able to live with it.

01

The bib sits flat against your chest, with no gap at the top

Stand in front of a mirror and look at the bib panel across your chest. It should sit flat without buckling, flaring or gaping away from the body. If it pulls away at the top, the straps are too tight. If it buckles across the middle, the bib length is wrong for your torso. If it sits flat but pulls tight across the chest, the bib is too small — a hard-to-fix problem. Reshaping a bib is a KSh 1,000 job; replacing it entirely is a full restyle.

02

The straps sit centred on your shoulders, not sliding outward

With the dungarees on and the straps buckled, look at where the straps pass over your shoulders. They should sit roughly centred — slightly inside the shoulder bone, not outside it. If they slide outward or fall off your shoulders during normal movement, the strap length or the X-back crossing point is wrong. Strap adjustment is KSh 800. Repositioning the X-back crossing is a longer, more technical job that starts at KSh 1,500.

03

The waistband sits at your natural waist, not on your hips

Dungarees hang from your shoulders, but the waistband still needs to sit at the right height on your body — usually at your natural waist, just below the belly button. If the waistband has slipped down to your hips, the straps are too long and need shortening. If it's riding up under your ribs, the straps are too short. Waist take-in is KSh 1,200; the strap adjustment to raise or lower the waistband is KSh 800.

04

The side seams are vertical, not skewed

Look at the side seams running down each leg from the waistband to the hem. They should drop vertically. If they skew backward or forward, the waist and seat don't align with the leg — usually from a previous alteration done wrong, or from the seat being too deep. We correct side-seam skew by re-shaping at the waistband and re-aligning the leg — from KSh 1,500. This is one of the jobs most alterations shops miss entirely because they don't inspect the side seam before cutting.

05

There's room to squat, bend and reach without pulling

Squat down and reach for something on the floor. Then reach up as if for a shelf. Then twist to the side. On properly fitted dungarees, none of those movements should pull hard at the crotch or lift the waistband more than a centimetre. If the crotch pulls hard during a squat, the rise is too short — a job for seat and rise adjustment (KSh 1,500). If the waistband lifts significantly when you reach up, the bib length is off and needs reshaping.

06

The legs fall cleanly without billowing or gripping

Look at the leg from the hip down to the hem. It should fall in a clean line — neither billowing out like a sail nor gripping the leg tightly. If the legs billow, they need slimming (KSh 1,500). If they grip, they were cut too narrow or the fabric has shrunk. On workwear, a slightly looser leg is expected — on fashion dungarees, a slimmer taper looks modern. Tell me which you prefer when you message.

07

The hem ends at the right point — usually just above the shoe

Hem length on dungarees is different from trouser length. On workwear, the hem traditionally ends slightly higher than a trouser hem so it sits over a boot without dragging. On fashion dungarees, the hem usually breaks once over the shoe. Check that your hem doesn't drag on the ground or sit so high that the dungaree looks cropped. Hem shortening with a heavy flat-felled finish is KSh 1,200. Original hem preservation is available on request.

Run through all seven. If three or more feel wrong, send me a photo on WhatsApp and I'll tell you honestly which alterations will make the biggest difference. You don't have to fix everything at once — often the right answer is to fix one thing and live with the rest.

An honest decision — alter, or replace?

When dungarees are worth altering, and when you should just buy a new pair

I turn down dungaree jobs every month. Not because I can't do them — because the honest answer is that a KSh 1,500 alteration on a KSh 1,200 fashion dungaree doesn't make sense. Here's the test I use before I quote you. If your dungarees fail more than two of these, buy a new pair.

✓

What fabric are they made from?Fashion dungarees in lightweight cotton twill have a short working life — the fabric thins, the buckles fail, and the seams start to fray within two years of daily wear. Heavy denim and canvas workwear improves with use and holds alteration results for years. The fabric tier determines whether the investment makes sense. Workwear almost always qualifies; fashion dungarees usually don't.

✓

Is the bib still in good condition?Run your hand across the bib panel. Does it feel solid, or has it gone soft and thin from repeated washing? The bib is a structural panel — if it's fatigued, any alteration we do will look right when you collect it and start to sag within a month. If the bib has gone soft, the pair is usually better replaced than altered.

✓

Are the straps and hardware still solid?Are the buckles metal or plastic? Are the strap anchor points still holding firm at the bib? If the buckles are plastic and one is already cracked, the pair needs hardware replacement (KSh 400 per buckle) before anything else makes sense. If the strap anchors at the bib are torn, we can reinforce them — from KSh 700 — but a full strap rebuild with new anchors is more expensive than a new pair on fashion dungarees.

✓

Will the alteration cost less than 40% of a new pair?If a mid-weight denim pair costs KSh 4,500 new, an alteration should cost no more than KSh 1,800 to be worth doing. If it costs more, the numbers usually favour buying new — unless the pair is a specific fit you can't easily replace or the fabric quality is exceptional. On heavy canvas workwear, the calculation shifts because replacement cost is much higher and the fabric lasts far longer.

✓

Is the fit fixable at all?Too big can be altered. Too small cannot — you can't create fabric where none exists, especially on a heavy fabric dungaree where the waistband has no seam allowance. If the pair is one size too small in the chest or hips, no tailor can save it. If it's one size too big, an alteration can make it fit perfectly. On a dungaree, the bib height is the dealbreaker — most other measurements can be adjusted.

✓

Does the pair have meaning beyond its price tag?A pair of dungarees from your first job, a pair that got you through art school, a workwear pair worn by a parent — the calculation changes entirely. On sentimental garments, we do the alteration no matter what the numbers say, because the value isn't in the fabric. Tell us when you message, and the quote is built around the meaning, not the price tag.

You won't get a hard sell when you message me. If the honest answer is "buy a new pair," I'll tell you that straight. Better that than charging you for an alteration that stops looking right in six months.

A short note from the bench

On a dungaree, every adjustment is a chain reaction

When a client asks me to "just shorten the hem" on a pair of dungarees, I always ask two questions before I quote. Where does the waistband sit right now? And how tight do the straps pull on the shoulders? If the answer to either question surprises the client, we're not talking about a hem job — we're talking about a chain reaction that starts at the hem and ends at the bib.

Here's what I mean. On trousers, you can shorten a hem without touching anything above it. The waist stays the same. The seat stays the same. Nothing else moves. On dungarees, that's not true. When you shorten the hem of a dungaree, the whole garment sits slightly differently on the wearer because the weight distribution changes. If the hem was short already, shortening it further can lift the waistband — which in turn changes how the bib sits — which in turn affects how the straps pull across the shoulders. I have seen a 2 cm hem shortening create a 4 cm rise in the waistband on a pair of heavy canvas workwear dungarees.

The same is true in reverse. When a client wants the straps shortened because the dungarees "hang low," we don't just shorten the straps. We shorten the straps, then re-check the bib height, then re-check where the waistband sits, then re-check the hem length relative to the new waist height. On average, a full strap adjustment job touches four parts of the garment — and we always confirm the sequence with the client before we cut.

This is why dungaree alterations cost more than trouser alterations of the same apparent scope. It's not because we're charging extra for the word "dungaree." It's because the actual work is on four parts of the garment, not one. A tailor who quotes KSh 500 for a "quick hem" on dungarees is either not planning to check the rest of the fit, or doesn't know that the chain reaction exists. Either way, the client pays for it in the second visit.

If your dungarees feel wrong somewhere and you're not sure where the problem actually lives — send me three photos. Front, back, and one of you wearing them side-on. I'll tell you which part of the chain is off and what the alteration actually needs to fix. That's the answer you deserve, and it's the one you'll get.

Make them last between alterations

Eight habits that keep dungarees out of the alteration pile

Dungarees take more punishment than any other garment in a workwear wardrobe. Worn daily, washed weekly, loaded with tools and repeatedly stretched at the straps. Here are the eight habits I teach every client when they collect an altered pair. The same ones I follow with my own.

1. Wash cold, wash inside out

Dungarees fade from the outside in, so turning them inside out protects the visible surface — the bib, the legs and the back panel. Cold water preserves the colour depth and prevents cotton and canvas from shrinking harder than they should. Skip the fabric softener; it leaves a residue that shortens the fibre's life and dulls the colour over the first year. Wash with similar colours and don't overfill the drum.

2. Air dry flat or on a line — never tumble

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shrink a heavy canvas or denim dungaree by half a size, and it weakens the strap anchor points at the bib. Hang the dungarees from the back waistband on a line, or dry them flat on a rack. If you're in a hurry, point a fan at them. Takes longer, saves the pair. Never hang by the straps alone — the weight of the wet fabric will stretch the strap anchors.

3. Unbuckle before washing

Dungarees with buckled straps should have the buckles released before washing. If they're left buckled, the metal buckles can damage the drum, the strap fabric can twist, and the tension at the bib anchor can distort. Unbuckle, lay the straps flat, and wash the dungarees as a single garment. This simple habit doubles the life of both the straps and the buckles.

4. Give the fabric 48 hours between wears

Canvas and heavy denim need 48 hours to recover their shape after a day's wear. Wear the same pair two days in a row and the bib stays compressed, the waistband stretches, and the strap anchors stay under tension. If you work in dungarees daily, own at least two pairs and rotate them. This single habit alone halves the number of strap-anchor repairs we do on any given workwear client.

5. Don't overload the bib pocket

The chest pocket on a bib is designed for a phone, a small notebook or a pen. If you carry heavy tools or a full water bottle in the bib pocket, the fabric stretches and the pocket bag tears at the bottom corner. Use the leg pockets or a tool belt for anything over 300 g. The bib pocket is the second most common failure point after the strap anchors.

6. Deal with small issues the week you notice them

A loose stitch at the strap anchor, a fraying hem edge, a slightly stiff buckle — these are cheap and easy to fix when fresh. Left for months, they become structural problems. A strap anchor reinforcement is KSh 700 today. A full strap-anchor rebuild with new strap ends is KSh 1,800 tomorrow. The clients who spend the least on alterations are the ones who message us first when they notice something.

7. Store hanging, not folded, if you can

Dungarees store best hanging by the back waistband from a sturdy hook or hanger, so the fabric can breathe and the straps don't crease. If you must fold them, fold at the knees and the waist — never fold at the strap anchors, because the fold line can turn into a permanent crease that eventually tears. On workwear, a proper storage hook in the workshop or garage pays for itself in the first year.

8. Replace hardware before it fails

If your buckles are plastic and one has started to show stress marks or a slight crack, replace it now — KSh 400 for the buckle. If you wait for it to fail at work, you'll have the strap flapping loose and the bib dragging against your chest for the rest of the day. On workwear, we recommend upgrading to metal buckles on the first alteration, before the plastic ones fail. Small cost, big difference over five years.

Follow those eight habits and your dungarees will hold their fit and hardware for years. And when they do eventually need an alteration, we're one WhatsApp message away.
