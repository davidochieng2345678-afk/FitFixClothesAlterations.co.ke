The most structure in the least fabric

Why a waistcoat alteration is harder than a suit jacket alteration

A waistcoat looks like the simplest garment in the suit. It has no sleeves, no collar roll, no shoulder pads. But that's exactly what makes it hard to alter. On a suit jacket, a tailor has room to work — the sleeve can be re-hung, the chest can be re-padded, the shoulder can be adjusted. A waistcoat has none of that. Every seam on a waistcoat is visible, every adjustment changes the relationship between the front and the back, and there is nowhere to hide a mistake. Here's what we actually look at when a waistcoat lands on the bench.

The waist has to be adjusted without touching the front

The waist of a waistcoat is adjusted at the back — not the front. The front of the waistcoat carries the buttons, the buttonholes, the lapels and the two front points at the bottom, and every one of those is a fixed design element. If you take in the waist from the front side seams, you move the button stance and the points inward, and the waistcoat starts to look narrow and cheap. On a proper waistcoat alteration, the take-in happens at the back — either by re-cutting the back panel, or by adjusting the back strap and re-setting the lining. The front stays exactly where it was. Waist adjustment is KSh 1,500, and it's the alteration clients ask for most often.

The front points must stay straight and level

The two points at the bottom of a waistcoat — the V shape that sits over the trouser waistband — are the most important visual detail on the garment. If the waistcoat is shortened or the waist is taken in without checking the points, they end up tilted, uneven, or at the wrong height relative to the belt. On a proper waistcoat alteration, the points are measured before any cut, and the alteration is planned so they stay at the same height and angle. This is the single detail that separates a waistcoat that looks altered from one that looks made to measure. It's why we charge KSh 2,000 for body slimming instead of KSh 1,000 — the point check alone takes twenty minutes.

The back strap is the adjustment the wearer never uses

Most waistcoats have a small strap at the back with a buckle — it's the traditional way of adjusting the waistcoat for the wearer. But the strap only gives 2 to 3 centimetres of adjustment, and it doesn't fix a waistcoat that's genuinely too large or too small. When we alter a waistcoat that has been sized up or down significantly, we re-set the strap and buckle at the correct height and check that the strap still functions as intended. On badly sized waistcoats we replace the strap entirely with a matching one, keeping the original buckle where possible.

The welt pockets are the giveaway of a rushed alteration

Waistcoats usually have two welt pockets at the lower front. A welt pocket is a slit cut into the fabric with a narrow folded edge around it — a small, precise construction that is easy to damage and hard to re-cut. When we adjust the front of a waistcoat, the welt pockets have to be preserved exactly where they are. If a previous alteration has distorted the pockets, we re-cut the welt by hand and re-set the pocket opening on the correct line. Pocket re-set is KSh 800, and it's one of the most common repairs we do on waistcoats that have been altered badly elsewhere.

The lining is hand-finished and has to stay that way

The lining on a proper waistcoat is set in by hand — the back panel is often a lighter fabric (satin or a contrasting lining) and it's stitched to the front panels with hand-finished seams that are invisible from the outside. When we open a waistcoat for a waist alteration, the lining has to be opened and re-closed by hand so the original finish is preserved. On a badly made alteration the lining is machine-stitched through to the front, which shows as a line of stitching along the inside edge. We've re-done hundreds of these jobs — and on a good waistcoat, the lining is where you can tell in five seconds whether the alteration was done properly.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 1,500 waist adjustment on a KSh 8,000 three-piece suit is a rescue — the waistcoat keeps its points, its pockets, its strap and its lining, and the whole suit goes back into rotation. The same KSh 800 adjustment by a tailor who doesn't check the points, doesn't re-set the pockets, and machine-stitches through the lining is money wasted, because the waistcoat will look "off" even if it fits — and the wearer will usually end up replacing the whole suit rather than admit the waistcoat is the problem. That's the same standard we hold on suit alterations, blazer alterations and bridal and formal wear alterations.

Not every waistcoat is the same job

Three waistcoat tiers — three completely different alterations

A KSh 2,500 market waistcoat and a KSh 25,000 three-piece suit waistcoat look similar at a glance. They are not the same garment. One is a simple two-panel construction with a machine-finished lining and no welt pockets. The other is a hand-finished garment with a curtained back, welt pockets, and a strap that has to be re-set by hand. Treating them the same way is how a good waistcoat gets ruined. Here's how we categorise waistcoats before deciding which technique to use.

Everyday tier

Casual, market and rental vests

Mass-produced waistcoats from market stalls, fast fashion and rental shops. Poly-viscose or poly-cotton fabric. Two-panel construction with a machine-finished lining. No welt pockets — usually patch pockets or no pockets at all. Back strap is a simple elasticated band or a basic buckle. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 2,000 and KSh 6,000. The everyday waistcoat.

What we do differentlyOn these we work pragmatically — the goal is to make the waistcoat fit, not to restore structure that wasn't there. Standard machine stitch on plain poly seams, medium heat press through a muslin cloth. Where the waist is too generous, we take it in from the back strap and re-set the lining. Where the waistcoat is genuinely too large, we tell the client upfront that a KSh 2,500 waistcoat is worth a KSh 1,500 alteration, not a KSh 3,000 one.

Mid tier

Three-piece suit vests and better retail waistcoats

Mid-tier three-piece suit vests and better retail waistcoats. Pure wool, wool blend or better poly-viscose. Four-panel construction — two fronts, two backs, joined at the shoulder. Real welt pockets at the lower front. Back strap is a proper buckle with a ribbon adjuster. Lining is a lighter fabric, usually satin or bemberg, machine-finished but visible. Common between KSh 6,000 and KSh 15,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier we treat the waistcoat like what it is — a proper piece of formal wear worth altering carefully. Waist adjustments preserve the front button stance and the bottom points at the same height. Back strap re-set at the correct height relative to the waistband. Welt pockets preserved exactly where they are. Lining opened and re-closed so the original finish is preserved. Every step is measured, not estimated.

Premium tier

Bespoke, Savile Row and designer waistcoats

Hand-finished waistcoats from bespoke makers and high-end designers. Pure wool, silk, or fine tweed. Four-panel construction with a hand-finished lining — often a contrasting colour or a hand-stitched edge. Welt pockets are hand-cut. Back panel often a fine satin or a contrasting wool. Bottom points are often hand-pressed. Back strap is a hand-stitched ribbon with a hand-forged buckle. Frequently above KSh 25,000 as part of a bespoke three-piece.

What we do differentlyOn this tier every alteration is done by hand where the original was hand-finished. We open the lining by hand, re-cut the back panel by hand, and re-finish the lining by hand. Welt pockets are hand-cut with a single-needle. The back strap is re-set by hand at exactly the original height. Where the fabric is silk or fine tweed, we press through two layers of muslin and steam rather than press flat. On bespoke waistcoats, we tell clients upfront that the alteration will be slower than a mid-tier job — usually 4 to 6 working days — because the hand-finishing itself cannot be rushed.

If you're not sure which tier your waistcoat falls into, send a close-up photo of the inside lining and one of the back strap on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.
