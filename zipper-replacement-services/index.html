The most underestimated repair in Nairobi

Why zipper replacement is a fifty-minute job, not a ten-minute one

There are tailors in Nairobi who will replace a zip for KSh 300 in ten minutes. Some of those jobs hold. Most of them fail within six months — the zip sits crooked, the placket doesn't close cleanly, the teeth skip at the bend, or the slider loses grip because it's the wrong size for the teeth. Here's what a proper zipper replacement actually involves, and why it takes the time it takes.

The zip has to match the original in four ways, not one

Most people think a zip is a zip. It isn't. When we replace a zip, we match it to the original in four ways: type (metal, nylon coil or plastic moulded), gauge (the size of the teeth or coil — #3, #5, #8, #10, with heavier garments needing larger gauge), length (measured exactly — not approximated to the nearest standard size), and finish (antique brass, bright brass, gunmetal, black oxide or painted to match the original). On a leather jacket, we also match the colour of the zip tape to the lining, not just to the leather. Getting all four right is what makes a replacement invisible. Getting one wrong is what makes it look like a repair.

The old zip has to come out without damaging the placket

Cutting an old zip out is where most damage happens. On a good garment — a wool coat, a leather jacket, a canvas bag — the zip is set into a placket with a specific seam allowance. Cut too deep and you cut through the placket. Cut too shallow and the new zip doesn't sit flush. On lined garments, the lining has to be opened to access the zip seam without cutting through it — and on a hand-finished lining, re-closing it invisibly is its own job. On leather, this is even more delicate: leather shows every needle mark, and you cannot re-stitch into the same holes twice. On trousers, the fly has a two-panel construction with a specific alignment between the left and right sides — the replacement zip has to be installed so that both panels still meet correctly when the fly is closed.

The placket has to be re-cut and re-aligned

After the new zip is set in, the placket — the fabric flap that covers the zip on jackets and trousers — has to be re-cut to match the new zip's position and re-aligned so the button, snap or hook-bar still closes cleanly. On a jacket with a storm flap, the placket may be a two-layer construction: an outer flap plus an inner flap. Both have to be re-aligned against the new zip. On a trouser fly, the placket has a specific angle where it curves back into the crotch seam, and the new zip has to sit so that angle is preserved — otherwise the fly buckles when you sit.

The zip has to be tested in three positions, not just closed

A zip that runs smoothly when the garment is laid flat on the bench may still fail when it's worn. The three tests we do on every zip we replace are: closed — the zip has to close cleanly along its whole length without skipping or catching; half-open at the bend — on jackets and trousers, the zip has to run smoothly where the fabric bends, which is where cheap replacements usually fail; and open and re-close — the slider has to lock and unlock properly, and the pull has to sit flat against the placket when the garment is worn. A zip that passes all three is a zip that will still work in two years. A zip that only passes the first one is a zip that will be back for a re-do in six months.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 800 zip replacement on a leather jacket that's worth KSh 30,000 is a rescue — the jacket keeps its shape, the placket keeps its line, the new zip runs like the original. The same KSh 300 replacement by someone who doesn't check gauge, doesn't match the finish, and doesn't test the zip under tension is money wasted, because the jacket will come back to the wardrobe within a season. The clients who get the most out of zipper replacement are the ones who send photos of the failed zip and the whole garment, so we can quote against the original not just the zip. That's the same standard we hold on leather jacket alterations, trenchcoat alterations and jeans alterations.

Not every zip is the same job

Three zipper tiers — three completely different replacements

A zip on a KSh 2,500 market jacket and a zip on a KSh 40,000 leather jacket are the same component in name only. One can be replaced in twenty minutes with a standard coil zip. The other takes three times as long because leather shows every needle mark, the placket is a two-layer construction, and the zip tape has to match the original lining colour. Treating them the same way is how a good garment gets marked permanently. Here's how we categorise zips before deciding which technique to use.

Everyday tier

Casual jackets, market bags, poly-cotton trousers

Mass-produced casual jackets, market bags, basic poly-cotton trousers and lightweight luggage. Zip gauge usually #5 or #8 nylon coil. Placket is a single-layer construction. Zip tape is usually black or matched to the outer fabric. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 2,000 and KSh 8,000. The everyday zip.

What we do differentlyOn these we work pragmatically — the goal is a working zip, not a museum-grade restoration. We fit a standard YKK nylon coil zip of the correct gauge, re-set the placket, and test the zip. Standard press, standard stitch. Where the garment's zip was a specific brand we don't stock, we tell the client upfront that a YKK of the same gauge is the sensible match — no shame in it.

Everyday tier

Jeans, better chinos, work jackets, quality bags

Jeans with metal flies, better chinos from mid-tier retail, work jackets with #5 or #8 metal zips, quality leather goods and canvas bags. Placket construction is properly two-layer. Zips are usually branded — YKK, Riri, Lampo, IDEAL. Original finish matters: brass, antique brass, gunmetal or black oxide. Common in Nairobi between KSh 8,000 and KSh 25,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier we match the original in four ways — type, gauge, length and finish — and we tell the client if a true match exists or if we're substituting. On jeans metal flies we use YKK brass or antique brass of the correct gauge and re-cut the fly so both panels still meet at the correct angle. On work jackets with storm flaps, we re-align both flaps against the new zip. Every zip is tested closed, at the bend, and open-reclose before we call it done.

Premium tier

Leather jackets, designer outerwear, high-end bags

Leather jackets, designer trenchcoats, high-end Italian and British outerwear, premium leather bags and luggage. Zips are branded — Riri, Lampo, IDEAL, YKK Excella — and often proprietary to the maker. Leather shows every needle mark and cannot be re-stitched in the same holes twice. Placket construction is sometimes hand-finished. The zip tape colour is matched to the original lining, not the outer fabric.

What we do differentlyOn this tier, every stitch is set by hand where the original was hand-set. We source Riri and Lampo zips through specialist suppliers rather than substitute a YKK. On leather we use a single-needle walking-foot machine and every stitch is measured before it's made — no re-stitching. The zip tape colour is matched to the lining on a swatch. Where a proprietary zip can't be sourced, we tell the client honestly and quote the closest specialist match. Leather zipper replacements start at KSh 1,500 and can go higher depending on the jacket.

A short note from the bench

The four things that decide whether a zip replacement holds — and only one of them is the zip

In fourteen years of zipper replacement I have taken out about two thousand failed zips. Some of those were the original factory zips, worn out from years of use. But a surprising number were replacements — jobs done by another tailor in Nairobi six months or a year earlier that had already failed. When I open a garment to replace a failed replacement, I can usually tell within thirty seconds what went wrong. And it's almost never that the zip itself was bad. It's that one of four things was ignored when it was installed.

The first is the gauge. Every zip has a size — #3, #5, #8, #10 — and the size has to match the weight and thickness of the garment. A #5 zip on a heavy work jacket will fail because the teeth are too small to hold the tension of the fabric. A #10 zip on a lightweight poly jacket will look industrial and wrong even if it works. On trousers and jeans, the gauge matters even more — a fly zip on denim has to be heavy enough to keep the fly closed when the wearer sits down, which is exactly the moment the fabric pushes hardest. When a client sends me a photo of a failed zip, the first thing I look at is the gauge relative to the garment. Half the failed replacements I see are the wrong gauge.

The second is the placket alignment. The placket is the fabric flap that covers the zip on jackets and trousers, and it has a very specific position relative to the zip. When the placket is re-aligned after a replacement, it has to sit so the outer flap still covers the teeth and the inner flap still folds back into the seam. On a jacket with a storm flap, there are two flaps, and both have to be re-aligned against the new zip. Get the placket alignment wrong by even three millimetres and the coat looks crooked when it's closed — you can see the teeth showing, or the flap buckles outward. On trousers, the fly placket has a specific angle where it curves back into the crotch seam, and if the new zip is set so that angle is off, the fly buckles every time the wearer sits down. That's the most common reason a replacement fly zip fails within a year.

The third is the testing. Most tailors test a zip by closing it once on the bench and calling it done. That's about forty percent of the test. A zip has to be tested closed — teeth meeting all the way up, no skip, no catch. Then half-open at the bend — this is where the fabric flexes and where a badly-set zip will fail. On a jacket that's where the chest bends, on trousers that's where the fly curves into the crotch, on a bag that's where the bag folds when you carry it. And then open-reclose — the slider has to lock and unlock properly, and the pull has to sit flat when the garment is worn. A zip that only passes the closed test is a zip that will come back in six months. Every zip I install passes all three tests before the item leaves the bench.

The fourth — and this is the one clients never ask about — is the tape colour. A zip tape is the woven fabric strip that the teeth are mounted on. On a jacket, the tape is visible when the jacket is open, and on a bag the tape is visible from inside every time the bag is opened. The tape colour has to match the lining on the inside, not the outer fabric — because the tape sits against the lining when the garment is closed. A black tape on a beige-lined jacket is the fastest way to make a replacement look like a replacement. On leather jackets we match the tape to the lining on a swatch. On designer bags we match to the inner lining. On technical waterproof jackets, the tape is often the same colour as the membrane — and getting that wrong is the giveaway that the zip has been replaced.

Clients often ask me why a zip replacement costs KSh 800 or KSh 1,500 when another tailor in Nairobi will do it for KSh 300. The honest answer is that the KSh 300 job takes twenty minutes and the KSh 800 job takes forty-five. The extra twenty-five minutes are the gauge check, the placket re-alignment, the three-position test and the tape colour match. Those four things are the difference between a zip that works for six months and a zip that works for six years. On a KSh 2,500 market jacket, the KSh 300 job may be the right answer — the jacket isn't worth the extra. Make your zips last longer

Eight habits that keep zips working between replacements

A zip is the most mechanical component on any garment, bag or piece of luggage. It's the part designed to move, and it's the part that fails first. Most zip failures we see are preventable with a handful of small habits. Here are the eight we teach every client when they collect an item with a replaced zip.

1. Close the zip before you remove the garment

The single biggest cause of zip failure is removing the garment while the zip is still open. When you pull a jacket off with the zip open, the two sides of the placket get pulled in opposite directions, and the slider and teeth take all the strain. The slider deforms slightly, the teeth spread, and the zip starts to gape. Close the zip first, then take the garment off. This one habit roughly doubles the working life of the zip on a jacket, coat or trousers.

2. Never force a sticking zip

If a zip sticks, stop pulling. A zip that sticks and gets pulled hard will either tear the teeth apart or strip the slider. The correct approach is to back the slider off a centimetre, check for a thread or a piece of fabric caught in the teeth, and try again gently. If it still sticks in the same place, the teeth have probably been damaged at that point — bring it in, and we can repair that section for KSh 600 rather than replacing the whole zip for KSh 800 or more. The clients who force a sticking zip are the ones who end up with a full zip replacement a week later.

3. Clean the zip teeth occasionally

Zips collect dust, lint and tiny fibres in the teeth — especially on jackets and bags that see daily use. That debris slowly jams the slider and eventually spreads the teeth. A soft toothbrush, run along the teeth on both sides every few months, clears the debris and lets the slider run cleanly. On metal zips, a very light rub with a graphite pencil along the teeth keeps the slider gliding smoothly. Never use oil or grease on a zip — it attracts dust and makes the problem worse over time.

4. Check the zip pull regularly

The zip pull — the tab you hold to move the slider — is the smallest and most fragile part of the zip. It's also the part that fails first. Check it every few weeks — if the pull is starting to loosen from the slider, bring it in and we'll replace just the pull for KSh 400 before the pull comes off entirely. Once the pull has come off, you either have to move the slider with pliers (which damages it) or replace the slider (KSh 500). Catching the problem one step early saves you money and saves the slider.

5. Don't over-pack bags and luggage

The zip on a bag or suitcase is designed to hold a certain amount of tension. Over-packing pushes the teeth apart, and over time the slider can't close them properly. This is why suitcase zips fail so often at the corners and the top curve — those are the points where an over-packed bag exerts the most pressure. Pack your bag so the zip closes easily without having to sit on it. On a soft bag, if you can see the fabric bulging between the teeth when the zip is closed, it's over-packed.

6. Wash trousers and jeans with the fly zip closed

In a washing machine, an open fly zip on a pair of trousers or jeans gets battered against the drum and against the rest of the load. The slider deforms, teeth spread, and the fly zip fails within a year. Close the fly zip before the trousers go in the wash — every time. On a jacket with a separating zip, close the zip before washing as well. If you're washing something with a long zip — a coat or a sleeping bag — put it in a mesh laundry bag so the zip doesn't catch on the drum.

7. Don't hang a bag by the zip pull

Hanging a bag or backpack by the zip pull or the zip tape is a common habit that ruins zips quickly. The entire weight of the bag pulls against one point on the tape, and the tape stretches, the teeth spread, and the zip starts to gape at that point. Always hang a bag by the handle, the shoulder strap, or the designated hang loop. This one habit alone can add years to the life of a bag zip.

8. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose zip pull is a KSh 400 fix today. Left for a month, the pull comes off, the slider gets damaged trying to move it without a pull, and the whole zip has to be replaced at KSh 800 or more. A zip that sticks at one point is a KSh 600 teeth repair today. Left for three months, the teeth spread, the slider loses grip, and a full zip replacement becomes the only option. A zip that gapes at the top is a KSh 500 top-stop repair today. Left alone, the slider runs off the end, and again a full zip replacement is needed. The clients who spend the least on zip repairs are the ones who message us the first time they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less, and they preserve more of the original zip and garment.

Follow those eight habits and your zips will hold their function for years rather than months. And when a zip does eventually fail — the slider loses grip, the teeth spread, the tape frays — we're one WhatsApp message away.
