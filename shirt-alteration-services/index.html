The counter-intuitive truth about shirts

Why a shirt is one of the hardest garments to alter properly

Most people assume shirts are the easy job. The tailor who did your suit can certainly shorten a sleeve, right? They can — but a shirt and a suit jacket sit on completely different structural rules, and a shirt altered like a jacket will look wrong from the second wash. Here's why.

Shirts have no canvas to hide behind

A suit jacket has three layers of hidden structure — canvas, horsehair interfacing, wadding — that mask imperfections in the alteration. You can be a few millimetres off on a jacket seam and the canvas absorbs the error. A shirt has none of that. It's a single layer of fabric, and every seam is visible from both sides. If your side seam is 4 mm out, the shirt pulls to one side when you sit. If your sleeve head is stitched too tight, the shoulder puckers. There's nowhere to hide — which is exactly what makes shirt alteration a different craft from jacket work.

The collar carries the whole shirt

A shirt collar is the most photographed part of any men's garment — it's what people see when they look at you across a table. The collar has to hold its shape, sit flat against the neck and chest, and stay crisp through a full day's wear. Fixing a collar properly means either re-shaping the existing one (fine on soft collar constructions) or fully removing and rebuilding it (necessary on stiff interlined collars). The technique depends on the collar construction, not on your preference, and getting it wrong means the collar will curl within a month.

Sleeves carry the arm shape

Sleeve shortening on a shirt sounds simple. But on a well-made shirt, the sleeve isn't a tube — it's curved along the natural line of the arm, with a slight bend at the elbow. If you shorten from the cuff without preserving the curve, the sleeve will look straight and stiff. If you shorten from the shoulder, the armhole has to be reset exactly or the whole shirt twists. We choose the method based on the cuff construction, the sleeve curve and the fabric weight. Sleeve shortening from the cuff is KSh 1,000. From the shoulder is KSh 1,200. The price difference reflects the difficulty.

The placket has to line up perfectly

The front placket — the strip of fabric carrying the buttons — runs straight down the centre of the shirt. When you slim the body, the placket has to stay perfectly vertical, otherwise the shirt looks twisted even when you're standing still. On cheap shirts this is a matter of stitching straight. On quality shirts with a stitched placket edge or a French front, the stitching has to match the original pattern exactly, or the alteration shows from a metre away.

Body slimming changes the drape of everything

Slack a shirt too aggressively and the side seams pull the front placket out of line, the pocket alignment shifts, and the hem lifts at the sides. We slim from the side seams only — never the front — and taper the waist by no more than 4 cm in one go. If you want a more aggressive fit, we do it in two stages with a fitting between. This is the same principle we apply to chino slimming and it's why our alterations hold their shape after washing.

Why this matters to your wallet

We charge KSh 1,000 to slim a shirt. A tailor who doesn't know about placket alignment, sleeve curve and collar construction might charge KSh 500. Eight weeks later, when the placket twists, the collar curls and the sleeve puckers, you'll spend KSh 1,000 getting it fixed by someone who does know. Doing it right the first time is cheaper than doing it twice — the same principle we teach clients on our darning page and our polo shirt alterations page.

Read the label before you book

Three tiers of shirt — three different alteration jobs

A KSh 1,200 market shirt and a KSh 15,000 Italian cotton-linen shirt require completely different tailoring approaches. If your tailor treats both the same way, one of them is going to end up looking wrong. Here's how I categorise shirts before I decide what technique to use.

Entry tier

Everyday cotton shirts

Mass-produced cotton shirts — market stalls, mid-tier retailers, fast fashion. Fabric weight 100–130 gsm, usually poplin or plain weave. Pre-shrunk but often still shrinks slightly on the first wash. Standard fused collars, machine-stitched placket, no pocket or a single patch pocket. The most common shirt in Nairobi.

What we do differentlyFused collars on entry-tier shirts can't be re-shaped — the interlining is glued in place. We repair them by re-stitching the collar band rather than reshaping the collar point. Cheaper, faster, and the collar keeps its original line.

Mid tier

Premium cotton and oxford shirts

Uniqlo, Muji, Charles Tyrwhitt, mid-range Italian cotton, oxford weave and brushed twill. Fabric 120–160 gsm, sometimes with mother-of-pearl buttons and a two-piece collar construction. Cleaner placket stitching, better side seam allowances, longer staple cotton. The standard office shirt for Nairobi professionals.

What we do differentlyThe two-piece collar construction means we can reshape the collar without removing it — a cleaner, faster alteration. Placket stitching has to match the original pattern width to the millimetre, or the alteration shows.

Premium tier

Italian, Japanese and designer shirts

Cotton-linen blends, sea-island cotton, Giza cotton, hand-finished Italian shirts from brands like Borrelli, Barba, Finamore and similar. Fabric 140–180 gsm, often with hand-attached collars, hand-rolled hem, mother-of-pearl buttons and floating interlinings. Precise seam tolerances to within a millimetre.

What we do differentlyHand-attached collars on premium shirts have to be removed and re-attached by hand — machine stitching leaves visible marks. The hand-rolled hem on the shirt body requires hand-finishing during hem shortening, which takes roughly triple the time of a machine hem. A careless alteration on a KSh 15,000 Italian shirt is a KSh 15,000 mistake.

The five parts of a shirt that affect the alteration

What a shirt actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a shirt as "a shirt." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a shirt has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what I look at before I take scissors to a shirt.

01

The collar — fused, semi-fused or floating

Shirt collars come in three constructions. Fused collars have the interlining glued to the fabric — cheapest, fastest to make, hardest to reshape. Semi-fused collars have some stitching and some glue. Floating (or "soft") collars have the interlining stitched in place but not glued, which means they can be reshaped, re-tensioned, or fully rebuilt. The collar determines whether we repair it in place or replace it entirely. On entry-tier shirts, repair. On quality shirts, rebuild.

02

The cuffs — single button or double button

Shirts end with single-button cuffs (most common), double-button cuffs (formal) or convertible cuffs (can be closed with buttons or cufflinks). Sleeve shortening from the cuff is straightforward on single-button cuffs. Double-button cuffs require repositioning both buttons. Convertible cuffs need the buttonhole preserved at the exact position. If we shorten from the shoulder instead, we can preserve any cuff construction — but the shoulder technique takes longer and costs more.

03

The placket — single layer or two-piece

The front placket runs from the collar down to the hem, holding the buttons and buttonholes. On cheaper shirts, it's a single strip of fabric folded and stitched. On better shirts, it's a two-piece construction with an inner facing. When we slim the body, the placket has to stay perfectly vertical — which means the side seams have to be re-shaped symmetrically. If the placket twists after slimming, the whole shirt looks off, no matter how good the fit.

04

The side seams — with or without gussets

Shirts have a side seam running from the armpit to the hem, sometimes with a small gusset (reinforcement triangle) at the bottom corner. When we slim, we re-shape from the side seam inward. The gusset has to be preserved — losing it means the hem corner weakens and eventually tears. This is the same gusset-aware technique we use on cargo pant slimming. Small detail, big difference.

05

The hem — machine-rolled, hand-rolled or shirt-tail curve

Shirt hems come in three finishes. Machine-rolled hems are common on everyday shirts — fast, uniform, but visibly machine-made. Hand-rolled hems appear on premium shirts — slower, finer, and unmistakable when done well. Shirt-tail curves (the traditional long shirt shape that tucks in) require a hem that follows the curve exactly. When we shorten a shirt, we match the original hem finish — machine for machine, hand for hand. Doing otherwise would be visible on the finished garment.

If a tailor quotes you a shirt alteration without asking about any of those five things, they're guessing — and the result will show up after a few washes.

Every fabric demands its own approach

The five shirt fabrics I work with most often — and how each one behaves

Shirt fabric is where most alterations either succeed or fail. Each one presses, frays, and responds to a needle differently. Here's what I've learned across fourteen years, fabric by fabric.

Poplin and broadcloth

The classic business shirt fabric. Fine, tightly woven, with a slight sheen. Poplin takes a press beautifully and holds a crisp collar line for years — but it also shows every needle mark, because the weave is so tight. We use fine needles and match thread weight precisely. A poplin shirt shortened with the wrong needle shows pinpricks along the seam, and no amount of pressing removes them. If your poplin shirt is a business shirt you wear weekly, tell me when you message so I can choose the right tooling.

Oxford cloth

Oxford has a slightly rougher weave with a matte finish and better air movement — the classic American dress shirt fabric, and a Nairobi favourite. It's more forgiving than poplin to alter because the texture hides minor stitching imperfections. It also takes a stronger taper than poplin without puckering. We can slim an oxford shirt by up to 5 cm without needing a two-stage process, whereas we cap poplin at 4 cm. Small difference. Means a lot on the finished fit.

Cotton-linen blends

Linen gives a shirt breathability and a natural lived-in look — but it also frays aggressively at the cut edge. Sleeve shortening on a linen shirt requires an overlock finish before the hem is turned, otherwise the cut edge unravels within two washes. The sleeve curve on linen shirts is also more forgiving — linen drapes rather than holds shape, so a slightly imperfect sleeve reset is less visible. Nairobi's warm months call for a lot of these. We do them weekly.

Sea-island, Giza and Egyptian cotton

Premium long-staple cottons with a finer feel and a smoother surface than standard cotton. Sea-island and Giza cottons are the two grades used most often on Italian and Japanese shirts. Both take alterations extremely well — the fibres are strong enough that tapering doesn't stress them, and the weave is fine enough that we can achieve a very precise placket line. Sea-island cotton shirts often have hand-rolled hems, which means we do hem shortening by hand to match. That's the difference between KSh 1,200 and KSh 1,600 for the same job — the hand-finishing.

Flannel and brushed cotton

Flannel has a brushed surface that hides stitching imperfections but also holds nap direction. Cutting or pressing without respecting the nap leaves a permanent light-versus-dark patch where the alteration was made. We lay the fabric flat before cutting, mark the direction with chalk, and press only through a muslin cloth. Flannel alterations take roughly 30% longer than poplin for the same job. If you have a flannel shirt you love, tell me when you message so I bring the correct tooling to the bench.

Chambray and denim shirts

Chambray and lighter denim shirts use heavier thread and wider seam allowances than standard dress shirts — the construction is closer to a work shirt than to a business shirt. That makes them easier to slim without visible strain, because the fabric has more structure. On heavier chambray and denim, we use a walking-foot industrial machine for the alteration. Same principle as our jeans alterations. Fewer stitch imperfections, more durability over years.

If you don't know what your shirt is made of, send a close-up photo of the fabric on WhatsApp. I'll identify the weave from the texture and tell you honestly what's possible.

Before you book — does your shirt actually fit?

Seven checkpoints that tell you if a shirt needs altering

Most clients message saying "my shirt doesn't fit right." That's not enough for me to quote accurately, and it's not enough for you to decide what to ask for. Here's the checklist I use on every shirt in the workshop. Run through it with the shirt on. If three or more are off, the shirt is worth altering. If one or two, you may be able to live with it.

01

The collar sits flat, with one finger of slack

Button the top button and slide one finger between the collar and your neck. It should slide easily. If you can fit two fingers, the collar is too big and needs taking in. If no finger fits, the collar is too tight and the shirt will be uncomfortable all day. On fused collars we can take the collar band in by 6–8 mm, which is usually enough. On floating collars we can reshape further.

02

The shoulder seam lands at your shoulder bone

Stand side-on to a mirror. The shoulder seam — where sleeve meets body — should end exactly at the widest point of your shoulder. If it hangs past, the shirt is too big and needs the shoulder and armhole reset (a technical job, KSh 1,800–2,500). If it sits inside your shoulder line, the shirt is too small and cannot be fixed. Shoulder width is the single most important measurement on a shirt.

03

The chest lies smooth, with no pull at the top button

Button the shirt up and run your hand across your chest. The fabric should lie smooth with no X-shaped wrinkle at the top button. If you see an X, the shirt is too tight in the chest and every button will pull open by lunchtime. If you see loose fabric pooling at the sides, the chest is too wide. Chest alterations are always done from the side seams — never the front — because front work distorts the placket.

04

The sleeve ends at your wrist bone, showing 1 cm of cuff

With your arm relaxed at your side, the sleeve should end at your wrist bone — roughly 1 cm above the base of your thumb. If the sleeve ends at your knuckles, it's too long. If it stops above the wrist bone, it's too short and can't be lengthened more than 1 cm. This is the single most common alteration we do and the one that makes the biggest visible difference on a shirt worn without a jacket.

05

The waist pulls in without billowing at the back

Tuck the shirt in and turn to the side. The fabric should follow the curve of your lower back without bubbling out or pulling tight across the front. If the back billows like a sail, the shirt needs side-seam slimming. If the back is tight while the front is fine, the shirt needs a back-dart adjustment instead. We handle both. Slimming side seams is KSh 1,000. Adding back darts is KSh 800.

06

The hem tucks in without riding up when you sit

Sit down and raise your arms slightly. The hem should stay tucked without riding up to your waist. If it does, the shirt is too short and needs lengthening (rare on shirts) or you need a longer cut. If the hem sits low enough but bunches awkwardly at the front, the shirt is too long and needs shortening. Hem shortening with a machine-rolled finish is KSh 1,000. Hand-rolled finish is KSh 1,600.

07

The buttons sit centred on your body when closed

Button the shirt fully and stand in front of a mirror. The line of buttons should run straight down the middle of your chest, not drift to one side. If the buttons drift, the shirt has been cut or previously altered unevenly. We can correct small drifts by reshaping the placket, but major drift means the shirt has been cut and can't be fully corrected. On quality shirts with stitched placket edges, this is rarely a problem.

Run through all seven. If three or more feel wrong, send me a photo on WhatsApp and I'll tell you honestly which alterations will make the biggest difference. You don't have to fix everything at once — often the right answer is to fix one thing and live with the rest.

An honest decision — alter, or replace?

When a shirt is worth altering, and when you should just buy a new one

I turn down shirt jobs every month. Not because I can't do them — because the honest answer is that a KSh 1,500 alteration on a KSh 1,000 market shirt doesn't make sense. Here's the test I use before I quote you. If your shirt fails more than two of these, buy a new one.

✓

Was the shirt good quality to begin with?A KSh 1,000 market shirt has probably been washed more than a hundred times and the fibres are fatigued. Altering it might fit better for a few months, then the seams will start to fray at the shoulder. A KSh 4,000+ oxford or Italian cotton shirt is worth the alteration investment because the fabric will hold its new shape for years.

✓

Is the fabric still supple and in good condition?Run your thumb across the fabric at the front and the elbows. Does it feel soft and smooth, or has it gone thin and papery? A supple shirt takes alteration well. A fatigued shirt will start to pull at the side seams within a few washes. If the fabric has gone stiff or dusty-feeling, alteration will accelerate the damage rather than fix the fit.

✓

Is the collar still holding its shape?If the collar has gone soft and floppy, replacing it costs almost as much as the shirt did originally. If the collar is still crisp but the sleeves and body need work, alteration makes sense. If the collar has gone, the whole alteration investment rarely pays back — unless the shirt has sentimental value, which changes the calculation entirely.

✓

Will the alteration cost less than 35% of a new one?If a mid-tier oxford shirt costs KSh 3,500 new, an alteration should cost no more than KSh 1,200 to be worth doing. If it costs more, the numbers usually favour buying new — unless the shirt is a specific fit you can't easily replace. On Italian or designer shirts, the calculation shifts because the replacement cost is much higher.

✓

Is the fit fixable at all?Too big can be altered. Too small cannot — you can't create fabric where none exists. If the shirt is one size too small in the shoulders or chest, no tailor can save it. If it's one size too big, an alteration can make it fit perfectly. On a shirt, the shoulder measurement is the dealbreaker — everything else can usually be adjusted.

✓

Does the shirt have meaning beyond its price tag?A shirt your father wore, a wedding shirt, a shirt bought on a specific trip — the calculation changes entirely. On sentimental garments, we do the alteration no matter what the numbers say, because the value isn't in the fabric. Tell us when you message, and the quote is built around the meaning, not the price tag.

You won't get a hard sell when you message me. If the honest answer is "buy a new one," I'll tell you that straight. Better that than charging you for an alteration that stops looking right in six months.

A short note from the bench

Why fused collars are the hardest thing to fix on a shirt

When clients ask me which shirt alteration is the hardest, they expect the answer to be sleeve shortening from the shoulder. It isn't. Sleeve work is technical but predictable. The hardest single job on a shirt is the collar — and specifically, the fused collar that has started to bubble.

A fused collar has a layer of interlining glued to the fabric with a heat-activated adhesive. The glue holds the collar shape, keeps it crisp, and makes the collar assembly faster and cheaper than a floating collar. It works beautifully until it doesn't. Over time — through washing, ironing, and normal wear — the adhesive starts to release unevenly. The collar bubbles. The surface goes lumpy. You press it flat and it comes back lumpy the next wash.

You cannot fix a bubbling fused collar by ironing it. The adhesive that held it together has physically changed. You cannot re-glue it — the glue available to a tailor's iron is weaker than what the factory used, and it will bubble again within weeks. The only permanent fix is to open the collar, remove the old interlining entirely, replace it with a fresh interlining, and re-stitch the collar band. That's a two-hour job on the bench and it costs from KSh 1,500.

The reason this matters is that most Nairobi tailors quote a collar repair at KSh 400 to KSh 600 and either over-press the collar (which bubbles again in two months) or replace the whole collar with non-matching interlining (which shows a different sheen from any angle). By the time you bring the shirt to us to fix, we're doing a repair on a repair. It's cheaper to have it done once, properly.

If you own a shirt where the collar has gone bubbly or floppy and you're not sure whether it's worth fixing — send me a photo. I'll tell you straight whether a collar replacement is worth the investment, or whether the shirt should be retired. That's the honest answer you deserve.

Make them last between alterations

Eight habits that keep shirts out of the alteration pile

Shirts take the hardest beating of any garment in your wardrobe — worn weekly, washed weekly, pressed weekly. That cycle is what ages them fastest. Here are the eight habits I teach every client when they collect an altered shirt. The same ones I follow with my own.

1. Wash cold, wash inside out

Shirts fade and thin from the outside in, so turning them inside out protects the visible surface. Cold water preserves the colour depth and prevents cotton from shrinking harder than it should. Skip fabric softener — it leaves a residue that dulls the colour and shortens the fibre's life. Wash with similar colours; on white shirts, use a dedicated white wash to avoid grey shading.

2. Air dry on a hanger, never tumble

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shrink a shirt's collar, cuffs and body length. Hang shirts damp on a wide-shouldered hanger and let them air-dry. If you're in a hurry, point a fan at them. This is also the moment to press out the sleeves and collar by hand while the fabric is still slightly damp — ironing a wrinkled dry shirt always takes longer than ironing it damp.

3. Iron inside out, collar first

Iron shirts from the inside out — always. Direct heat on the outside shines the fabric, especially on poplin and sea-island cotton. Press the collar first while it's flat, then the cuffs, then the sleeves, then the body. On quality shirts with a fused collar, use a slightly lower heat so you don't re-activate the adhesive. On hand-rolled hems, avoid pressing the hem itself — you'll flatten the roll.

4. Give the fabric 24 hours between wears

Cotton needs 24 hours to recover its shape and dry out fully from body moisture. Wear the same shirt two days in a row and the collar, cuffs and side seams stay stretched out. Own four or five shirts you rotate — the fabric breathes better and the shirt looks fresher for years longer. This single habit alone halves the number of collar repairs we do on any given client.

5. Button the shirt before washing

Shirts with unbuttoned plackets twist in the wash and put stress on the buttonholes. Button all the way up before washing — the placket stays flat, the buttonholes stay intact, and you won't find yourself needing a placket rebuild six months early. If the shirt has collar stays, remove them before washing and store them somewhere safe; losing a collar stay is the small beginning of a big collar repair.

6. Deal with small issues the week you notice them

A loose button, a fraying cuff edge, a small pull in the collar — these are cheap and easy to fix when fresh. Left for months, they become structural problems. A loose button is KSh 300 today. A rebuilt placket is KSh 800 tomorrow. A stretched collar that could have been saved with a KSh 500 re-tension becomes a KSh 1,500 collar replacement. The clients who spend the least on alterations are the ones who message us first when they notice something.

7. Store on hangers, not folded

Folding a shirt creates creases across the chest and sleeves that eventually become permanent — especially on cotton with a stiff finish. Hang shirts on wide-shouldered hangers with space between them, so air can circulate. If you're storing long-term, use a breathable cotton garment bag rather than plastic. Never stack shirts in a pile more than three deep — the ones at the bottom take on the folds of the ones above.

8. Keep one shirt out of the daily rotation

If you have a shirt you wear only for special occasions — a wedding guest shirt, a travel shirt, an interview shirt — keep it out of the daily cycle. Wash it less often, air it more. A shirt that's washed once a month instead of once a week lasts five times longer. The fabric stays supple and the collar stays crisp. When you do wear it, it looks like it just came from the shop.

Follow those eight habits and your shirts will hold their fit and finish for years. And when they do eventually need an alteration, we're one WhatsApp message away.
