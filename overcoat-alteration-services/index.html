The heaviest cloth in menswear

Why overcoat alterations are the hardest outerwear work a tailor does — and why most Nairobi tailors quietly refuse them

An overcoat is a different animal from a suit jacket. The cloth weighs twice as much. The construction has more layers — outer cloth, canvas, padding, lining — and every layer has to survive the alteration. The pressing has to be softer, the stitching has to be slower, and the hem line has to be measured over the shoes the client actually wears. Most Nairobi tailors will take in a suit jacket without blinking but will quietly turn down an overcoat, because a mistake on an overcoat is expensive and visible. Here's what we actually do when a heavy coat lands on the bench.

Overcoat cloth weighs two to four times what a suit jacket cloth weighs

A good overcoat is cut from cloth that weighs between 400 and 700 gsm — Huddersfield wool at 550 gsm, cashmere at 480 gsm, loden at 620 gsm, camel hair at 500 gsm. A suit jacket is cut from 240 to 320 gsm. That's twice the weight, sometimes three times. Heavy cloth behaves differently under the needle. It pulls on the machine. It resists being pressed flat. It hangs from the shoulder in a different way, and the drape changes as soon as you shorten the hem by even two centimetres. Every overcoat alteration we do starts with the client wearing the coat — not standing on a hanger — because the weight of the coat changes the hem line by up to three centimetres when it's on the body. A hem measured on a hanger is a hem measured wrong. Book wool overcoat hem shortening with free home collection and M-Pesa on collection Nairobi — we measure on you, not on a hanger.

The hem has to be set over your shoes, not over your socks

Overcoat length is not a fixed measurement — it depends on the shoes the client wears with the coat. A coat that sits at the knee over a pair of thin leather Oxfords sits lower over a pair of chunky boots. A coat that hits mid-calf over a pair of loafers may drag over a pair of sneakers. When we shorten an overcoat hem, we ask the client to bring the shoes they'll wear with the coat, and we set the hem over those shoes. We also ask what the client wants the coat to do — sit above the knee for a modern look, at the knee for a classic look, or below the knee for a traditional look. Every one of those choices changes the final length by two to four centimetres, and every one of them is decided before we cut. Pressing an overcoat is where most tailors ruin it

Heavy wool shows a hot press. The shine that appears on a cheaply pressed overcoat never comes out — it's not dirt, it's the wool fibre flattened by heat, and once it's flattened it stays flattened for the life of the coat. Every overcoat we alter is pressed through two layers of muslin cloth at medium heat, and the press lines are steamed rather than pressed flat. On cashmere and camel hair we press through three layers of muslin, because these fibres flatten faster than pure wool. On loden — a densely felted Austrian cloth that most Nairobi tailors have never worked with — the rule is dry-press only at low heat, because loden absorbs moisture and swells under a wet press. The pressing is what separates a proper overcoat alteration from a job that looks good on the bench and looks ruined by the time it reaches the wardrobe. The lining and the outer cloth age on different schedules

An overcoat has a full lining that runs from the shoulders to the hem, usually a bemberg or a satin or a quilted satin on heavier winter coats. The lining fails first — it takes the friction of the arms, the seat and the hem. By the time the outer wool shows wear, the lining has often already been patched once. When we alter an overcoat, we always check the lining state before we quote. If the lining is starting to fail, we quote the re-lining as a separate job — because the alteration and the re-lining can be done in one visit and the cost is lower than doing them separately. On a Burberry, an Aquascutum or a good Italian overcoat, re-lining is the single most common restoration job we do. See our re-lining services for the full detail on how that works.

Shoulder work on an overcoat is a two-person job

Overcoat shoulders are wider and heavier than suit jacket shoulders, and the sleeve head has to be re-set by hand — one person holds the sleeve head in position while the other stitches it. On a suit jacket this is a solo job; on an overcoat it needs a second pair of hands, and the amount of hand-finishing is roughly double. When we narrow the shoulders on an overcoat, we also re-set the sleeve length because narrowing the shoulder changes where the sleeve attaches, and the sleeve head has to be re-cut to match. Not every overcoat is the same job

Three overcoat tiers — three completely different alterations

A KSh 8,000 market overcoat and a KSh 150,000 cashmere overcoat look similar at a distance. They are not the same garment. One is a simple cotton-poly shell with a light lining and no canvas. The other is a hand-canvassed, hand-finished garment with padded lapels, a hand-set collar and a full bemberg lining — and every stitch on it was made by hand. Treating them the same way is how a good coat gets ruined. Here's how we categorise overcoats before deciding which technique to use.

Make your overcoat last fifteen years

Eight habits that keep an overcoat looking new for a decade and beyond

An overcoat is the most expensive garment in most wardrobes and the one that rewards care the most. A good wool overcoat, treated properly, lasts fifteen years. A cashmere overcoat lasts twenty. Most of what wears an overcoat out early is preventable, and every habit below is something we teach clients when they collect an altered coat. None of them takes more than five minutes.

1. Hang on a wide wooden hanger, always

An overcoat should be hung on a wide wooden hanger that supports the full width of the shoulders. This is not optional. Heavy cloth pulled out of shape by a thin wire hanger will distort the shoulder seam within a season, and once the shoulder seam distorts on a heavy coat, re-setting it costs more than a hem shortening. A wide wooden hanger costs KSh 500 and protects a garment worth a hundred times that. When we deliver an altered overcoat, we bring it back on a proper hanger, and we ask clients to transfer it to their own wide hanger before they do anything else. Never hang an overcoat on a doorknob, over the back of a chair, or on a radiator. The weight of the coat will pull the collar out of shape within a week.

2. Brush after every wear, two minutes only

A soft clothes brush, run down the shoulders, the lapels, the sleeves and the back of the coat after every wear, keeps dust and lint out of the wool. Embedded dust dulls the colour and speeds up wear at friction points. Brush downward, following the direction of the nap, not against it. This is the single most important habit for keeping an overcoat looking new, and it takes two minutes. On a heavy wool coat worn twice a week, that is four minutes a week. Clients who brush their coat every week and dry clean once a year will get fifteen years out of a good coat. Clients who never brush and dry clean three times a year will get six.

3. Dry clean sparingly, once or twice a year maximum

Every dry clean strips a little more of the natural lanolin out of the wool, which makes the fibres brittle and shortens the life of the coat. On a wool overcoat worn regularly in winter, one dry clean a year is usually enough. On a cashmere overcoat worn a few times a season, one dry clean every eighteen months is plenty. Between dry cleans, brush and air the coat on a wide hanger for a few hours. Spot clean obvious marks with a damp cloth and let the coat air dry. Never put an overcoat in a washing machine, never have it cleaned by a service that uses a hot press directly on the fabric, and never let a dry cleaner press the coat without a muslin cloth between the iron and the fabric. The shine that appears on a badly pressed coat never fully comes out. When you do dry clean, ask the cleaner to press through a cloth and never directly on the fabric.

4. Store in a breathable garment bag, never plastic

When the coat is not being worn for a season, store it in a cotton or canvas garment bag, never plastic. Plastic traps moisture against the wool, which creates the environment mildew loves, and mildew on a wool overcoat shows up as small dark spots that are difficult to remove without damaging the fabric. A cotton garment bag costs KSh 1,500 and protects a coat worth forty times that. Add a cedar block or lavender sachet to each pocket before storing. Cedar is more effective for long-term storage, lavender smells better when you open the bag. If you live in a humid area, add a silica gel packet to the garment bag and replace it every two months.

5. Never press an overcoat at home

The single biggest mistake clients make with an overcoat is pressing it at home with a hot iron. A hot iron flattens the wool and creates a permanent shine down the front panel that no amount of steaming will remove. It also flattens the canvas in the chest and the padding in the lapel, which changes the drape of the whole coat. If the coat needs a press between dry cleans, take it to us or to a proper tailor. We use a steam press at the correct temperature for the fabric, we press through muslin, and we know where the canvas sits inside the garment. On a wool overcoat, an over-enthusiastic home press can undo years of quality in twenty minutes. If you want to refresh the coat at home, use a steamer held six to eight inches away from the fabric. Steam lifts the surface wrinkles without touching the canvas.

6. Rotate between coats, do not wear one every day

Wool is a living fibre. After a day of wear, the fibres need 24 hours to relax back into shape. If you wear the same overcoat every day, the fibres never get to reset, and the coat starts to look tired within a single season. The shoulders lose their crisp line, the elbows crease, the hem softens. Rotate between two overcoats through the cool season. If you only own one coat, hang it in a well-ventilated spot overnight after every wear and let it breathe before you wear it again. If you live in a cold climate and own two coats, rotate them weekly. This roughly doubles the working life of each coat.

7. Empty the pockets and check the lining once a month

Heavy objects in the front or back pockets pull the coat out of shape over time. Empty the pockets when you hang the coat at the end of the day. Once a month, turn the coat inside out and check the lining at the armpits, the seat and the hem, because those are the three points where an overcoat lining fails first. If you see a small tear at the armpit, get it patched for KSh 1,500 today. Left for six months, the tear spreads, and the job becomes a full lining replacement at KSh 5,500. The same goes for the outer wool at the elbows and the back panel. Small patches today, no full restyle tomorrow. 8. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose button is a KSh 1,500 full-set replacement today. Left for a month, the button comes off and the buttonhole takes the strain, and re-cutting a hand-worked buttonhole is a specialist job that is not always possible. A hem starting to fray is a KSh 3,500 hem re-set today. Left for a year, the hem frays up into the body of the coat and the job becomes a KSh 5,000 hem rebuild with fabric reinforcement. A lining starting to wear at the armpit is a KSh 1,500 patch today. Left alone, the failure spreads and the job becomes a KSh 5,500 full lining replacement. A zipper or button on a technical storm coat that has stopped working is a KSh 1,500 fix today. Left alone, the whole closure system has to be re-built. The clients who spend the least on overcoat maintenance are the ones who message us the first time they notice something small. Extra habit, store the coat dry and clean

One more habit that is not about the coat itself but about the long-term storage. Before you put an overcoat into seasonal storage, make sure it is clean and dry. A coat stored with a small stain or a little moisture in the wool will develop mildew over six months, and by the time you take it out again, the stain has set and the mildew has spread. Send the coat for one dry clean before it goes into storage, let it air for a day on a wide hanger, then put it into a breathable bag with a cedar block. When you take it out of storage six months later, brush it down, air it for a few hours, and press it through muslin if it needs it. The cloth decides the technique

Every overcoat fabric behaves differently under the needle, and the alteration changes with the fabric

Most tailoring advice treats overcoats as one category of garment. They are not. A 550 gsm Huddersfield wool overcoat and a 480 gsm cashmere overcoat are not the same job, even when they are cut to the same pattern. The needle, the tension, the press, the thread, and the amount of hand-finishing all change with the cloth. Here is how we handle every overcoat fabric we work with, and why each one needs a different approach.

Pure wool overcoats, the standard reference point

Wool is the reference point for overcoats. It is what most British and Italian overcoat makers cut from, and it is the cloth that behaves most predictably under the needle. A good pure wool overcoat weighs between 450 and 600 gsm, has a tight twill or plain weave, and takes a press beautifully at medium heat through muslin. When we alter a pure wool overcoat, we work at medium tension with a standard needle, press through two layers of muslin, and steam the press lines rather than pressing them flat. The wool fibre has natural memory, so a wool overcoat that is pressed correctly will hold its new line for years. The single biggest risk with wool is over-pressing at too high a heat, which creates a shine that never comes out. Every wool overcoat we alter is checked under daylight at three points before we call the job done. The same approach runs through our suit alterations, where the same wool fibre behaves the same way.

Cashmere overcoats, the most delicate cloth in the workshop

Cashmere is the most delicate cloth we work with on outerwear. It has shorter fibres than wool, it flattens under heat faster than any other cloth, and it stretches in ways that wool does not. A good cashmere overcoat weighs between 380 and 480 gsm, and the fibre is finer than wool by roughly half. When we alter a cashmere overcoat, we work at very low tension with fine needles, we press through three layers of muslin at low heat, and we do not use steam at any point, because steam swells the cashmere fibre and can leave a permanent ripple in the cloth. After the alteration, we rest the coat for 24 hours before we call the job done, because cashmere needs time to settle and a coat that looks right on the bench will drift by the time it reaches the wardrobe. Cashmere shoulder work is the single most delicate job we do on outerwear, and we tell clients upfront that rush service is not available on cashmere for this reason.

Camel hair overcoats, warmer, heavier and more forgiving than cashmere

Camel hair overcoats sit between wool and cashmere in both weight and behaviour. A good camel hair overcoat weighs between 450 and 550 gsm, and the fibre has a natural warmth that wool and cashmere do not quite match. Camel hair is slightly more forgiving than cashmere under the needle, because the fibres are longer and the cloth has more structure. But camel hair has its own quirk: the fibre has a natural lustre that shows every needle mark under certain lighting, and a poorly pressed camel hair overcoat will shine worse than a wool one. When we alter a camel hair overcoat, we work at medium-low tension, press through three layers of muslin at low heat, and check the surface of the coat at an angle under daylight before the coat leaves the bench. Camel hair work takes 4 to 6 working days because of the extra checking.

Loden overcoats, the Austrian cloth most tailors have never worked with

Loden is a densely felted Austrian cloth that most Nairobi tailors have never worked with, and it is one of the fabrics that most needs a specialist hand. Loden is made by a process called milling, where the cloth is shrunk and felted until the fibres lock together into a solid surface. It is water-resistant without a coating, wind-resistant without a lining, and warmer than wool of the same weight. A good loden overcoat weighs between 550 and 700 gsm. The rules for altering loden are strict. Dry press only, at low heat, with no steam at any point, because loden absorbs moisture and swells under a wet press. Low tension with fine needles, because stitching through dense loden at high tension creates permanent needle marks. And where the coat has a traditional Austrian cut with a full lining, the lining has to be opened and closed by hand. Loden work takes 4 to 6 working days and we tell clients upfront that loden is one of the fabrics we charge more for, because the technique is more careful.

Tweed and herringbone overcoats, textured cloth, careful handling

Tweed and herringbone overcoats are cut from textured wool with a visible pattern, and the pattern itself is the challenge. A herringbone weave has a direction, and every seam on the coat has to preserve the direction through the cut. When we alter a herringbone overcoat, we mark the direction of the weave on every panel before we cut anything, and we re-check the alignment three times before closing the seam. On a tweed overcoat with a check or a plaid, the same rule applies, but the pattern match has to be preserved at the hem, the sleeve head, and the side seams all at once. Tweed and herringbone take 4 to 6 working days because of the extra pattern work. The single most common mistake we fix on tweed overcoats is a hem that was shortened without preserving the pattern at the new hem line, which leaves the bottom of the coat reading visibly wrong even though the length is technically correct.

Technical storm cloth overcoats, the modern rain-and-wind coats

Technical storm overcoats are the modern rain-and-wind coats, cut from coated nylon, bonded polyester or a laminated technical cloth with a waterproof membrane. These are the most sensitive overcoats to needle and heat of any fabric we work with. A technical storm coat cannot be pressed with steam at all, because the coating is a plastic film that will bubble and lift under heat. It cannot be stitched with a standard needle, because the needle leaves a visible hole in the coating that water can get through. When we alter a technical storm overcoat, we work at the lowest possible tension with the finest available needle, we dry press only, and where the coat has bonded seams we re-stitch with a hidden seam rather than trying to re-bond on the bench. Technical storm overcoat alterations start at KSh 4,500, and we tell clients upfront that on some technical coats we recommend against the alteration entirely, because the new seam will compromise the water resistance at that point.

Vintage cloth, mixed blends and overcoats older than twenty years

Vintage overcoats, mixed wool-blend coats, and overcoats older than twenty years are their own category. The cloth on these coats may be weaker than when it was new, either because the fibres have aged or because a previous owner pressed it too hot. When we alter a vintage overcoat, we always check the cloth strength first by hand, feeling the surface for signs of thinning at the elbows, the seat and the shoulders. If the cloth is strong, we proceed with the standard technique for that fibre. If the cloth is weakening, we adjust the tension and often the amount of hand-finishing to spread the stress more evenly. Vintage coat restoration is one of the most satisfying jobs we do, because a twenty-year-old overcoat that is altered properly can last another twenty. A short note from the bench

The four things that decide whether an overcoat alteration will hold for ten years

In fourteen years of tailoring I have altered over four hundred overcoats. Every one of them was a different job. Not because the technique changes, but because the cloth does. A wool overcoat behaves differently from a cashmere overcoat. A loden coat behaves differently from a tweed coat. A technical storm coat behaves differently from all of them. And yet most of the alterations we fix each month were done by someone who treated every overcoat the same way, with the same needle, the same tension, and the same press. That is why an overcoat that should have lasted twenty years comes back looking tired after three. Here are the four things I check on every overcoat before I pick up a needle.

The first thing is the cloth strength at the point of alteration. When I shorten a hem, I check the cloth at the hem line first, because that is where the coat has been dragging on the ground, on car seats, on the pavement. If the cloth is thin at the hem line, cutting it and re-setting it will make the failure worse, because the new hem edge will fray faster than the old one. In that case I re-cut the hem higher, above the weakened area, or I reinforce the hem edge from inside before I close it. On a coat where the cloth has failed across the shoulders or the seat, I tell the client upfront that the alteration will not hold, and I recommend against it. Fourteen years of tailoring has taught me that the strongest thing a tailor can say is sometimes no, and it is the reason clients trust the rest of what I do.

The second thing is the sleeve head on shoulder work. Shoulder reduction on an overcoat is a two-person job. One person holds the sleeve head in position while the other stitches it. On a suit jacket, the sleeve head can be re-set by one person because the cloth is lighter and the shoulder is smaller. On an overcoat, the sleeve head is wider, the cloth is heavier, and the sleeve itself has more weight, so the sleeve head has to be held in position with two hands while the stitch is set. When I was at Manix Clothing Store and later at Italian Men's Wear, I learned to do this solo by using a dress form to hold the coat in position, but even now, on a heavy wool overcoat, I call in my colleague to hold the sleeve head while I stitch. It is slower, and it is why shoulder reduction on an overcoat costs more than the same job on a suit jacket, but it is the only way the sleeve head sits correctly on the finished coat.

The third thing is the press. The press is the single biggest difference between a coat that looks right on the bench and a coat that looks right for the next ten years. Overcoat cloth, especially heavy wool, shows a hot press immediately. The shine that appears from a hot iron is not dirt, and it does not wash out. It is the wool fibre flattened by heat, and once it is flattened, it stays that way. Every overcoat I press is pressed through two layers of muslin at medium heat, and every press line is steamed rather than pressed flat. On cashmere and camel hair, I use three layers of muslin and lower heat. On loden, I dry press only at low heat, because loden swells under steam. On technical storm cloth, I dry press only at the lowest heat the cloth will tolerate, and where the coating is too sensitive, I do not press the coat at all, I let it hang. This is the detail that clients do not see, but it is the reason a coat that leaves my bench still has its surface ten years later.

The fourth thing is the lining. The lining is what the client sees when they open the coat, and it is what fails first. When I alter an overcoat, I always check the lining at the armpits, the seat and the hem, because those are the three points where a lining fails. On a good overcoat, the lining lasts eight to twelve years, and the outer wool lasts twenty. That means there is almost always a lining job coming, either now or within two or three years. When I find lining wear at the armpits, I quote a lining patch as a separate job, because the alteration and the patch can be done in one visit and the cost is lower. On a Burberry, an Aquascutum or a good Italian overcoat, the lining is often the only thing between the coat and retirement, and a full lining replacement at KSh 5,500 is what keeps a KSh 100,000 coat in rotation for another decade.

If I were to add a fifth thing, it would be the button row on sleeve shortening. On an overcoat with four buttons on the cuff, the distance between the buttons and the cuff edge is fixed by the original maker. When I shorten a sleeve, I remove the buttons, cut the sleeve, and re-attach the buttons at exactly the same distance from the new cuff edge. Not half a centimetre closer, not half a centimetre further. This is the single most common failure I see on overcoats altered by other tailors, and it is the detail a client notices without knowing why. A sleeve that has been shortened and re-buttoned with the buttons at the wrong distance reads as wrong on the coat, even if everything else about the alteration is correct. It is a small thing, and it is a thing I check twice on every overcoat sleeve I shorten.

How we approach overcoats across every tier, from market coats to hand-finished designer pieces

Some tailoring shops only work on high-end coats and quietly send budget clients away. Other shops only work on mid-tier coats and refuse to touch a Burberry because the work is too slow. We do not. Budget overcoats, straightforward work at a fair price

A budget overcoat, cut from cotton-poly or a lightweight wool blend and priced between KSh 5,000 and KSh 12,000, is not a coat we try to turn into a Burberry. It is a coat we make fit. When a client brings us a budget overcoat for a hem shortening, we check the cloth strength first, because cheap cotton-poly can be weak at the hem after a couple of seasons. If the cloth is strong, we shorten the hem with a standard machine stitch, press it through muslin at medium heat, and return it within 3 to 5 working days. If the cloth is weak, we reinforce the new hem from inside so the shortened edge does not fray. Either way, the price is between KSh 3,000 and KSh 3,500, and we tell the client upfront that a budget overcoat is worth a straightforward alteration, not a full restoration. There is a right price for every coat, and we do not pretend a KSh 8,000 overcoat is worth a KSh 12,000 restyle.

Mid-tier wool overcoats, careful work with a long timeline

A mid-tier wool overcoat, cut from 400 to 500 gsm wool or wool-cashmere and priced between KSh 15,000 and KSh 60,000, is where our standard overcoat service lives. Every overcoat that comes to our workshop gets the same four checks, regardless of tier. First, we check the cloth strength at the point of alteration. Can you shorten an overcoat without ruining the drape?
Yes, and preserving the drape is the whole point of how we work. Overcoat cloth is heavy, and heavy cloth hangs from the shoulder in a specific way that changes as soon as you shorten the hem. Every overcoat hem we shorten is measured on the client wearing the coat and wearing the shoes they will wear with the coat. We do not measure on a hanger. We cut the new hem line, re-set the hem allowance, re-anchor the lining hem so it sits two to four centimetres above the outer hem, and press through two layers of muslin at medium heat. Hem shortening on a standard wool overcoat is KSh 3,500 and takes 3 to 5 working days.
Can you shorten overcoat sleeves and keep the buttons in the right place?
Yes. Sleeve shortening with the button row preserved is KSh 3,000 and is the standard job on any overcoat with four buttons on the cuff. We remove the buttons first, shorten the sleeve from the cuff end, and re-attach the buttons at exactly the same distance from the new cuff edge as the original. Not half a centimetre closer, not half a centimetre further. This distance is the single most common failure we fix on overcoats altered by other tailors, because a button row at the wrong distance reads wrong on the whole coat even when everything else about the alteration is correct. Do you alter overcoats with a permanent shine on the wool?
Sometimes. A permanent shine on wool is not dirt, it is the wool fibre flattened by a previous hot press, and once it is flattened it stays that way.
