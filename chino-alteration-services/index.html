Read the label before you book

Not every chino is made the same — and the alteration should match

A KSh 2,000 cotton chino from a market stall and a KSh 12,000 Italian cotton-linen chino require very different tailoring approaches. If your tailor treats both the same way, one of them is going to end up looking wrong. Here's how I categorise chinos before I decide what technique to use.

Entry tier

Everyday cotton chinos

Mass-produced cotton twill chinos, usually from market stalls, mid-tier retailers or fast fashion. Fabric weight 200–280 gsm. Pre-shrunk and often washed. Colour tends to fade over the first year. Standard flat seams, sometimes a synthetic lining at the waistband. The most common chinos in Nairobi.

What we do differentlyWe cut a slightly larger seam allowance on hem and slimming work because these fabrics continue to shrink 1–2% over the first two years. A hem cut too short will be a problem in eighteen months.

Mid tier

Premium cotton and cotton-blend chinos

Uniqlo, Muji, Massimo Dutti, or mid-range Italian cotton. Fabric weight 280–340 gsm, sometimes with 1–2% elastane for stretch. Cleaner seam construction, better pocket bags, slight colour depth. Often with a small internal waistband reinforcement strip. A very common tier among Nairobi professionals.

What we do differentlyThe elastane blend means the fabric pulls back after cutting. We cut with a small allowance and use a slightly looser stitch tension so the fabric still moves. Doing this on a nylon-thread machine at a normal tension is how most tailors buckle the hem.

Premium tier

Italian, Japanese and designer chinos

Cotton-linen, brushed cotton, moleskin, cotton-silk blends from Incotex, PT Torino, Boggi, Slowear or similar. Fabric weight often 300–380 gsm. Long-staple cotton, sometimes hand-finished. Precise seam tolerances to within a millimetre. Visible construction detail — flat-felled seams, reinforced crotch, hidden inner pockets.

What we do differentlyOn this tier, the alteration has to be invisible. We preserve the original hem wherever possible, match thread colour and stitch length precisely, and never cut through the crotch reinforcement. A careless alteration on a KSh 15,000 chino is a KSh 15,000 mistake.

If you're not sure which tier your chinos fall into, send a photo of the label and the inside of the waistband on WhatsApp. I'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five parts of a chino that affect the alteration

What a chino actually is — layer by layer

Chinos are usually described as "casual trousers," which doesn't tell you anything useful. Structurally, a chino has five parts, and each one changes how the alteration should be done. Here's what I look at before I take scissors to a pair.

01

The fabric weave — twill, canvas or ripstop

Almost all chinos are cut from cotton twill, which is a diagonal weave that gives the fabric its characteristic diagonal lines. Some higher-end chinos use canvas (a plain, tighter weave) or ripstop (a grid-reinforced weave for heavier use). Twill takes a press beautifully; canvas holds structure; ripstop resists tearing. The weave determines what stitch length and needle you use — press twill too hard and it gets shiny, force a needle through ripstop and it tears along the grid lines.

02

The waistband and its reinforcement

Chino waistbands are usually 4 cm wide, sometimes with a hidden elastic section at the sides, and always with an internal reinforcement strip. That strip is what stops the waistband from stretching out of shape over time. When we take in a waist, we have to preserve or re-anchor the strip — if we just cut and sew without it, the waistband will sag within a few months.

03

The crotch construction — V-joint or flat seam

This is where the front and back legs meet, and it's the most fragile area on any trouser. Chinos usually use a "V-joint" — a reinforced stitch pattern that distributes stress across multiple directions. When the V-joint fails (it happens from squatting, cycling or just normal wear), the trousers sag at the crotch. Repairing a V-joint is a specific skill. Adjusting the crotch depth for a better fit is a different skill. We do both.

04

The hem finish — flat, overlock, or cuff

Chino hems come in three main finishes. Machine straight is the most common — a single needle straight seam that folds the raw edge inside. Overlock (also called OB) is a wrapped edge finish, usually on cheaper chinos. Turn-up cuff (also called a rolled hem or "turn-up") is a folded finish where the hem is stitched visibly — common on formal chinos. Each finish has a different alteration approach. We preserve the original finish wherever possible.

05

The rise — where the waistband sits relative to the crotch

Rise is measured from the crotch seam to the top of the waistband. Most chinos come in a mid-rise (rise 26–29 cm) or low-rise (rise 23–26 cm). If chinos feel like they're sliding down, or the crotch is sitting too low, the fix is usually adjusting the rise — not tightening the belt. Reducing the rise by 1–2 cm lifts the whole trouser without changing the waist measurement. It's a common Nairobi request and one most tailors avoid. We do it regularly.

Every quote I send is based on these five factors. If a tailor quotes you without asking about any of them, they're guessing — and the result will show it in six months.

Fabric decides the technique

How the chino fabric changes the job

Two chinos can look identical on the shelf and behave completely differently under a needle. Here's my honest breakdown by fabric type — the same one I'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

100% cotton twill

The classic chino fabric. It presses beautifully, holds a crease, and takes any alteration without complaint. The only catch is shrinkage — untreated cotton twill shrinks 1–2% on the first wash and continues to shrink slightly over the first couple of years. When we hem chinos in this category, we always cut with a small allowance so the hem doesn't ride up later. If you've already washed the chinos several times, tell me — it changes the measurement I use.

Cotton-elastane blends

Chinos with 1–3% elastane are the middle-tier standard. They stretch, they move with you, and they don't wrinkle as much. But elastane means the fabric pulls back after cutting — cut to an exact measurement and the finished hem ends up a few millimetres short. We cut with a larger allowance and use a slightly looser stitch tension so the fabric can still move. Done right, you can't tell the hem was altered. Done wrong, the hem buckles.

Cotton-linen blends

The fabric of choice for Nairobi's warmer months and for clients who wear chinos to brunch. Linen blends drape beautifully but fray easily at the cut edge — we overlock the raw edge before hemming, or use a French seam. Linen also doesn't press as crisply as pure cotton, so slimmed waists need reshaping from the side seams rather than heat-formed darts. If your chinos are linen-blend, tell me when you message — the technique is different from the start.

Brushed cotton and moleskin

Premium chinos — sometimes called "peached cotton" or "moleskin." The fabric surface has a brushed, suede-like finish that presses flat with the nap. Every seam and every cut has to respect the nap direction, otherwise the fabric shows a permanent light-versus-dark patch where you pressed. We lay the pieces flat before cutting, mark the direction with chalk, and press with a muslin cloth on low heat. Slower, but the result is invisible.

Cotton-silk and cotton-wool blends

Designer chinos in this category — Incotex, Boggi and similar — use small amounts of silk or wool blended into cotton for a slight sheen and improved drape. The silk content means the fabric is fragile under a hot needle; the wool content means it responds to steam. We never machine-hem these chinos; everything is hand-finished. Slower, and priced accordingly, but it's the only way to preserve the fabric quality.

Synthetic and stretch-twill chinos

Budget chinos and some workwear chinos use polyester-cotton blends with higher elastane content (5–8%). These are the most durable and the easiest to alter — as long as you control the heat. Poly blends melt under a hot iron, so we press through a protective cloth at low temperature. The seams also have less natural give, so alterations are done with slightly looser allowances. Common among security and hospitality uniforms we handle.
