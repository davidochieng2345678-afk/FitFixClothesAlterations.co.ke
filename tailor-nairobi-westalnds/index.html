The most common Westlands alteration, every week. The second most common Westlands alteration. Office trousers come long, and the average Westlands professional is not the same height as the trouser was cut for. The third most common Westlands alteration. Off-the-rack dress shirts fit correctly at the collar and the shoulders but billow at the waist, especially under a suit jacket. The fourth most common Westlands alteration. A blazer that fits the shoulders and the chest but gapes at the waist is the single most common blazer problem in the world. The fifth most common Westlands alteration, especially among women who work in Westlands offices and attend client dinners. Make your altered garments last longer in a Westlands wardrobe

Six habits that keep a Westlands office wardrobe looking sharp between alterations

A Westlands wardrobe takes more wear than a wardrobe anywhere else in Nairobi. The garments get worn every workday, they sit in cars and on office chairs, they are pressed and dry cleaned more often than casual clothes, and they are usually stored in apartments where hanging space is tight. All of that wears a garment out faster than normal. These are the six habits we teach every Westlands client when they collect an altered garment, and none of them takes more than five minutes.

1. Hang on a wide wooden hanger, never wire

A suit jacket or blazer should be hung on a wide wooden hanger that supports the full width of the shoulders. On a thin wire or plastic hanger, the weight of a wool jacket pulls the shoulder seams out of shape and the collar starts to stand away from the neck. This is worse in Westlands apartments, where hanging space is tight and jackets get pushed together. A wide wooden hanger costs about KSh 500 and saves the shoulder of a garment worth forty to a hundred times that. Trousers should be hung from the hem on a hanger that grips the cuff. The weight of the leg pulls the crease out overnight, whereas hanging from the waistband pulls the waistband out of shape.

2. Let the garment rest 24 hours between wears

Wool is a living fibre. After a day of wear, the fibres need 24 hours to relax back into shape, the creases fall out, the drape resets and the fabric breathes. If you wear the same suit three days a week, the fabric never gets to reset and the suit starts to look tired within a season. Rotate between two or three suits on workdays. This roughly doubles the working life of each garment. If you only own one suit, hang it in a well-ventilated spot overnight and let it breathe before you wear it again.

3. Brush the suit after every wear, two minutes only

A soft clothes brush, run along the shoulders, the lapels, the sleeves and the back of the jacket after every wear, keeps dust and lint out of the wool. Embedded dust dulls the colour and speeds up wear at friction points. Brush downward, following the direction of the nap, not against it. This is the single most important habit for keeping an office suit looking new, and it takes two minutes. On a suit worn twice a week, that is four minutes a week.

4. Dry clean sparingly, twice a year maximum

Every dry clean strips a little more of the natural lanolin out of the wool, which makes the fibres brittle and shortens the life of the canvas. On an office suit worn twice a week, two dry cleans a year is usually enough. On a blazer worn once a week, one dry clean a year is plenty. Between dry cleans, brush and air the garment on a wide hanger for a few hours. Spot clean obvious marks with a damp cloth and let the garment air dry. Never put a suit in a washing machine and never have it cleaned by a service that uses a hot press directly on the fabric. A hot press on wool creates a permanent shine that never comes out.

5. Store in a breathable garment bag, not plastic

Westlands apartments have limited hanging space, and many clients store suits in plastic dry cleaner bags because the bags are free. Do not do this. Plastic traps moisture against the wool, which creates the environment that mildew loves. Mildew on a wool suit shows up as small dark spots that do not come out without damaging the fabric. Use a breathable cotton or canvas garment bag instead. A cotton garment bag costs KSh 1,500 and protects a suit worth thirty to sixty times that. Add a cedar block or lavender sachet to the pocket between seasons to keep moths away.

6. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose button on a suit jacket is a KSh 1,500 full-set replacement today. Left for a month, the button comes off and the buttonhole takes the strain, and re-cutting a hand-worked buttonhole is a specialist job. A short note from the bench

The eight things I check on every Westlands garment before I pick up a needle, and why each one matters more in Westlands than anywhere else

In fourteen years of tailoring, I have altered about six thousand garments for clients who live or work in Westlands. That is more than any other Nairobi neighbourhood I work with, including Kilimani, Karen and Lavington. Westlands is not just the busiest tailoring neighbourhood in Nairobi. It is the neighbourhood with the highest concentration of high-value garments, the highest concentration of professional dress codes, and the highest concentration of clients who notice when a fit is not quite right. A garment that I alter for a Westlands client is going to be worn in front of colleagues, clients, partners, board members and strangers on the street, and every one of those people will notice if the fit is off.

That is why I do eight checks on every Westlands garment before I pick up a needle. Here is what those eight checks are, why each one matters, and what happens when a tailor skips it.

The first check is the fabric. I need to know what the garment is made of, because everything else follows from the fabric. A wool suit is worked differently from a wool-linen blazer. A cotton poplin dress shirt is worked differently from an Oxford cloth shirt. A structured crepe dress is worked differently from a silk chiffon dress. A cashmere overcoat is worked differently from a technical storm coat. When a Westlands client sends us a garment, we always ask for a close-up photo of the fabric and a photo of the inside label, because those two photos tell me the fibre, the weight and the manufacturer, and those three things decide the needle, the tension, the thread and the press. If the client cannot tell me the fabric, I will identify it myself at the pickup before the rider leaves the building. Nothing is cut until I know what I am working with. When a tailor skips the fabric check, the result is a garment that has been altered with the wrong needle and the wrong tension, and the client notices six months later when the fabric starts to pucker along the seam.

The second check is the garment construction. A fused jacket is worked differently from a half-canvassed jacket, and a half-canvassed jacket is worked differently from a fully canvassed jacket. A three-piece suit is worked differently from a two-piece suit, and a waistcoat has to be treated as its own garment even when it comes in as part of a set. A blazer with a partial lining has different internal structure from a blazer with a full lining. A dress with boning in the bodice is worked differently from a dress with a soft bodice. When I check the construction, I am looking at how the garment was made, because that tells me what the alteration can and cannot do without changing the way the garment behaves. A fused jacket cannot be slimmed as aggressively as a canvassed one. A dress with boning cannot be taken in at the waist without adjusting the boning to match. Those are the constraints, and I would rather tell a Westlands client upfront what is possible than deliver a garment that has lost the structure it was built with. When a tailor skips the construction check, the result is a jacket that has lost its chest shape or a dress that has lost its bodice structure, and both of those failures are permanent.

The third check is the posture of the client. This is the check most Nairobi tailors skip, and it is the check that matters most in a professional wardrobe. A client who works at a desk eight hours a day does not stand the same way as a client who is on their feet. A client who drives long distances sits differently from a client who takes the matatu. A client who presents in front of a room stands differently from a client who spends the day at a screen. When a Westlands client sends us a garment for alteration, I ask for two photos of the client wearing the garment: one standing normally, one seated. I do not ask them to stand up straight. I ask them to stand the way they actually stand, because the garment has to fit the actual body, not the idealised one. That is the difference between a jacket that fits on a dress form and a jacket that fits on the client every day of the year. When a tailor skips the posture check, the result is a garment that looks right in the workshop mirror and slightly wrong every time the client wears it out.

The fourth check is the shoes. This matters more for Westlands clients than for clients anywhere else in Nairobi, because the Westlands wardrobe is built around a specific dress shoe. A trouser hem that sits correctly over a leather Oxford sits wrong over a chunky boot. A dress hem that breaks correctly over a heeled shoe drags over a flat shoe. A suit trouser break that reads correctly with a formal loafer reads too long with a sneaker. When we alter a Westlands garment that involves the hem, I always ask the client to send a photo of the shoes they will wear with the garment. For a suit, it is the dress shoes the client wears to client meetings. For a dress, it is the shoes the client will wear to the event. For an overcoat, it is the shoes the client wears on the coldest morning of the year. If the client does not have the shoes at the pickup, we hold the garment until we have the photo, because a hem set without the shoes is a hem set wrong. When a tailor skips the shoe check, the result is a hem that looks correct on the bench and slightly wrong on the street, and the client notices it every time they put the garment on.

The fifth check is the wear pattern on the garment. Westlands garments take more wear than garments in any other neighbourhood. A suit worn four times a week in a Westlands office has different wear than a suit worn once a week in another part of the city. The areas that wear first on a Westlands wardrobe are the seat of the trouser, the elbows of the jacket, the inner thigh of the trouser, the collar of the shirt, and the cuff of the dress. When I check a Westlands garment, I look at those five points before I make any alteration. If a point is starting to thin, I reinforce it while I am doing the main alteration. If a point has already failed, I patch it as part of the same job. The clients who get the most out of their Westlands wardrobes are the ones who let us handle the small reinforcement work at the same time as the alteration, because doing both in one visit costs a fraction of doing them separately. When a tailor skips the wear check, the result is a garment that fails at a point that was already weak, and the client comes back for a repair that could have been prevented.

The sixth check is the deadline. This is the Westlands check that matters most, because Westlands clients are the busiest clients I work with. A Westlands professional does not have a free afternoon to pick up a garment twice. They have their calendar, their meetings, their travel and their events, and the alteration has to fit into the gaps. When a Westlands client sends us a garment, I always ask when they need it back. If the answer is "whenever", I do the work on our standard timeline. If the answer is "before Thursday", I look at the schedule and tell the client straight whether Thursday is realistic or whether they need to book the rush service. I would rather tell a client "we cannot hit Thursday" and have them use a different tailor for the week than take the job and deliver a garment that is rushed and slightly off. That is the promise I make to every Westlands client. When a tailor skips the deadline check, the result is a rushed alteration that has been done at the wrong speed, and the client notices the rushed finish on the first wear.

The seventh check is the pairing. A Westlands professional rarely sends one garment. They send a suit and three shirts, or a blazer and two pairs of trousers, or a dress and a jacket, or a full wedding party wardrobe. When we alter more than one garment for the same client, we check the pairing between them. A jacket that has been shortened at the sleeve has to still work with the shirts the client wears under it, which means the cuff opening has to show the correct amount of shirt cuff on each shirt.
