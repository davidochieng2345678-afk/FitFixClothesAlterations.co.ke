Why We Run Corporate Work Differently to Walk-In Clients

A single client bringing one suit to a workshop is a fifteen-minute transaction. A company bringing fifty staff suits to a workshop is a small logistics operation, and if the workshop does not plan for it, pieces get mixed up, staff complain that the alterations are inconsistent, and the client is left with a lot of paperwork for what should have been a simple job. That is the reason corporate batches at FitFix run on a different workflow to general clients, with staff tagging at collection, matched standards across the batch, and per-staff reporting on delivery.

The tagging is the single most important part of the workflow. When we collect from your reception, every garment gets a tag with the staff name or the staff ID you have provided. That tag stays with the garment through the workshop, so a junior associate's suit and a senior partner's suit sitting next to each other on the bench never get mixed up. When we return the batch, we include a per-staff breakdown sheet that lists what was altered on each garment, matched to the staff name or ID. If a staff member has a query about their suit three weeks later, you have a record of exactly what was done and when.

The matched standard is what stops a batch looking uneven. When fifty staff suits are all taken in at the same office and each individual client brings their own preferences to a workshop, the batch ends up looking like fifty separate alterations that happen to have the same colour fabric. That is the wrong outcome for a corporate uniform programme. The correct way to handle a corporate batch is to set one standard for the alterations across the batch, matched to the way the client wants their staff to look, and hold every staff member's suit to that standard. A slightly shorter jacket sleeve on one staff member and a slightly longer sleeve on another is the difference between a professional-looking team and a group of individuals in matching colours.

Per-staff reporting on delivery is what makes an invoiced corporate account work. If a client is being billed per piece and per staff member, the invoice has to show exactly what was done for each person. Our invoices break the batch down by staff name or ID, list the alteration done on each piece and the unit price, and total at the bottom. That means the client's accounts department can reconcile the invoice against the batch without calling us, and the whole thing runs on the first submission. That is the entire reason we prefer per-staff invoicing over a single lump-sum invoice, even though the lump-sum invoice is easier for us to produce.

The one ask we make of every corporate client is a clean staff list before the first pickup. Names, IDs, the pieces per person and any specific requirement per staff member. With that list we can tag the batch correctly the first time, schedule the work in the right sequence, and return the batch on the promised date. Without it, the batch still gets done but the process runs slower because we have to identify pieces on collection, which adds a day or two to the turnaround. The list is a ten-minute job for the client's HR or admin team and it saves a day on our end.

Tagging discipline

Every piece is tagged per staff member at collection, and that tag stays with the piece until it is returned. A company sending 40 suits to a workshop and getting 40 suits back with no mix-ups is not automatic, it is the result of a tagging system that has to be followed every time. Our tagging has been running for years and is the single biggest reason corporate clients stay with us across multiple batches. The tag has the staff name, the piece number, the service booked and the batch reference, and it is written on a waterproof label that stays readable through the workshop process.

Matched standards

The alterations on every garment in a corporate batch are set to one standard before work begins, based on the client's brief. That means the staff look consistent as a team, not like a group of individuals in matching colours. Matched standards are especially important for hotel, school, clinic and retail uniforms where the team is seen together in public. The standard is documented in the batch file and applied to every subsequent batch from the same client, so a replacement uniform in six months comes back the same as the original batch.

Per-staff invoicing

When a batch of 40 suits is invoiced as one lump sum, the client's accounts department has no way to reconcile the invoice against the batch. Our invoices break the batch down per staff member, so reconciliation is a ten-minute job rather than a phone call to us. That is what makes an invoiced corporate account work from the client's end. The invoice lists the staff name or ID, the piece altered, the alteration done and the unit price, and totals the batch at the bottom on the same page.

Recurring schedules

A security firm with 200 staff does not want to book a pickup every time a pair of boots needs resoling. That is what makes a corporate account predictable from both sides, and it is why most of our corporate clients stay on annual contracts rather than booking batch by batch. Corporate Warranty

Every corporate batch is covered by a 14-day workmanship warranty from the date the batch is delivered back to the client. If a piece fails because of our work, we correct it at no cost, on the next scheduled pickup or on a dedicated pickup if needed. This is the same warranty that applies to individual FitFix clients and it is described in full on the warranty page.

What the corporate warranty covers

The warranty covers workmanship on every piece in the batch. A seam that opens, a stitch that fails, a zip that breaks away from the slider, a hem that unravels within the 14-day period is corrected at no cost. That applies whether it is a single suit, a hotel uniform batch, a school uniform programme, a security firm boot batch or a corporate bespoke suit. There is no separate corporate warranty process — it is the same policy, applied per piece.

What it does not cover

Normal wear and tear, wearer damage, damage from external causes such as chemicals or extreme heat, and any subsequent alteration by another workshop are not covered. Where a staff member has stained or scuffed a uniform within the 14-day period through normal wear on the job, that is wearer damage and the correction is quoted at a fair price. Where the failure is clearly our work, we correct it at no cost regardless of how the piece has been worn.

Where a corporate client is on a recurring monthly or quarterly pickup, the warranty on each piece runs from the date that piece was returned to the client, not from the date of the most recent pickup. This is the fairest way to run the warranty on a recurring contract, and it is the way we do it by default.

Recurring Contracts and Scheduled Work

Most of our corporate work runs on recurring contracts rather than one-off bookings. A recurring contract means the client's operations team does not have to think about tailoring; the pickup arrives on the scheduled day, the batch is processed, and the batch is returned on the scheduled day. Four patterns of recurring contract cover almost every corporate client we serve.

Monthly uniform top-up (hotels, clinics, retail)

A hotel with 120 staff typically replaces or alters five to ten pieces a month as staff join, leave or change positions. The monthly top-up contract collects on the first Tuesday of every month, processes the batch in three to five working days, and returns on the second Tuesday. The client's HR team knows exactly when the next pickup is coming and can plan new starter uniforms around it.

Quarterly boot resole (security firms, industrial)

A security firm with 200 guards typically rotates 60 to 80 pairs of boots through us every quarter for resoling, stitching and polish. The quarterly contract collects on a fixed week each quarter, resoles and repairs the batch, and returns the whole set in one delivery. The client's operations manager knows that their team's boots will be back in service on a specific date, which is critical because boots cannot be off for weeks at a time.

Term-start uniform programme (schools)

Schools run their uniform programme three times a year at the January, May and September term starts. The programme collects the batch one week before term starts, alters every piece to the standard, and returns the batch three days before term begins so parents and students have time to pick up their uniforms. This is the tightest schedule we run on any corporate contract and it is why schools need to book as early as possible.

Annual suit refresh (offices, banks, consultancies)

A bank or consultancy with 60 professional staff typically runs an annual suit refresh in Q1 of every year. Every staff member's suits are collected on one batch day, assessed against the same matched standard, and returned within a week. Over time we build a personal record for each staff member, so a suit altered in year one can be re-altered in year two if the staff member's measurements have changed, without the client having to send measurements again.
