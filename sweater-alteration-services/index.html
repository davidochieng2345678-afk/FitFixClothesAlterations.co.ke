The structural difference nobody explains

Why knitwear alterations are a completely different craft from tailoring

Most tailors treat knitwear the same way they treat woven fabric — mark it, cut it, stitch it. That's the wrong approach, and it's the reason so many altered sweaters end up unwearable. Knitwear alteration is not tailoring. It's re-knitting, and it takes a completely different skill set. Here's what that means in practice.

Woven fabric vs knit fabric — the fundamental divide

A shirt, a trouser, a jacket — these are woven. Two sets of threads crossing at right angles, stable in both directions, and when you cut them, the seams stay where you put them. A sweater is knit. One continuous loop of yarn running in connected rows. When you cut knit fabric, the loops near the cut edge start to unravel, and the fabric pulls back toward its natural state. Cut a sweater sleeve to the wrong length and it will look shorter by the time you finish the alteration, because the yarn has relaxed back.

How knitwear is actually shortened

We don't cut a sweater to shorten it. We un-link the row of loops at the point we want the new length, remove the surplus rows entirely — carefully, stitch by stitch — and then re-link the remaining rows to the cuff, hem or collar ribbing. This process is called "unravelling to length," and it's the only way to shorten a knit garment without creating a visible seam. On a fine-gauge cashmere sweater, unlinking a hem row takes about forty minutes of focused work. On chunky knit wool, it's roughly twice as long, because every loop is bigger and the yarn has more memory.

Why most alterations shops get this wrong

An alterations shop that's used to working on shirts and trousers will try to shorten a sweater by cutting off the hem, folding it up, and stitching a new hem. The result looks passable for about three washes. Then the knit starts to unravel where it was cut, the folded hem sags, and the sweater is ruined. If a tailor quotes you KSh 500 to shorten a sweater, they're planning to do this — because that's the only way to do it that cheap. A properly unlinking-and-relinking alteration on a standard merino sweater starts at KSh 1,500 and takes two to four days. The price difference reflects the actual work.

Ribbing is not the same as knit

The cuffs, hem and collar on most sweaters are knitted in "rib" — a repeating knit-purl pattern that creates vertical stretch and grip. Ribbing behaves completely differently from the knit of the sweater body. It's denser, has more elasticity, and stretches asymmetrically along its length. When we re-link a knit body to a ribbed hem, the tension has to be matched exactly, otherwise the ribbing grips too tightly (creating a bubble) or too loosely (creating a flare). Getting tension right on a knit-to-rib join is the single hardest part of sweater alteration.

You can't adjust a knit sweater at the side seam

On a woven shirt, slimming is done at the side seams — cut, taper, re-stitch. On a sweater, the "side" is a continuous curve of knit rows. There is no seam to open and re-stitch. Slimming a sweater body means taking in rows along the length of the garment — a process called "reducing stitch count" — and it can only be done when the sweater is fully un-linked and re-knit. This is why sweater slimming is one of the most labour-intensive alterations we do, and why it costs between KSh 2,500 and KSh 3,500 depending on the yarn gauge.

Why this matters to your wallet

We charge KSh 2,500 to slim a sweater body. A general alterations shop might charge KSh 1,000 — and they'll do it wrong, and the sweater will look worse after three washes than it did before. A properly re-knitted alteration holds its shape for the life of the sweater. That's the difference between paying once and paying twice, and it's the same principle we teach on our darning page and our polo shirt alterations page.

Read the label before you book

Three tiers of knitwear yarn — three different alteration jobs

A KSh 1,500 acrylic sweater from a market stall and a KSh 18,000 Scottish cashmere sweater require completely different alteration approaches. If your tailor treats both the same way, one of them is going to end up looking wrong. Here's how I categorise knitwear before I decide what technique to use.

Entry tier

Acrylic and synthetic blends

Mass-produced knitwear from market stalls, mid-tier retailers, and fast fashion. Yarn weight 200–300 gsm, usually a coarse gauge. Colour tends to pill and fade over the first year. Standard ribbed cuffs and hem, sometimes with a fused-on collar. The most common sweater in Nairobi.

What we do differentlyAcrylic has poor natural memory — it doesn't return to shape like wool. Re-linking has to be done at lower tension so the finished alteration doesn't pull. Acrylic also melts under a hot iron, so pressing has to be done through a muslin cloth at low temperature.

Mid tier

Merino wool and cotton knits

Uniqlo, Muji, mid-range Italian labels. Yarn weight 250–340 gsm, often fine-gauge machine knit. Merino has natural memory and holds shape through re-linking. Cotton knit is heavier and less elastic. Both often have a small percentage of elastane for stretch. The standard sweater for Nairobi professionals.

What we do differentlyThe natural memory of merino means we can achieve very precise re-linking — the yarn returns to its original gauge after adjustment. On cotton knits, we cut the surplus with a slightly larger allowance because cotton stretches unpredictably after cutting.

Premium tier

Cashmere, alpaca and hand-knitted

Scottish cashmere, Italian and Mongolian cashmere, baby alpaca, and hand-knitted pieces from local artisans or family heirlooms. Yarn weight 180–400 gsm, often a fine gauge with hand-finished seams. Cashmere has exceptional softness and complex fibre memory. Hand-knitted pieces have no two rows exactly alike. Irreplaceable.

What we do differentlyEvery cashmere alteration is done by hand on a bright-daylight bench. The yarn is delicate, the fibres short, and every stitch leaves a mark. We work slowly. On hand-knitted pieces, we match the original knitter's tension and stitch pattern — even if it means the alteration takes twice as long as a machine-knit equivalent. A careless alteration on a KSh 18,000 cashmere sweater is a KSh 18,000 mistake.

The five parts of a sweater that affect the alteration

What a sweater actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a sweater as "woolly" or "chunky" or "cashmere." None of those descriptions tell you anything useful for alteration. Structurally, a sweater has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what I look at before I take a needle to a sweater.

01

The knit structure — gauge and stitch pattern

Sweaters are knitted in different gauges — fine gauge (10–12 stitches per inch), medium gauge (6–8 stitches per inch), or chunky gauge (3–5 stitches per inch). The gauge determines the size of the loops and how long re-linking takes. Fine-gauge cashmere has small loops and takes the most time per row; chunky wool has larger loops but each one is heavier and less forgiving of tension error. The stitch pattern — plain knit, rib, cable, Fair Isle — also matters, because a cable knit has to be re-linked so the cable pattern continues seamlessly.

02

The ribbing — cuffs, hem and collar

Ribbing is a knit-purl alternating pattern that creates vertical stretch and grip. It's used at cuffs, hems and collars on nearly every sweater. The ribbing has a different tension and elasticity from the sweater body, which means any alteration that touches the body has to be joined back to the ribbing at exactly the right tension. On a well-made sweater, this join is done with a specific linking machine in the factory. We do it by hand, and match the factory tension.

03

The shoulder join — raglan or set-in

Sweaters have two shoulder constructions. Raglan sweaters have a diagonal seam from the collar to the underarm — this join is easy to adjust because it follows the shoulder slope. Set-in sweaters have a horizontal shoulder seam where the sleeve meets the body at the shoulder point — this construction is more structured and requires the armhole to be reshaped precisely. Shoulder adjustment on a set-in sweater is one of the most technical alterations we do, and it starts at KSh 2,500.

04

The neckline — crew, V, turtleneck or polo

Crew necklines have a circular ribbed collar that sits close to the neck. V-necklines have a ribbed edging that follows the V. Turtlenecks have a taller ribbed collar that folds over. Polo-collar sweaters have a knitted flat collar with a placket. Each neckline adjusts differently. On a crew neck, we can re-link the collar at a tighter or looser tension. On a V-neck, we re-shape the V angle by adding or removing rows. On a turtleneck, we can shorten or lengthen the collar itself.

05

The seams — knitted in or hand-linked

Sweater seams come in two types. Machine-knitted sweaters usually have linked seams — a fine, flat seam made by a linking machine that joins the front and back panels. Hand-knitted sweaters usually have hand-linked seams — a slower, more decorative seam made with a needle and thread. When we alter a sweater at any seam, we match the original seam construction. On hand-linked sweaters, that means the alteration is done entirely by hand. On machine-linked sweaters, the finish still looks machine-made after the alteration.

If a tailor quotes you a sweater alteration without asking about the gauge, the ribbing or the seam type, they're guessing — and the result will show up in the first wash.

Every fibre demands its own approach

The five knitwear fibres I work with most often — and how each one behaves

Knitwear fibre is where most alterations either succeed or fail. Each one re-links, stretches, and presses differently. Here's what I've learned across fourteen years, fibre by fibre.

Merino wool

Merino is the ideal knitwear fibre for alteration. The fibres are long, fine, and full of natural crimp — which means the yarn has excellent memory. When we re-link a merino hem, the yarn returns to its original gauge naturally. Merino also presses beautifully, holds shape after washing, and is forgiving of small tension variations. If your merino sweater needs slimming or shortening, the alteration will hold for the life of the garment. Merino is the fibre I most enjoy working with, and the one that responds most predictably to alteration.

Cashmere

Cashmere is the fibre I handle with the most respect. The fibres are short, soft, and delicate. Every stitch leaves a small mark, and if you over-handle the yarn it starts to pill and lose softness within a season. All cashmere alterations at our workshop are done by hand, on a daylight bench, using yarn matched to the original weight. On a fine-gauge cashmere sweater, re-linking a hem row takes about forty minutes. On a heavier gauge, closer to an hour. The result is worth it — a hand-altered cashmere sweater looks and feels untouched.

Cotton knit

Cotton knit is heavier than wool and less elastic. That makes it easy to handle but harder to re-link precisely, because cotton doesn't return to its original shape the way wool does. When we shorten a cotton sweater, we cut with a small allowance and re-link at a slightly tighter tension so the finished hem sits correctly after washing. Cotton also shows needle marks more than wool, so we use fine needles and match thread weight to the yarn. Nairobi's warm months call for a lot of cotton knitwear. We do them weekly.

Alpaca and alpaca blends

Alpaca is warmer than wool, softer than merino, and slightly less elastic — which means it takes re-linking differently from a wool sweater. The fibres are long and smooth, so the knit has a natural drape and a slight sheen. Alpaca also doesn't spring back the way wool does, so a re-linked alpaca hem needs a slightly looser tension to avoid looking strained. On alpaca blends (usually 70% alpaca / 30% wool), the behaviour sits between the two fibres — we handle them like a fine wool with extra care.

Acrylic and synthetic blends

Acrylic has poor natural memory. It doesn't return to shape like wool, and it stretches permanently under tension. That changes the technique on every alteration — we re-link at lower tension so the finished work doesn't pull, and we avoid pressing anything beyond lukewarm because acrylic melts under a hot iron. Acrylic sweaters are the least expensive to alter and also the least rewarding, because the fibre has a limited lifespan regardless of how well the alteration is done. We still do them properly — but we tell clients honestly when the numbers favour buying new.

Hand-knitted wool and hand-spun yarns

Hand-knitted sweaters are the most rewarding knitwear to alter and the most difficult. No two rows are identical, the tension varies slightly across the garment, and the yarn may be hand-spun (irregular thickness). We match the original knitter's tension and stitch pattern on every row of the alteration. On hand-knitted pieces, we allow roughly double the time of a machine-knit equivalent. It's slow, meditative work — and the result, on a hand-knitted heirloom, is the difference between an alteration that disappears and one that stands out like a scar.

Before you book — does your sweater actually fit?

Seven checkpoints that tell you if a sweater needs altering

Most clients message saying "my sweater doesn't feel right." That's not enough for me to quote accurately, and it's not enough for you to decide what to ask for. Here's the checklist I use on every sweater in the workshop. Run through it with the sweater on. If three or more are off, the sweater is worth altering. If one or two, you may be able to live with it.

01

The shoulder seam sits at your shoulder point

Stand side-on to a mirror. The shoulder join — where the sleeve meets the body — should end exactly at the widest point of your shoulder bone. If it hangs past that point, the sweater is too big and needs the shoulder adjusted (raglan construction is easier to adjust; set-in construction starts at KSh 2,500). If it sits inside your shoulder line, the sweater is too small and cannot be fixed. Shoulder width is the dealbreaker on knitwear, exactly as it is on shirts.

02

The sleeves end at the wrist bone, showing no more than 2 cm of shirt cuff

Sweater sleeves should end at your wrist bone — the same point as a shirt sleeve, maybe 1 cm below. If they cover your hands, the sleeves are too long and need re-linking at the cuff. If they end well above your wrist bone, the sweater is too small in the arm and can't be lengthened more than 1 cm. Sleeve shortening on knitwear is KSh 1,500 and takes two to three days because the cuff ribbing has to be un-linked and re-linked at matched tension.

03

The body follows your silhouette without billowing at the sides

With the sweater on and your arms relaxed at your sides, look at the fabric between the armpit and the hem. It should follow the curve of your body without bubbling out. If it billows like a tent, the body needs slimming — KSh 2,500 for a standard-weight sweater, from KSh 3,000 on fine-gauge cashmere. If the fabric strains across your chest or back, the sweater is too small in the body and cannot be fixed.

04

The hem ribbing sits flat against your hips, not flared or gripped

Look at the ribbed hem at the bottom of the sweater. It should sit flat and comfortable — neither flaring out nor gripping your hips tightly. If the hem rib is stretched out and flares, the rib has lost its memory and needs replacing (KSh 1,800 for a new rib at matching colour and gauge). If the hem rib is gripping too tightly and creating a bubble of fabric above it, the sweater body needs shortening or the rib needs re-linking at a looser tension (KSh 1,500 for hem shortening).

05

The collar sits flat around your neck, with one finger of slack

On a crew neck or V-neck sweater, the collar ribbing should sit flat around your neck with roughly one finger of slack. If it gapes widely, the neckline is too big and needs re-linking at tighter tension (KSh 1,500). If it grips uncomfortably, the ribbing needs re-linking at looser tension. On turtleneck sweaters, the collar itself can be shortened or lengthened by removing or adding rows — a slow hand job, but the result is a collar that fits properly.

06

The overall length ends at the right point on your body

Stand in front of a mirror and check where the hem falls. A crew neck sweater should end just below the waistband of your trousers, showing roughly 3–5 cm of shirt tail if you're layering. If it falls well below your belt, it's too long. If it ends above your waistband and rides up when you raise your arms, it's too short and can't be lengthened more than 1–2 cm. Length adjustment is KSh 1,500 and takes two to three days.

07

The knit isn't pulling, twisted or skewed

Look at the vertical columns of stitches — called "wales" — running up the front of the sweater. They should run perfectly straight, top to bottom. If the wales twist or skew to one side, the yarn has relaxed unevenly — usually from a bad wash or from previous alteration done incorrectly. We can sometimes correct a minor skew by re-blocking the sweater (KSh 1,200), but a major skew means the yarn has permanently changed shape and the sweater can't be fully corrected.

Run through all seven. If three or more feel wrong, send me a photo on WhatsApp and I'll tell you honestly which alterations will make the biggest difference. You don't have to fix everything at once — often the right answer is to fix one thing and live with the rest.

An honest decision — alter, or replace?

When a sweater is worth altering, and when you should just buy a new one

I turn down knitwear jobs every month. Not because I can't do them — because the honest answer is that a KSh 1,500 alteration on a KSh 1,800 acrylic sweater doesn't make sense. Here's the test I use before I quote you. If your sweater fails more than two of these, buy a new one.

✓

What fibre is it made from?Acrylic and polyester knitwear has limited lifespan — the fibres pill, lose shape and go shiny within two years, no matter how carefully you handle them. Altering it is a short-term fix. Merino wool, cashmere, alpaca and cotton knitwear improve with age and hold alteration results for years. The fibre determines whether the investment makes sense.

✓

Is the knit still holding its shape?Stretch the body fabric gently between your hands and let it go. Does it snap back, or does it stay stretched? Knitwear with good memory is worth altering. Knitwear that's gone floppy or baggy — usually from over-washing or heat — won't hold a new alteration either. The yarn has already relaxed permanently.

✓

Are the cuffs, hem and collar ribbing still in good condition?Ribbing is the first thing to go on a worn sweater. If the cuffs are stretched, the hem rib is floppy and the collar rib is baggy, replacing all of them costs almost as much as the sweater did originally. If the ribbing is crisp but the body needs work, alteration makes sense. If the ribbing is gone, the whole alteration investment rarely pays back.

✓

Will the alteration cost less than 40% of a new one?If a mid-tier merino sweater costs KSh 4,500 new, an alteration should cost no more than KSh 1,800 to be worth doing. If it costs more, the numbers usually favour buying new — unless the sweater is a specific fit or colour you can't easily replace. On cashmere and alpaca, the calculation shifts because replacement cost is much higher.

✓

Is the fit fixable at all?Too big can be altered. Too small cannot — you can't create yarn where none exists. If the sweater is one size too small in the shoulders or chest, no tailor can save it. If it's one size too big, an alteration can make it fit perfectly. On knitwear, the shoulder measurement is the dealbreaker — everything else can usually be adjusted.

✓

Does the sweater have meaning beyond its price tag?A sweater your grandmother knitted, a jumper from a specific trip, a piece with sentimental value — the calculation changes entirely. On sentimental garments, we do the alteration no matter what the numbers say, because the value isn't in the yarn. Tell us when you message, and the quote is built around the meaning, not the price tag.

You won't get a hard sell when you message me. If the honest answer is "buy a new one," I'll tell you that straight. Better that than charging you for an alteration that stops looking right in six months.

English

"What's the difference between re-knitting and darning?"

Darning creates a woven patch over a hole — the traditional basket-weave pattern you see on the reverse. Re-knitting recreates the actual knit loop structure, so the finished area looks and behaves identically to the surrounding fabric. On knitwear, re-knitting is always the better result, because it preserves the stretch and drape of the garment. Darning works on knitwear in a pinch, but the patch will feel stiff and won't stretch with the sweater. See our darning and invisible mending page for the full distinction.

A short note from the bench

Why I re-link sweaters by hand — not on a machine

There are two ways to re-link a knit garment. You can do it on a linking machine — the industrial tool used in factories — or you can do it by hand with a needle and thread. The machine is faster. It's uniform. It produces a seam that looks exactly like the original factory seam, because it is the original factory method.

I own a linking machine. I use it on factory-knit sweaters where the original seams were machine-linked. But for cashmere, for hand-knitted pieces, for anything older than twenty years, and for any garment where the original knitter's hand is visible in the work — I do it entirely by hand. Not because I can't use the machine. Because on those garments, the machine would leave a visible signature.

Here's what I mean. A hand-linked seam has a slightly irregular, organic quality — the loop tension varies by a fraction of a millimetre between stitches, because a human hand made it. A machine-linked seam is perfectly uniform. If you place a machine-linked alteration next to a hand-linked original, an experienced eye picks it up instantly. The colour is right, the yarn is right, the gauge is right — but the rhythm is wrong.

On a fine-gauge cashmere sweater, hand re-linking a hem row takes me about forty-five minutes. On chunky hand-knitted wool, closer to an hour and a half, because every loop is bigger and the yarn has more memory — I have to work slowly to keep the tension even. Multiply by the number of rows involved in any alteration, and you understand why knitwear alteration costs what it costs. It is genuinely slow work, and there is no shortcut that produces the same result.

If you own a sweater where the fit has gone wrong and you're not sure whether it's worth fixing — send me a photo. I'll tell you straight whether a re-link is worth the investment, or whether the honest answer is to keep the sweater as it is and buy another for daily wear. That's the answer you deserve.

What's the difference between re-knitting and darning on a sweater?

Darning creates a woven patch over a hole — the traditional basket-weave pattern you see on the reverse. Re-knitting recreates the actual knit loop structure, so the repaired area looks and behaves identically to the surrounding fabric. On knitwear, re-knitting is always the better result because it preserves the stretch and drape of the garment. Darning works in a pinch, but the patch feels stiff and doesn't stretch with the sweater. See our darning and invisible mending page for the full distinction.

Can you shorten sweater sleeves and preserve the cuff ribbing?

Yes, and preserving the ribbing is the whole job. We un-link the sleeve rows at the cuff, remove the surplus length, and re-link the sleeve back to the ribbing at matched tension. If the tension isn't matched, the cuff grips too tightly or the sleeve twists after washing. Sleeve shortening is KSh 1,500 and takes two to three days. Done right — how we do it — the sleeve looks factory-standard after the alteration.

Make them last between alterations

Eight habits that keep sweaters out of the alteration pile

Knitwear is the most fragile thing in most wardrobes and the most easily ruined by a single bad wash. Here are the eight habits I teach every client when they collect an altered sweater. The same ones I follow with my own.

1. Wash cold, wash by hand if you can

Wool, cashmere and alpaca should be hand-washed in cool water with a gentle knitwear detergent — never a standard laundry detergent, which strips the natural oils from the fibres. If you must use a machine, use the wool cycle at 30°C or lower with a mesh laundry bag. Skip fabric softener entirely. Turn the sweater inside out before washing — this reduces the pilling caused by friction.

2. Never tumble dry — ever

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shrink a wool sweater by a full size and to permanently felt the fibres. It also weakens the ribbing on cuffs and hems. After washing, lay the sweater flat on a clean towel, roll the towel up gently to absorb excess water, then lay the sweater flat on a fresh dry towel or mesh drying rack. Never hang a wet sweater — the weight of the water will stretch the shoulders out of shape.

3. Store folded, never on a hanger

Hanging a sweater is the single biggest cause of shoulder stretch on knitwear. The entire weight of the garment hangs on two small points at the shoulders, and over a season those points stretch permanently. Fold sweaters flat and stack them in a drawer or on a shelf. If you're storing for the season, use a breathable cotton garment bag — not plastic, which traps moisture and can cause mildew or moth damage.

4. Give knitwear 48 hours between wears

Knitwear needs longer to recover than woven fabric — 48 hours, ideally. Wool fibres have natural memory but they need time to return to shape after being worn. Wearing the same sweater two days in a row means the ribbing stays stretched and the elbows stay bagged. Rotate three or four sweaters through the week. This single habit reduces the frequency of cuff and hem repairs we do by more than half.

5. Depill with a proper sweater comb, not a razor

Pilling is normal — the surface fibres tangle as they rub against each other. Remove pills with a proper sweater comb or a fabric shaver designed for knitwear. Never use a disposable razor — it cuts the fabric itself, not just the pills, and creates thin patches that will eventually open into holes. Depill gently, in one direction, with the sweater laid flat on a table.

6. Deal with moths before they deal with your sweater

Moths eat wool, cashmere, silk and any blend with more than 30% animal fibre. Store knitwear with cedar blocks, lavender sachets or camphor (wrapped so it doesn't touch the fabric). Refresh the deterrent every four to six months — the scent fades long before the deterrent does. If you see small holes in one sweater, check the rest of your wool wardrobe immediately — moth damage spreads quickly in a closed drawer.

7. Air between wears, before putting it away

Wool neutralises odours naturally, so knitwear rarely needs washing after a single wear. Instead, lay the sweater flat on a clean surface and let it air for a few hours before folding it back into the drawer. This lets body moisture evaporate and lets the fibres recover their shape. You'll wash the sweater half as often — which will double its lifespan and keep the shape sharper for years.

8. Fix ribbing before it fails completely

When a cuff or hem rib starts to lose its grip, it's a KSh 1,200 repair — re-linking at tighter tension. Once the rib yarn has fully relaxed, it's a KSh 1,800 job — replace the rib entirely. The difference is patience. If you notice your cuffs starting to feel loose, message us. Small fixes at the right moment always cost less than waiting for the failure.

Follow those eight habits and your sweaters will hold their shape and ribbing for years. And when they do eventually need an alteration, we're one WhatsApp message away.
