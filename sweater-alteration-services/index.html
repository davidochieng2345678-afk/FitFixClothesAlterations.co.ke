The structural difference nobody explains

Why knitwear alterations are a completely different craft from tailoring

Most tailors treat knitwear the same way they treat woven fabric — mark it, cut it, stitch it. That's the wrong approach, and it's the reason so many altered sweaters end up unwearable. Knitwear alteration is not tailoring. It's re-knitting, and it takes a completely different skill set. Here's what that means in practice.

Woven fabric vs knit fabric — the fundamental divide

A shirt, a trouser, a jacket — these are woven. Two sets of threads crossing at right angles, stable in both directions, and when you cut them, the seams stay where you put them. A sweater is knit. One continuous loop of yarn running in connected rows. When you cut knit fabric, the loops near the cut edge start to unravel, and the fabric pulls back toward its natural state. Cut a sweater sleeve to the wrong length and it will look shorter by the time you finish the alteration, because the yarn has relaxed back.

How knitwear is actually shortened

We don't cut a sweater to shorten it. We un-link the row of loops at the point we want the new length, remove the surplus rows entirely — carefully, stitch by stitch — and then re-link the remaining rows to the cuff, hem or collar ribbing. This process is called "unravelling to length," and it's the only way to shorten a knit garment without creating a visible seam. On a fine-gauge cashmere sweater, unlinking a hem row takes about forty minutes of focused work. On chunky knit wool, it's roughly twice as long, because every loop is bigger and the yarn has more memory.

Why most alterations shops get this wrong

An alterations shop that's used to working on shirts and trousers will try to shorten a sweater by cutting off the hem, folding it up, and stitching a new hem. The result looks passable for about three washes. Then the knit starts to unravel where it was cut, the folded hem sags, and the sweater is ruined. If a tailor quotes you KSh 500 to shorten a sweater, they're planning to do this — because that's the only way to do it that cheap. A properly unlinking-and-relinking alteration on a standard merino sweater starts at KSh 1,500 and takes two to four days. The price difference reflects the actual work.

Ribbing is not the same as knit

The cuffs, hem and collar on most sweaters are knitted in "rib" — a repeating knit-purl pattern that creates vertical stretch and grip. Ribbing behaves completely differently from the knit of the sweater body. It's denser, has more elasticity, and stretches asymmetrically along its length. When we re-link a knit body to a ribbed hem, the tension has to be matched exactly, otherwise the ribbing grips too tightly (creating a bubble) or too loosely (creating a flare). Getting tension right on a knit-to-rib join is the single hardest part of sweater alteration.

You can't adjust a knit sweater at the side seam

On a woven shirt, slimming is done at the side seams — cut, taper, re-stitch. On a sweater, the "side" is a continuous curve of knit rows. There is no seam to open and re-stitch. Slimming a sweater body means taking in rows along the length of the garment — a process called "reducing stitch count" — and it can only be done when the sweater is fully un-linked and re-knit. This is why sweater slimming is one of the most labour-intensive alterations we do, and why it costs between KSh 2,500 and KSh 3,500 depending on the yarn gauge.

Why this matters to your wallet

We charge KSh 2,500 to slim a sweater body. A general alterations shop might charge KSh 1,000 — and they'll do it wrong, and the sweater will look worse after three washes than it did before. A properly re-knitted alteration holds its shape for the life of the sweater. That's the difference between paying once and paying twice, and it's the same principle we teach on our darning page and our polo shirt alterations page.

Read the label before you book

Three tiers of knitwear yarn — three different alteration jobs

A KSh 1,500 acrylic sweater from a market stall and a KSh 18,000 Scottish cashmere sweater require completely different alteration approaches. If your tailor treats both the same way, one of them is going to end up looking wrong. Here's how I categorise knitwear before I decide what technique to use.

Entry tier

Acrylic and synthetic blends

Mass-produced knitwear from market stalls, mid-tier retailers, and fast fashion. Yarn weight 200–300 gsm, usually a coarse gauge. Colour tends to pill and fade over the first year. Standard ribbed cuffs and hem, sometimes with a fused-on collar. The most common sweater in Nairobi.

What we do differentlyAcrylic has poor natural memory — it doesn't return to shape like wool. Re-linking has to be done at lower tension so the finished alteration doesn't pull. Acrylic also melts under a hot iron, so pressing has to be done through a muslin cloth at low temperature.

Mid tier

Merino wool and cotton knits

Uniqlo, Muji, mid-range Italian labels. Yarn weight 250–340 gsm, often fine-gauge machine knit. Merino has natural memory and holds shape through re-linking. Cotton knit is heavier and less elastic. Both often have a small percentage of elastane for stretch. The standard sweater for Nairobi professionals.

What we do differentlyThe natural memory of merino means we can achieve very precise re-linking — the yarn returns to its original gauge after adjustment. On cotton knits, we cut the surplus with a slightly larger allowance because cotton stretches unpredictably after cutting.

Premium tier

Cashmere, alpaca and hand-knitted

Scottish cashmere, Italian and Mongolian cashmere, baby alpaca, and hand-knitted pieces from local artisans or family heirlooms. Yarn weight 180–400 gsm, often a fine gauge with hand-finished seams. Cashmere has exceptional softness and complex fibre memory. Hand-knitted pieces have no two rows exactly alike. Irreplaceable.

What we do differentlyEvery cashmere alteration is done by hand on a bright-daylight bench. The yarn is delicate, the fibres short, and every stitch leaves a mark. We work slowly. On hand-knitted pieces, we match the original knitter's tension and stitch pattern — even if it means the alteration takes twice as long as a machine-knit equivalent. A careless alteration on a KSh 18,000 cashmere sweater is a KSh 18,000 mistake.

The five parts of a sweater that affect the alteration

What a sweater actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a sweater as "woolly" or "chunky" or "cashmere." None of those descriptions tell you anything useful for alteration. Structurally, a sweater has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what I look at before I take a needle to a sweater.

01

The knit structure — gauge and stitch pattern

Sweaters are knitted in different gauges — fine gauge (10–12 stitches per inch), medium gauge (6–8 stitches per inch), or chunky gauge (3–5 stitches per inch). The gauge determines the size of the loops and how long re-linking takes. Fine-gauge cashmere has small loops and takes the most time per row; chunky wool has larger loops but each one is heavier and less forgiving of tension error. The stitch pattern — plain knit, rib, cable, Fair Isle — also matters, because a cable knit has to be re-linked so the cable pattern continues seamlessly.

02

The ribbing — cuffs, hem and collar

Ribbing is a knit-purl alternating pattern that creates vertical stretch and grip. It's used at cuffs, hems and collars on nearly every sweater. The ribbing has a different tension and elasticity from the sweater body, which means any alteration that touches the body has to be joined back to the ribbing at exactly the right tension. On a well-made sweater, this join is done with a specific linking machine in the factory. We do it by hand, and match the factory tension.

03

The shoulder join — raglan or set-in

Sweaters have two shoulder constructions. Raglan sweaters have a diagonal seam from the collar to the underarm — this join is easy to adjust because it follows the shoulder slope. Set-in sweaters have a horizontal shoulder seam where the sleeve meets the body at the shoulder point — this construction is more structured and requires the armhole to be reshaped precisely. Shoulder adjustment on a set-in sweater is one of the most technical alterations we do, and it starts at KSh 2,500.

04

The neckline — crew, V, turtleneck or polo

Crew necklines have a circular ribbed collar that sits close to the neck. V-necklines have a ribbed edging that follows the V. Turtlenecks have a taller ribbed collar that folds over. Polo-collar sweaters have a knitted flat collar with a placket. Each neckline adjusts differently. On a crew neck, we can re-link the collar at a tighter or looser tension. On a V-neck, we re-shape the V angle by adding or removing rows. On a turtleneck, we can shorten or lengthen the collar itself.

05

The seams — knitted in or hand-linked

Sweater seams come in two types. Machine-knitted sweaters usually have linked seams — a fine, flat seam made by a linking machine that joins the front and back panels. Hand-knitted sweaters usually have hand-linked seams — a slower, more decorative seam made with a needle and thread. When we alter a sweater at any seam, we match the original seam construction. On hand-linked sweaters, that means the alteration is done entirely by hand. On machine-linked sweaters, the finish still looks machine-made after the alteration.

If a tailor quotes you a sweater alteration without asking about the gauge, the ribbing or the seam type, they're guessing — and the result will show up in the first wash.

Every fibre demands its own approach

The five knitwear fibres I work with most often — and how each one behaves

Knitwear fibre is where most alterations either succeed or fail. Each one re-links, stretches, and presses differently. Here's what I've learned across fourteen years, fibre by fibre.

Merino wool

Merino is the ideal knitwear fibre for alteration. The fibres are long, fine, and full of natural crimp — which means the yarn has excellent memory. When we re-link a merino hem, the yarn returns to its original gauge naturally. Merino also presses beautifully, holds shape after washing, and is forgiving of small tension variations. If your merino sweater needs slimming or shortening, the alteration will hold for the life of the garment. Merino is the fibre I most enjoy working with, and the one that responds most predictably to alteration.

Cashmere

Cashmere is the fibre I handle with the most respect. The fibres are short, soft, and delicate. Every stitch leaves a small mark, and if you over-handle the yarn it starts to pill and lose softness within a season. All cashmere alterations at our workshop are done by hand, on a daylight bench, using yarn matched to the original weight. On a fine-gauge cashmere sweater, re-linking a hem row takes about forty minutes. On a heavier gauge, closer to an hour. The result is worth it — a hand-altered cashmere sweater looks and feels untouched.

Cotton knit

Cotton knit is heavier than wool and less elastic. That makes it easy to handle but harder to re-link precisely, because cotton doesn't return to its original shape the way wool does. When we shorten a cotton sweater, we cut with a small allowance and re-link at a slightly tighter tension so the finished hem sits correctly after washing. Cotton also shows needle marks more than wool, so we use fine needles and match thread weight to the yarn. Nairobi's warm months call for a lot of cotton knitwear. We do them weekly.

Alpaca and alpaca blends

Alpaca is warmer than wool, softer than merino, and slightly less elastic — which means it takes re-linking differently from a wool sweater. The fibres are long and smooth, so the knit has a natural drape and a slight sheen. Alpaca also doesn't spring back the way wool does, so a re-linked alpaca hem needs a slightly looser tension to avoid looking strained. On alpaca blends (usually 70% alpaca / 30% wool), the behaviour sits between the two fibres — we handle them like a fine wool with extra care.

Acrylic and synthetic blends

Acrylic has poor natural memory. It doesn't return to shape like wool, and it stretches permanently under tension. That changes the technique on every alteration — we re-link at lower tension so the finished work doesn't pull, and we avoid pressing anything beyond lukewarm because acrylic melts under a hot iron. Acrylic sweaters are the least expensive to alter and also the least rewarding, because the fibre has a limited lifespan regardless of how well the alteration is done. We still do them properly — but we tell clients honestly when the numbers favour buying new.

Hand-knitted wool and hand-spun yarns

Hand-knitted sweaters are the most rewarding knitwear to alter and the most difficult. No two rows are identical, the tension varies slightly across the garment, and the yarn may be hand-spun (irregular thickness). We match the original knitter's tension and stitch pattern on every row of the alteration. On hand-knitted pieces, we allow roughly double the time of a machine-knit equivalent. It's slow, meditative work — and the result, on a hand-knitted heirloom, is the difference between an alteration that disappears and one that stands out like a scar.
