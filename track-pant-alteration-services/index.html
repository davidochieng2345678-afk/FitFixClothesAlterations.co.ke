The fabric is the garment

Why a track pant alteration is closer to sportswear engineering than to tailoring

Every other trouser on this bench — a chino, a suit trouser, a pair of jeans, a pair of shorts — is woven cloth that has been given a fit. A track pant is different. It's a piece of performance knitwear that has been cut to a body shape, and the alteration has to respect the performance as much as the shape. If a tailor uses the same technique they'd use on a chino, the elastic waistband stops gripping, the ankle cuffs lose their snap, and the side stripes don't line up. That's the reason track pants get their own bench here.

The fabric has stretch — and stretch is the whole point

Track pants are cut from knit fabric with significant elastane content — usually 4 to 12% — which is what gives them the stretch and recovery that make them comfortable to run in, sit in, and lounge in. That stretch is not a side effect. It's the reason the garment exists. Which means: every seam we alter has to stretch with the fabric. A standard straight stitch on a stretch fabric will pop the first time the wearer squats. We use a stretch stitch or a cover-stitch on every seam we add to a track pant, and ballpoint needles so the needle pushes between the knit loops rather than cutting them. We also build a small amount of "give" into every new seam — because a track pant that has no room to stretch at the new seam will bind the moment the wearer moves, and it will fail at the seam within a few months.

The side stripe is a design feature — and it has to keep running straight

Most classic track pants — the Adidas three-stripe style, the school sports style, the retro athletics look — have a stripe or a set of stripes running down the outside of the leg. That stripe is usually tape-stitched onto the fabric, sometimes a woven tape, sometimes a heat-sealed strip. When you taper the leg, the stripe has to keep running continuously down the new seam line — not disappear into it, and not step sideways. If the taper is done carelessly, the stripe stops at the new seam and reappears below it at a different angle. Every taper job we do on a striped track pant starts with marking the stripe position before we cut. Same with side pockets on jogger-style track pants: the pocket has to stay in its intended position, not get dragged forward or back by the alteration.

The elastic ankle cuff — the piece that has to still grip

Almost every track pant ends in an elastic ankle cuff — a ribbed band that grips the ankle and keeps the pant leg from dragging on the ground. That cuff is knitted differently from the body of the trouser: it's a rib structure with a specific stretch percentage, usually 40 to 60% more than the body fabric. When we shorten a track pant, we shorten above the cuff — never through it — and re-attach the original cuff at the correct tension. If the tension is wrong, the cuff either grips too tightly (creating a bulge above it) or flops loose (creating a sagging hem). On track pants where the cuff has already failed — usually because the original elastic has gone slack — we replace the cuff with a matching rib knit, hand-linking the new rib to the pant leg at the same tension as the original.

The elastic waistband — the piece that fails first

Every track pant has an elastic waistband — often with a drawstring running through a separate channel — and that waistband is the piece that fails first. The elastic loses its spring within eighteen to twenty-four months of regular wear, and by two years the track pants will not stay up without a belt. Replacing the waistband elastic is one of the most common track pant repairs we do. The job involves opening the waistband channel from the inside, extracting the old elastic, threading new heavy-duty elastic through (usually a firmer gauge than the original), and re-stitching the channel so nothing shows on the outside. Same with drawstrings — when the drawstring has frayed or lost its aglets, we replace it with a heavier cord and metal or plastic aglets so it doesn't fray again.

Moisture-wicking and bonded finishes — the parts you can't stitch through

Higher-end track pants sometimes have additional technical layers — a moisture-wicking coating on the inside, a bonded seam at the pocket, a heat-sealed reflective strip. None of those can be stitched through the way plain fabric can. On a heat-sealed reflective strip, a needle hole leaves a permanent mark and the strip can peel. On a bonded pocket seam, stitching through breaks the bond. We identify those details before we start, and where we can't sew through them, we work around them — reshaping from other panels or using a hidden hand-sewn finish. This is one of the reasons a Nike Tech Fleece track pant costs more to alter than a basic poly-cotton track pant: the technical construction requires slower, more careful work.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 800 track pant hem with the ankle cuff preserved is a rescue — the pant keeps its shape, the cuff keeps its grip, and the pair goes back into weekly rotation for another year. The same KSh 800 on a track pant hemmed with a straight stitch that pops at the second wash is money wasted, because the pant will fail at the new seam and end up in the back of the drawer. We turn down track pant alterations every month — on pairs where the fabric has gone brittle from too many hot washes, on acrylic-heavy pairs where the alteration accelerates the pilling, on track pants where a previous alteration has already compromised the ankle cuff. On the pairs that can take it, the alteration pays for itself many times over. It's the same standard we hold on jogger set alterations and elastic replacement.

Not all track pants are the same job

Three track pant tiers — three completely different alterations

A KSh 1,500 market track pant and a KSh 12,000 Nike Tech Fleece require completely different handling. One is a poly-cotton knit that will age quickly; the other is a bonded technical fabric that can't be stitched the way ordinary fabric can. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be ruined. Here's how we categorise track pants before deciding what technique to use.

Entry tier

Poly-cotton and basic blend track pants

Everyday track pants from market stalls, school uniform suppliers and budget retail. Fabric weight 200–280 gsm. A poly-cotton knit, sometimes with a small amount of elastane. Rib ankle cuffs are usually thin and lose their grip within a year. Side stripes are printed rather than taped on cheaper pairs. Waistband elastic is usually 20–25 mm and softens quickly. Common in Nairobi retail under KSh 3,000. The everyday track pant.

What we do differentlyOn these we work quickly and pragmatically — the goal is to make the track pants fit, not to add years of life they can't support. We use a stretch stitch on every seam and reinforce the crotch and seat while the garment is open, because those are the two places cheap track pants fail first. When the ankle cuff has gone, we replace it with a firmer rib so it actually grips — replacing a soft cuff with another soft cuff just delays the failure by a few months.

Everyday tier

Mid-tier performance track pants

Better-made track pants from mid-tier brands — Adidas Essentials, Nike Club Fleece, Puma, mid-range Italian sportswear. Fabric weight 260–360 gsm. Poly-elastane or cotton-elastane blends with 4–8% elastane. Ribbed ankle cuffs are wider (5–6 cm) and hold their grip better. Side stripes are usually taped on rather than printed. Waistband elastic is 30–40 mm, sometimes with a drawstring and eyelets. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 3,000 and KSh 8,000. The everyday premium track pant.

What we do differentlyOn mid-tier track pants, we treat the garment like what it is — a performance piece worth altering properly. We use a fine-gauge needle at low tension, cover-stitch every visible hem, and preserve the taped side stripe by marking the stripe position before cutting. On track pants with a drawstring, we replace the drawstring with a heavier cord and metal aglets so it doesn't fray again. On these pairs, the preservation of the original ankle cuff is the whole job — a badly re-set cuff is visible within a week.

Premium tier

Technical and designer track pants

Nike Tech Fleece, Adidas Z.N.E., Lululemon, Gymshark and designer athletic trousers — plus high-end running trousers from Arc'teryx, Salomon and similar. Fabric weight 200–400 gsm. Sometimes bonded (thermally sealed) rather than stitched, with heat-sealed reflective strips, laser-cut ventilation, stretch panels and technical linings. Waistbands are often fused or bonded rather than stitched. Rib cuffs are technically engineered rather than plain rib. Common above KSh 8,000. The track pants you only alter once.

What we do differentlyOn this tier every alteration is done at low tension with the finest available needle, and every visible seam is finished to match the original construction. On bonded-seam track pants we re-stitch with a hidden seam rather than try to re-bond on the bench. On reflective strips we work around the strip rather than through it, because a needle hole is permanent. On Nike Tech Fleece and similar, we dry-press only — heat damages the coating that gives the fabric its water resistance.

If you're not sure which tier your track pants fall into, send a close-up photo of the fabric, the ankle cuff and the side stripe on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five parts of a track pant that affect the alteration

What a track pant actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a track pant as "a comfortable sports trouser." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a track pant has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a pair.

01

The knit body — performance fabric with elastane

The body of a track pant is a knit fabric with significant elastane content — usually 4 to 12% — that gives the garment its stretch and recovery. The knit is often a double-knit or interlock construction, denser than a t-shirt jersey and smoother than fleece. On technical track pants, the fabric can be bonded, with a water-resistant coating on the outside and a soft lining on the inside. This is the fabric that has to be respected: a standard straight stitch will pop on the first stretch, and a hot iron will melt the coating. Every seam we add is stitched with a stretch stitch or cover-stitch at low tension, and every track pant we alter is pressed through a muslin cloth — never with direct heat.

02

The elastic waistband and drawstring

Every track pant has an elastic waistband, sometimes with a drawstring running through a separate channel. The waistband construction is usually one of three types: a fully elasticated band, a half-elastic band (elastic at the back, fixed at the front), or a technical bonded waistband on higher-end track pants. When we take in a waist, we don't cut the waistband — we open the channel from the inside, shorten the elastic, and re-stitch the channel so nothing shows on the outside. Same with the drawstring: when it's frayed, we replace it with a heavier cord and metal aglets. The waistband is the piece that fails first on any track pant, and it's the piece most tailors alter badly because they try to reshape it like a woven waistband.

03

The side stripe and side seam

Classic track pants have a stripe running down the outside of the leg — a woven tape, a printed stripe, or a heat-sealed reflective strip depending on the tier. On jogger-style track pants, the side seam sometimes has a pocket or a panel that's set into the seam. When we taper a track pant leg, the stripe position and the pocket position have to be marked before cutting, and the new seam has to land so the stripe continues cleanly. On heat-sealed reflective strips, we work around the strip — a needle hole in reflective tape is permanent, and stitching through it can cause the tape to peel. Every striped track pant alteration we do starts with the stripe position drawn on with chalk and confirmed with the client before the first cut.

04

The leg taper — the athletic silhouette

Track pant legs taper from the thigh to the ankle — sometimes gently, sometimes aggressively (jogger style). The taper is what gives the pant its athletic look, and it's what most clients want to adjust when they bring in a pair that's too wide. When we slim a track pant, we follow the original taper rather than impose a new one, because the pant was designed to taper a specific way and pushing it further than the design allows makes the pant look modified. The knee is usually the widest point of the leg — anything we do below the knee has to preserve the natural drape that starts there. Tapering a stretch knit takes longer than tapering a woven trouser because every seam has to be stitched with the fabric still relaxed — otherwise the seam will pucker once the pant is worn.

05

The ankle cuff — the ribbed elastic finish

Almost every track pant ends in an elastic ankle cuff — a ribbed band that grips the ankle and stops the pant leg dragging on the ground. The cuff is knitted differently from the body of the trouser: it's a rib structure with 40–60% more stretch than the body fabric, and the tension of that rib is what makes the cuff grip correctly. When we shorten a track pant, we shorten above the cuff and re-attach the original cuff at the correct tension. When the cuff itself has failed, we source a matching rib and hand-link the new rib into the pant leg at the same stretch percentage as the original. Getting the tension wrong is the most common mistake we fix on track pant alterations — the cuff either grips too tightly or hangs loose, and neither is what the client asked for.

If a tailor quotes a track pant alteration without asking about the stripe, the ankle cuff or the fabric's elastane content, they're about to do a job that will look fine when you collect it and wrong the second time you wear it. We'd rather ask five questions upfront than take on a job we can't finish properly.

The fabric decides the technique

The five track pant fabrics we work with most often — and how each one behaves

The fabric decides how the alteration behaves under a needle, how it presses, and how it holds shape after the first wash. Here's our honest breakdown by fabric type, the same one we'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Poly-cotton blend — the everyday track pant fabric

The workhorse fabric of mass-market track pants. A poly-cotton knit with 2–4% elastane. Fabric weight 200–280 gsm. Resists wrinkling, washes well, holds colour. The trade-off is that poly-cotton ages faster than pure cotton — the fabric pills at friction points, and the elastic waistband softens quicker. Poly-cotton is forgiving to alter because it doesn't shrink much and doesn't react badly to a moderate press. We use a stretch stitch on every seam, press through a muslin cloth at medium heat, and reinforce the crotch and seat while the garment is open — the two places poly-cotton track pants fail first.

Poly-elastane performance fabric

The mid-tier performance fabric — 85–92% polyester with 8–15% elastane. This is the fabric of most running track pants, training track pants and mid-tier athletic trousers. Fabric weight 220–320 gsm. High stretch and recovery, quick-drying, wind-resistant. The elastane content makes this fabric easy to alter but demanding on the seam — a straight stitch will pop the first time the wearer moves. We use a stretch stitch or cover-stitch throughout, at low tension, and build a small amount of give into every new seam so the alteration doesn't restrict the stretch.

Nylon-elastane technical fabric

The fabric of premium running and training track pants — Arc'teryx, Salomon, high-end Nike. Fabric weight 180–260 gsm, sometimes bonded, sometimes with a water-repellent coating. Very high stretch and recovery, but also sensitive to heat — the coating can melt under an iron and the bonding can lift. We dry-press only on this fabric, use the finest available needle, and where the seams are bonded rather than stitched, we re-stitch with a hidden seam rather than try to re-bond on the bench.

Brushed fleece — the winter track pant fabric

Brushed fleece (also called "brushed-back fleece") is a knit fabric with a soft, brushed inner surface. Common on track pants designed for cold weather — the fabric weight is usually 300–400 gsm, and the brushed inner traps warmth. Fleece takes alterations well but pills if you over-stitch it — a heavy needle or too many stitch lines through the same area will pull the brushed surface into visible clumps. We use a fine-gauge needle and stitch slowly with even tension so the fleece stays smooth. Fleece also shrinks on the first hot wash — usually by 3–5% — so we cut with a small allowance on fresh fleece track pants.

French terry — the mid-weight athletic fabric

French terry has a smooth outer face and a looped inner face, giving it a smoother look and better shape retention than brushed fleece. Fabric weight 260–360 gsm. Common on mid-tier jogger-style track pants and on premium sweats. French terry responds beautifully to alteration — the looped inner surface hides minor stitch imperfections, and the flat outer face presses crisply. The only tricky part is that the terry fabric pulls at the seam if the tension isn't even, so we use a walking-foot machine for even feeding on every French terry track pant we alter.

What we won't alter

We turn down track pant alterations every month. On track pants where the fabric has gone brittle — usually acrylic-heavy pairs that have been through twenty hot washes — the alteration accelerates the failure and the pant starts to crack at the new seam within weeks. On heavily pilled poly-cotton track pants, the pilling doesn't stop when we alter — it concentrates at the new seam. On track pants where a previous alteration has already compromised the ankle cuff or the waistband channel, a second alteration often can't achieve a clean result. And on any track pant where the bonding on a heat-sealed seam is already lifting, stitching through the bonded area makes the lift worse. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money. It's the same standard we hold on jogger set alterations.

A short note from the bench

Why the fabric does most of the work on a track pant — and why that changes the alteration

Every other trouser that comes through my workshop is asking to be changed. A chino that's too loose needs to be tightened. A suit trouser that's too long needs to be shortened. A pair of jeans that's too wide needs to be tapered. The garment has to be altered to fit the wearer. Track pants are the opposite. A track pant already fits — because it stretches, recovers, breathes, and moves with the body. The fabric does most of the work. Our job is to change as little as possible while the client gets what they want — and to keep all the things the fabric was doing in the first place.

Here's what I mean by that. On a woven garment, you can stitch a straight seam anywhere, at any tension, and the garment will hold. On a track pant, the fabric is a knit with 4 to 12% elastane — it stretches every time the wearer moves. A straight stitch doesn't stretch. So the first time the wearer crouches or jumps, the thread holds while the fabric stretches, and the seam rips. This is the single most common mistake I fix on track pants that were altered somewhere else. The alteration looked fine on collection day and popped within a week. It didn't pop because the tailor was lazy. It popped because the tailor stitched it like a chino. Every seam we add to a track pant is stitched with a stretch stitch or a cover-stitch at low tension, on a ballpoint needle, and every track pant we alter gets a small "give" built into the new seam so it moves with the fabric instead of fighting it.

The second thing is the ankle cuff. That ribbed band at the bottom of every track pant is not just a finish — it's the piece that grips the ankle, stops the leg from dragging on the ground, and gives the pant its athletic silhouette. It's knitted differently from the body of the trouser: it's a rib structure with 40 to 60 percent more stretch than the surrounding fabric, and the tension of that stretch is what makes the cuff grip correctly. When we shorten a track pant, we don't cut through the cuff. We un-link the cuff from the leg, shorten the leg, and re-link the cuff back at the same tension. Getting the tension right is fiddly. Getting it wrong is visible within a week. On the pairs where the cuff has already failed, we source a matching rib knit and hand-link it at the correct percentage, because a soft cuff replaced with another soft cuff will fail again in six months.

The third thing is the side stripe. Most classic track pants have a stripe running down the outside of the leg — Adidas three-stripe style, or the retro athletics look. That stripe is a design feature, and if the alteration doesn't preserve it, the pant looks wrong even if the fit is perfect. When we taper a striped track pant, we mark the stripe position with chalk before we cut, and we place the new seam so the stripe continues cleanly down the leg at the same angle. On taped stripes, that's a slow job — the stripe has to be re-stitched at the correct tension on both sides of the new seam. On heat-sealed reflective strips, it's different — a needle hole in reflective tape is permanent, so we work around the strip rather than through it. If the only way to hit the fit the client wants is to cut through the strip, we say so upfront and let the client decide. That's the honest answer, and it's better than charging for a job that ruins the pant.

I turn down track pant alterations every month. Usually because the fabric has gone brittle from too many hot washes — the fibre has lost its elasticity, and the alteration will accelerate the failure rather than improve the fit. Sometimes because a previous alteration has compromised the ankle cuff or the waistband channel so badly that a second alteration won't achieve a clean result. And on some heavily pilled poly-cotton pairs, because the pilling doesn't stop when we alter — it concentrates at the new seam and the pant looks worse after a month than it did before. In all those cases, the honest answer is that the client is better off replacing the pair than paying me to alter a garment that can't take it. I'd rather say that than take the money.

Fourteen years of doing this has taught me that track pants are the most useful trousers in any wardrobe. Not the smartest, not the most formal — the most useful. The pair you wear on a Saturday morning. The pair you take to the gym. The pair that gets worn more than any other trouser you own. That's why they deserve a proper alteration. Not the fastest alteration — the right one. The one that keeps the fabric doing its job, keeps the cuffs gripping, keeps the stripes running straight. That's the work — helping a track pant stay the track pant the client actually wants to wear.

Make your track pants last between alterations

Eight habits that keep track pants out of the workshop

Track pants are the most-worn sportswear garment in most wardrobes — and the one that ages fastest if the care is wrong. Most of the damage we repair in the workshop was preventable. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect an altered pair.

1. Wash cold, always cold

Heat is the biggest killer of track pants. Hot water softens the elastane in the fabric, degrades the elastic in the waistband and cuffs, and dulls the colour. Over twenty or thirty hot washes, the garment gradually loses its stretch, the waistband sags, and the ankle cuffs stop gripping — and that's the single biggest reason track pants end up on our bench. Cold water — 30°C or below — preserves the fabric and the elastic far longer. Wash on a gentle cycle or by hand with a mesh bag to protect the fabric from over-agitation.

2. Turn them inside out before washing

Track pants fade and pill from the outside in, so turning them inside out protects the visible surface. This matters most on dark poly-cotton track pants where the outer surface pills fastest, and on technical track pants where the outer coating can be worn away by friction in the drum. Wash with similar colours and don't overfill the drum. If your track pants have a drawstring, tie it loosely before washing so it doesn't tangle or pull through.

3. Air dry flat — never tumble

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shrink a track pant and kill the elastic. The heat softens the rubber, the tumbling stresses the fibres, and the result is a waistband that goes slack within a few months. Air dry instead — laid flat on a rack, or hung over the back of a chair. If you must use a dryer, use the lowest heat setting and remove the pieces while still slightly damp. Never hang track pants by the drawstring — the whole weight of the wet garment on one cord stretches the eyelet holes and eventually tears them.

4. Depill with a proper fabric shaver

Pilling is normal on poly-cotton track pants — surface fibres tangle as they rub against each other. Remove pills with a fabric shaver or a knitwear comb. Never use a disposable razor — it cuts the fabric itself, not just the pills, and creates thin patches that eventually open into holes. Depill gently, in one direction, with the track pant laid flat on a table. Ten minutes of depilling every few months adds a year to the life of the garment and keeps the fabric looking smooth rather than worn.

5. Rotate between two or three pairs

Knit fabric needs 24 hours to recover its shape between wears. Wear the same track pants every day and the elastic stays stretched, the cuffs stay compressed, and the fabric ages twice as fast as it should. If you own two or three pairs and rotate them, each one gets worn a third as often — which roughly triples its life. This single habit alone roughly doubles the working life of the waistband elastic, which is the piece that fails first.

6. Don't stretch the drawstring when putting on or removing

The drawstring eyelets on track pants are reinforced, but not indestructible. Pulling the drawstring hard every time you put the pants on or take them off stretches the eyelet holes and eventually tears through the fabric. Loosen the drawstring before you put the pants on; adjust it once they're on. When you take them off, loosen it again. This one habit keeps the eyelet area in good condition for years, which is the difference between a KSh 400 drawstring replacement and a KSh 1,000 waistband channel rebuild.

7. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose stitch at the ankle cuff is a KSh 400 reinforcement today. Left for three months, the cuff loses its shape and needs a full rib replacement at KSh 1,200. A drawstring starting to fray is a KSh 400 fix; left alone, the aglets come off and eventually the cord frays through, requiring a full drawstring replacement. A small tear at the side pocket is a KSh 500 repair now; left alone, it becomes a KSh 800 pocket rebuild. The clients who spend the least on track pant repairs are the ones who message us the first time they notice something small.

8. Store folded, not hanging

Hanging track pants by the waistband — especially on a thin hanger — stretches the elastic and pulls the fabric out of shape over a few months. Fold track pants flat in a drawer or on a shelf. Stack them loosely rather than tightly, so the knees and cuffs don't take on permanent creases. If you're storing a technical fabric pair long-term, use a breathable cotton garment bag rather than a plastic one — plastic traps moisture and degrades the coating on the fabric.

Follow those eight habits and your track pants will hold their shape and stretch for years. And when they do eventually need work — a hem, a new waistband, a cuff replacement — we're one WhatsApp message away.
