The most detail-heavy coat in menswear

Why a trenchcoat alteration is more like maintenance work than tailoring work

Think about every other coat you own. A wool overcoat has a collar, sleeves, a hem, buttons, maybe a vent. A trenchcoat — a proper one, cut to the classic pattern — has all of those plus five extra functional details that most coats don't have: a belt with a buckle, a storm flap across the chest, a throat latch at the collar, D-rings at the waist, and a gun flap on the right shoulder. Every one of those details serves a specific purpose originally, and every one of them has to survive the alteration. That's what makes trenchcoat work different from any other coat on the bench.

The belt is the trenchcoat's spine — and it has to keep working

The belt on a trenchcoat is not decoration. It cinches the waist, holds the coat closed against wind, and gives the classic trench its silhouette. Most trenchcoat belts are attached at the waist with either fixed belt loops or a belt keeper system, and the buckle is a specific hardware piece — usually brass or gunmetal with a prong-style closure, sometimes a D-ring or a horn toggle on modern designer trenches. When you alter the waist on a trenchcoat, the belt has to be re-fitted to the new waist measurement or shortened independently. When the belt is missing or has been cut, sourcing a matching replacement buckle is one of the harder sourcing jobs we do — because vintage trench buckles are almost never produced again. We keep a small library of common replacement buckles on hand, and for unusual ones we tell the client the honest truth about whether we can source a match.

The storm flap — a functional layer that can't be cut through

A proper trenchcoat has a storm flap — a panel of fabric that runs vertically down the chest, over the button placket, designed to overlap and shed rain away from the front of the coat. On the classic Burberry and Aquascutum patterns, the storm flap is stitched at the shoulder and free-hanging at the bottom, so it moves slightly with the wearer but always covers the buttons. When you slim the body of a trenchcoat, the storm flap cannot be cut through — if you remove it or reshape it, the coat loses the front protection that makes it a trenchcoat at all. Every body slimming job we do on a trenchcoat starts by measuring the storm flap position and planning the alteration so the flap stays where it was, on its original line.

The throat latch — small piece, high stress point

The throat latch is a small tab of fabric, usually fastened with a button or a snap, that closes the collar up around the throat. It sits at the front of the collar, which is the highest-stress point on the coat because it takes movement every time the wearer turns their head. The latch is usually held by a single button or a snap, and both of those fail over time — the button comes loose, the snap loosens its grip, and the latch becomes unusable. Replacing a throat latch button is a KSh 500 fix. Replacing the latch itself, if it's been torn out, is a KSh 800 job because the collar has to be partially opened to re-anchor the latch at the correct point. Small, but it's one of the details that separates a well-maintained trenchcoat from one that looks tired.

The D-rings and gun flap — the details most clients don't notice

Classic trenchcoats have two D-rings attached at the waist on either side, originally designed to hold a map case or a small bag, and a gun flap on the right shoulder — a flap of fabric stitched across the shoulder that was originally designed to shed rain away from the chest. On modern trenchcoats, the D-rings are often decorative and the gun flap has been simplified, but on proper vintage trenchcoats and modern designer trenchcoats, both of these are functional and stitched in a way that has to be preserved. When we alter a trenchcoat with D-rings, we re-anchor them after the waist alteration so they sit at the same height relative to the belt. When we shorten a sleeve on a trenchcoat with a gun flap, we work the sleeve from the cuff end only — never through the shoulder — because cutting through the gun flap stitching destroys the flap.

The fabric — cotton gabardine, and why it hates heat

Classic trenchcoats are cut from cotton gabardine — a tightly woven twill that was specifically invented for the trenchcoat in the early 20th century because it sheds rain without a coating. Modern trenchcoats are often cut from poly-cotton blends or from technical waterproof fabrics (coated nylon, bonded polyester, sometimes with a membrane between layers). Cotton gabardine takes a press beautifully, but it goes shiny the moment a hot iron touches it directly. Technical waterproof fabrics hate heat even more — a hot iron will melt the coating, lift the bonding, and permanently mark the fabric. Every trenchcoat we alter is pressed through a muslin cloth at medium heat, and on technical fabric we dry-press only.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 2,000 sleeve shortening on a KSh 40,000 Burberry trenchcoat is a rescue — the coat keeps its shape, the cuff strap keeps its function, and the coat goes back into regular rotation for another decade. The same KSh 2,000 on a coat altered by someone who cuts through the storm flap, replaces the original belt buckle with a generic one, and presses the gabardine to a shine is money wasted, because the coat will never look right again. Trenchcoat alterations are one of the few garment jobs where the "before" and "after" difference is invisible — the client gets back a coat that fits but that also still looks like the coat they bought. That's the standard we hold every trenchcoat to. It's the same standard we hold on overcoat alterations and blazer alterations.

Not all trenchcoats are the same job

Three trenchcoat tiers — three completely different alterations

A KSh 4,000 poly-cotton trenchcoat from a Nairobi market and a KSh 90,000 Burberry trenchcoat from Milan require completely different handling. One is a lightweight fashion piece; the other is a piece of engineering that has been refined for over a century. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be ruined. Here's how we categorise trenchcoats before deciding what technique to use.

Entry tier

Poly-cotton and budget trenchcoats

Mass-produced trenchcoats from market stalls, budget retail and fast fashion. Fabric weight 220–320 gsm, usually a poly-cotton blend with a light water-repellent finish. Belt and buckle are usually plastic or lightweight alloy. Storm flap is often just a decorative seam. Throat latch is often missing entirely. D-rings are usually plastic and non-functional. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 3,000 and KSh 8,000. The everyday trenchcoat.

What we do differentlyOn these we work pragmatically — the goal is to make the coat fit, not to restore engineering that wasn't there in the first place. We use standard stitches on plain cotton-poly seams, press through a muslin cloth at medium heat, and preserve whatever functional details the coat does have (a working buckle, a real belt). Where the belt buckle is plastic and has failed, we can source a lightweight replacement — but we tell the client upfront that a plastic trenchcoat is worth a plastic buckle, not a brass one.

Everyday tier

Mid-tier and better fashion trenchcoats

Better-crafted trenchcoats from mid-tier retail — Zara, Massimo Dutti, Banana Republic, and mid-range Italian makers. Fabric weight 280–380 gsm. 100% cotton gabardine or a higher-grade poly-cotton. Real metal hardware — usually gunmetal or brass-plated buckles and buttons. Functioning storm flap. Working throat latch. Real belt with a proper prong closure. D-rings sometimes present and functional. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 8,000 and KSh 25,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier, we treat the coat like what it is — a proper piece of outerwear worth altering carefully. We hand-finish any visible seam, preserve the storm flap position, re-anchor the throat latch at the correct point, and re-set the D-rings at the same height after waist work. On poly-cotton blends with a water-repellent finish, we press through a muslin cloth at lower heat to preserve the coating. On cotton gabardine, we steam the press line out instead of pressing flat, so the fabric doesn't go shiny.

Premium tier

Classic and designer trenchcoats

Burberry, Aquascutum, Mackintosh, and designer trenchcoats from high-end Italian, British and Japanese makers. Fabric weight 320–450 gsm — dense cotton gabardine, sometimes with a hand-finished water-repellent treatment. Full brass hardware with a signature buckle pattern. Working storm flap with a functional gun flap. Full throat latch with a leather or horn button. Functional D-rings in brass or gunmetal. Sometimes hand-finished at the collar and cuff. Common above KSh 40,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier every alteration is done by hand where the original was hand-finished. We re-anchor D-rings by hand, preserve the gun flap without cutting, re-set the throat latch at the original hand-stitched point, and match any thread to the original colour and gauge. On cotton gabardine we hand-press over a ham and let the cloth settle for 24 hours. On Burberry and Aquascutum specifically, we source hardware matches from specialist suppliers, because the original buckles are part of the coat's identity. A careless alteration on a KSh 90,000 trenchcoat is a KSh 90,000 mistake.

If you're not sure which tier your trenchcoat falls into, send a close-up photo of the belt buckle, the storm flap and the inside label on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five functional details that affect every trenchcoat alteration

What a trenchcoat actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a trenchcoat as "a long coat with a belt." That description misses the four other functional details that make a trenchcoat a trenchcoat — and that have to survive the alteration. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a trenchcoat.

01

The belt and buckle — the functional waist closure

The belt is the single most important functional detail on a trenchcoat. It cinches the waist, holds the coat closed against wind, and gives the coat its classic silhouette. The belt is usually 4 to 5 cm wide, cut from the same fabric as the coat, and fastened with a prong buckle in brass, gunmetal or a plated alloy. On designer trenchcoats, the buckle is often a signature piece — a specific pattern of brass or a specific finish that identifies the maker. When we alter the waist on a trenchcoat, the belt either has to be re-fitted to the new waist measurement, shortened independently, or left untouched if the waist alteration doesn't affect the belt line. When the belt is missing or has been cut, sourcing a matching replacement is one of the harder jobs we do — we keep a small library of common buckles on hand, and for unusual ones we tell the client the honest truth about sourcing.

02

The storm flap — the front chest protective layer

The storm flap is a panel of fabric that runs vertically down the chest, over the button placket, designed to shed rain away from the front of the coat. On a proper trenchcoat it's stitched at the shoulder and free-hanging at the bottom, so it moves slightly with the wearer but always covers the buttons. When we slim the body of a trenchcoat, the storm flap cannot be cut through — the alteration has to be planned so the flap stays exactly where it was, on its original line. When we shorten a trenchcoat, the storm flap is one of the pieces we check first, because a shorter coat with a full-length storm flap looks wrong even if the length itself is correct.

03

The throat latch — the high-stress collar closure

The throat latch is a small tab of fabric, usually fastened with a button or a snap, that closes the collar up around the throat. It sits at the front of the collar, which is the highest-stress point on the coat because it takes movement every time the wearer turns their head. The latch is usually held by a single button or a snap, and both of those fail over time — the button comes loose, the snap loosens, the leather tab stiffens. Replacing a throat latch button is a KSh 500 fix. Replacing the latch itself, if the tab has torn, is a KSh 800 job because the collar has to be partially opened to re-anchor the latch at the correct point.

04

The D-rings and gun flap — the shoulder and waist details

Classic trenchcoats have two D-rings attached at the waist on either side, originally designed to hold a map case or a small bag, and a gun flap on the right shoulder — a flap of fabric stitched across the shoulder that was originally designed to shed rain away from the chest. On modern trenchcoats, the D-rings are often decorative and the gun flap has been simplified, but on proper vintage and modern designer trenchcoats, both are functional and stitched in a way that has to be preserved. When we alter a trenchcoat with D-rings, we re-anchor them at the same height relative to the belt. When we shorten a sleeve on a trenchcoat with a gun flap, we work the sleeve from the cuff end only — never through the shoulder — because cutting through the gun flap stitching destroys the flap.

05

The cuff strap and the sleeve detail

Most trenchcoat sleeves end in a cuff strap — a small tab of fabric that wraps around the wrist and fastens with a buckle or a snap, allowing the sleeve to be cinched tight against rain. The cuff strap is usually 2 to 3 cm wide, sometimes with a matching metal buckle, and it's stitched to the sleeve at the wrist. When we shorten a trenchcoat sleeve, the cuff strap has to be removed, the sleeve shortened, and the cuff strap re-attached at the same distance from the cuff edge. Getting this wrong is the most common mistake we fix on trenchcoat sleeve alterations — the strap either ends up too close to the cuff edge (making it unusable) or is positioned at a different height than the original (making the two sleeves look mismatched).

If a tailor quotes a trenchcoat alteration without asking about the belt, the storm flap or the cuff straps, they're about to do a job that will look right on collection day and wrong every day after. We'd rather ask five questions upfront than take on a job we can't finish properly.

The fabric decides the technique

The five trenchcoat fabrics we work with most often — and how each one behaves

The fabric decides how the alteration behaves under a needle, how it presses, and how it holds shape after the first rain. Here's our honest breakdown by fabric type, the same one we'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Cotton gabardine — the classic trenchcoat fabric

The original trenchcoat fabric — a tightly woven cotton twill with a smooth, dense hand. Gabardine was invented by Thomas Burberry in the 1870s specifically because it shed rain without a coating, and it's still the standard for classic trenchcoats. Fabric weight 280–400 gsm. Takes a press beautifully but goes shiny the moment a hot iron touches it directly. Every trenchcoat we alter in gabardine is pressed through a muslin cloth at medium heat, and the press line is steamed rather than pressed flat. Gabardine also ages beautifully — a well-cared-for gabardine trenchcoat develops a soft patina that coated fabrics can't replicate.

Poly-cotton blend — the everyday trenchcoat fabric

The workhorse fabric of mass-market trenchcoats. A poly-cotton twill with a light water-repellent finish. Fabric weight 220–320 gsm. Resists wrinkling, washes well, holds colour. The trade-off is that poly-cotton ages faster than pure cotton — the fabric pills at friction points, the water-repellent coating wears off within a couple of seasons, and the fabric can go shiny if pressed too hot. Poly-cotton is forgiving to alter but demands moderate heat and a muslin cloth throughout. We also check the water-repellent finish before altering — a heavy press can strip the coating and leave the coat without rain protection.

Technical waterproof fabric — coated nylon, bonded polyester

The fabric of modern performance and designer trenchcoats. Fabric weight 180–260 gsm. Coated nylon or bonded polyester with a water-repellent top layer and often a membrane between two fabric layers. This is the most heat-sensitive fabric in any trenchcoat — a hot iron melts the coating, lifts the bonding, and permanently marks the fabric. Every alteration on technical trench fabric is dry-pressed only, with the finest available needle at low tension. Where the seams are bonded rather than stitched, we re-stitch with a hidden seam rather than try to re-bond on the bench.

Wool-blend trenchcoats — the winter variation

A smaller category but one that shows up on the bench regularly — trenchcoats cut from a wool-cotton or wool-poly blend for cold-weather use. Fabric weight 380–500 gsm. Heavier than gabardine and warmer, but the classic trench details are usually preserved (belt, storm flap, throat latch). Wool blends respond beautifully to steam and press crisply through a muslin cloth. The trade-off is weight — a wool-blend trench is heavy, so alterations have to account for the coat hanging differently on the body than a lightweight gabardine. Every seam we add on a wool-blend trench is reinforced against the extra weight.

Coated poplin and oilcloth — the vintage and heritage tier

Old-fashioned oilcloth and coated poplin trenchcoats — sometimes vintage, sometimes reproduced by heritage makers. Fabric weight 300–400 gsm. The coating is usually a wax or an oil finish that gives the coat its water resistance but also makes it stiff and sensitive to heat. We work on these very carefully — dry-press only, hand-finish every seam, and never apply heat directly to the coated surface. If the coating has cracked or flaked, we tell the client honestly that the coat may not be alterable without the coating failure showing at the new seam.

What we won't alter

We turn down trenchcoat alterations every month. On trenchcoats where the water-repellent coating has already failed across the whole coat — flaking off in patches, leaving a visible layer of dust on the fabric — stitching through the coated area will make the failure worse, and the coat is usually better off replaced than altered. On very old trenchcoats where the lining has already been replaced once and the storm flap has been simplified by a previous tailor, we're cautious about further alterations. On technical trenchcoats where the bonding has begun to lift at the seams, a further alteration accelerates the failure. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money. It's the same standard we hold on re-lining services.

A short note from the bench

The five things on a trenchcoat that can never be cut through

I once watched a Nairobi tailor ruin a Burberry trenchcoat in about twenty minutes. The client wanted the body slimmed. The tailor took in both side seams without checking anything else, and by the time he was finished, the storm flap — that vertical panel of fabric that runs down the chest of every proper trenchcoat — was sitting at an angle, the D-rings no longer sat at the same height as the belt, and the throat latch button was visible from the wrong side of the collar when the coat was closed. The client paid. The coat was never worn again.

Trenchcoats are unusual because they're the only coat in menswear that was designed around a set of specific functional details. Every one of those details originally served a purpose in the mud and rain of World War I — the storm flap shed rain away from the buttons, the throat latch closed the collar against wind, the D-rings held a map case, the gun flap on the right shoulder shed rain away from the rifle position, and the belt cinched the waist to keep the coat from billowing. Most of those details are decorative on a modern trenchcoat, but the ones that are still functional are the ones that define the coat as a trenchcoat. Cut through one of them, and you don't have a trenchcoat anymore. You have a long coat that used to be a trenchcoat.

So when I alter a trenchcoat, I don't start with scissors. I start with a list — five things on the coat that cannot be cut through. The storm flap position is number one. If we slim the body, the storm flap has to stay exactly where it was, on its original line, or the coat no longer closes properly. The throat latch attachment point is number two — if we touch the collar, the latch has to be re-anchored at the same precise point, or the coat will feel closed when it should be open and vice versa. The D-rings are number three — after any waist alteration, we re-anchor them at the same height relative to the belt, because on a proper trenchcoat those D-rings sit at a specific height and moving them throws off the whole waist detail. The gun flap is number four — on the shoulder, the gun flap is stitched at a specific angle, and if we shorten a sleeve through the shoulder we cut through the flap. And the cuff strap is number five — when we shorten a sleeve, the strap has to come off, the sleeve has to come up, and the strap has to go back on at exactly the same distance from the cuff edge as the original. Not a millimetre more, not a millimetre less.

Here's the honest part. Most Nairobi tailors alter trenchcoats without checking any of those five things. They open the side seams, reshape the coat, stitch it back up, and hand it back to the client. The coat fits better — but the storm flap doesn't sit right, the throat latch is off-centre, and the cuff straps are at the wrong height. The client notices something feels different but can't quite name it, and the coat quietly stops getting worn. The reason we charge KSh 3,000 for body slimming instead of KSh 1,500 is that we check and re-anchor all of these. That extra work — the storm flap measurement, the D-ring re-anchoring, the throat latch confirmation, the cuff strap positioning — adds about ninety minutes per coat. But it's the difference between a coat that gets worn for another decade and one that ends up in the back of a cupboard.

The other thing clients should know is about the fabric. Cotton gabardine is the original trenchcoat fabric — Burberry invented it specifically for trenchcoats because it sheds rain without a coating. But gabardine goes shiny under a direct hot iron, and once that shine appears it never comes out. So every gabardine trenchcoat I alter is pressed through a muslin cloth at medium heat, and the press lines are steamed instead of pressed flat. On technical waterproof trenchcoats — coated nylon, bonded polyester — the rule is even stricter: dry-press only, no exceptions. A hot iron on a technical trenchcoat melts the coating, lifts the bonding, and marks the fabric permanently. When a client sends me a technical trenchcoat for alteration, I always tell them upfront that the work will be slower than on a gabardine coat, because the pressing itself takes three times as long.

Fourteen years of altering outerwear has taught me that a trenchcoat is the most personal coat a man can own. Not the most expensive, not the most formal — the most personal. A trenchcoat you buy and wear for a decade is a coat that has been through your life with you: it's been to interviews, it's been through specific winters, it's been to specific cities. When I alter a trenchcoat, I'm not just making it fit the wearer. I'm keeping all the details that made that specific coat worth wearing in the first place. That's the standard — the coat should fit you, and it should still be the coat you bought.

Make your trenchcoat last between alterations

Eight habits that keep trenchcoats out of the workshop

A trenchcoat is the most useful coat in any Nairobi wardrobe — light enough for a cool morning, heavy enough for a wet afternoon, formal enough for a meeting. It's also the coat most clients own for the longest. A well-cared-for trenchcoat lasts ten to fifteen years, and most of what wears it out early is preventable. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect an altered trenchcoat.

1. Hang, never fold — on a wide hanger

A trenchcoat should be hung, not folded, and always on a wide wooden or shaped hanger that supports the full width of the shoulders. Folding a trenchcoat for storage leaves permanent creases down the storm flap and the belt line — and on a gabardine coat those creases never fully come out. A wide hanger distributes the weight across the shoulders and keeps the coat's drape intact. On a heavy trenchcoat, a thin wire hanger will eventually pull the shoulders out of shape. Use a wide one. It costs KSh 500 and saves the shoulder of a KSh 40,000 coat.

2. Brush the hem and storm flap after every wear

A trenchcoat's hem and storm flap collect more dust than any other part of the coat because they're the highest-contact points with the outside world. A soft clothes brush, used along the hem and down the storm flap after every wear, keeps the fabric looking clean and stops the dust from working into the weave. This matters most on cotton gabardine, where embedded dust dulls the colour over a year. Two minutes per coat, after every wear. It's the single most important habit for keeping a trenchcoat looking new.

3. Dry clean sparingly — twice a year is plenty

Dry cleaning is hard on the water-repellent finish on gabardine. Every dry clean strips a little more of the DWR coating off the fabric, and after four or five cleans the coat stops beading water. On a trenchcoat worn regularly through Nairobi's rainy months, one dry clean at the end of the season is usually enough — plus spot cleaning between for obvious marks. Between dry cleans, brush the coat down and air it on a hanger for a few hours. Every unnecessary dry clean shortens the life of the water-repellent finish by one cycle.

4. Reproof the coat before the rainy season — not after

Reproofing a trenchcoat works best as a preventative measure, not a repair. Before Nairobi's long rains in April and the short rains in October, spray your gabardine trenchcoat with a DWR reproofer or use a wash-in treatment on poly-cotton coats. This restores the water-beading quality that makes a trenchcoat genuinely rain-resistant. If you wait until the coat is soaking through, the fabric has already started absorbing water into the weave, and the finish is harder to restore. We offer water-repellent reproofing at KSh 1,000 for a full coat.

5. Loosen the belt before hanging and before washing

When a trenchcoat is stored or washed with the belt cinched tight, the belt presses a permanent line into the waist of the coat. That line stays visible even after the coat is worn loose again. Always loosen the belt buckle and let the belt hang loosely before hanging the coat or putting it in the wash. The same rule applies to the cuff straps on the sleeves — loosen them before storage so they don't press a permanent mark into the sleeve. Small habit, big difference over a decade.

6. Don't cinch the belt too tightly when wearing

The belt on a trenchcoat is designed to cinch the waist — but not to be pulled as tight as a leather belt on trousers. Pulling the belt too tight stresses the belt loops, distorts the D-rings, and eventually tears the belt attachment points. If you feel the belt digging in, it's too tight. A trenchcoat should sit on the waist with a slight drape, not clamp it. This one habit alone roughly doubles the life of the belt loops and the D-rings.

7. Store in a breathable garment bag, not plastic

Never store a trenchcoat in a plastic dry-cleaner's bag — plastic traps moisture against the fabric and creates exactly the environment that mildew loves. On gabardine and wool-blend trenchcoats, mildew shows up as small dark spots that are difficult to remove without damaging the water-repellent finish. Use a breathable cotton or canvas garment bag, or a clean cotton pillowcase if you don't have one. If the coat is being stored long-term (between cool seasons), put a cedar block or lavender sachet in each pocket — moths eat gabardine.

8. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose throat latch button is a KSh 500 fix today. Left for three months, the tab pulls out of the collar and the whole collar has to be re-opened at KSh 800. A hem edge starting to fray is a KSh 2,500 hem re-set today; left for a year, it becomes a KSh 3,000+ hem rebuild with fabric reinforcement. A belt starting to separate at the buckle is a KSh 700 fix; left alone, the belt is gone and a new belt with matching buckle starts at KSh 2,500. The clients who spend the least on trenchcoat maintenance are the ones who message us the first time they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less, and they preserve more of the original coat.

Follow those eight habits and your trenchcoat will hold its shape and its water resistance for a decade or more. And when it does eventually need work — a sleeve shortened, a belt rebuilt, a lining replaced — we're one WhatsApp message away.
