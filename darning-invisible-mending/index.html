Five kinds of damage. Five different repairs.

What kind of damage has your garment taken?

Darning is not one technique — it's a family of techniques. A moth hole in cashmere, a cigarette burn on a wool suit jacket, and a split seam on a cotton shirt need three completely different approaches. Below is the damage library I work from — the same one I'd walk you through on WhatsApp if you sent a photo. Tap a card to go to the relevant service page or ask about it directly.

01 🦋

Moth holes

Small, irregular holes in wool, cashmere and knitwear — usually found in storage, often in a cluster along a fold.

Fixed by re-weaving or fine darning. The goal is that the hole disappears into the surrounding fabric direction.

Ask David about moth holes → 02 🔥

Cigarette burns

A small singed hole, often on the front of a suit jacket or the sleeve of a blazer. The burn also scorches the fabric around it.

The surrounding scorched area must be treated first, then the hole is re-woven. Doing this in the wrong order leaves a shadow.

Ask David about burn repair → 03 ⚡

Tears and rips

A split that follows the weave of the fabric — caught on a nail, a door handle, or pulled in the wash. On thinner fabric, it can run.

Tears are re-woven along the original thread direction. On structured jackets, the underlying canvas must be reinforced first.

Ask David about tear repair → 04 🪡

Seam splitting

The stitching has given way along a seam, usually at a stress point — the crotch of trousers, the underarm of a jacket, or the seat of trousers.

Hand-stitched back along the original seam line so it's undetectable, then reinforced at the point of failure so it doesn't split again.

Ask David about seam repair → 05 🧵

Wear-through

Not a hole yet — the fabric has thinned and is about to fail. Elbows of shirts, seats of trousers, cuffs of jackets. Caught early, most wear-through can be saved.

The thinned area is reinforced internally, then re-woven on the surface. If you catch it before the hole opens, the repair is invisible.

Every fabric demands its own mend

The five fabrics I repair most often — and how each one behaves

Nobody teaches you that mending is really a conversation with the fabric. Each one behaves differently under a needle. Here is what I've learned across fourteen years, fabric by fabric — practical, not textbook.

Wool and wool blends

Wool is the most forgiving fabric to repair because its fibres have natural crimp and hold moisture. When I re-weave a moth hole in wool, I work with the direction of the fibres — the weave has a nap, a slight direction to the pile. If I stitch across it, the repair shows. If I work with it, the mend disappears. On suit jackets, I also check the underlying canvas — a moth hole that has penetrated through to the canvas needs the canvas reinforced first. Skip that step and the hole reopens within a year.

Cashmere and fine wool

Cashmere is the most delicate fabric I work with and also the most rewarding. The fibre is short and soft, which means it takes very fine thread and very small stitches — smaller than a standard sewing machine can produce. Every cashmere mend I do is hand-finished, usually under a bright lamp, using the same fibre weight as the original yarn. A good cashmere mend, done by hand, adds five years to the garment. Done badly, it looks like a scar. If you own a cashmere sweater that's been damaged, send it to someone who will actually take the time.

Cotton and cotton blends

Cotton is the workhorse fabric — shirts, chinos, uniforms, bedsheets. Cotton repairs are usually about resisting further tearing rather than hiding the mend completely, because cotton takes a slightly visible stitch even with perfect thread matching. On quality cotton garments, I use the same overlock-then-darn technique that lets the repair blend into the pattern. On everyday cottons, I focus on making the repair strong, flat and durable — because a visible but strong repair is worth more than an invisible one that fails in a month.

Silk and silk blends

Silk is the fabric that scares most tailors away — and that's understandable. Silk marks permanently under a needle, it snags if you breathe on it wrong, and the weave is tighter than wool, so re-weaving is a slower process. When I repair a silk shirt or a silk scarf, I work in daylight, with fine needles, under a muslin press cloth. On silk, the difference between a good mend and a ruined garment is roughly one second of careless needlework. If your silk garment has been damaged, tell me the exact fibre content when you message — the technique changes depending on the blend.

Knitwear and knitted garments

Knitwear is a different craft from woven fabric. Where woven fabric has warp and weft, knitwear has loops — and every repair has to recreate those loops, one by one. This is what most people call "darning" — the traditional woven basket pattern that shows on the reverse of the mend. On chunky knits, the darning pattern can be beautiful in its own right. On fine gauge knitwear, the mend has to disappear into the original knit structure. Hand-darning a knitwear hole takes me between forty-five minutes and three hours, depending on the size and gauge. It's slow work. It's also the only way it looks right.

Denim and heavy cotton twill

Denim repairs are usually about honesty rather than invisibility. A well-repaired pair of jeans shows the mend — that's part of the charm. But the repair still has to be structurally sound: reinforcing the surrounding fabric so the tear doesn't run, then weaving the torn edges back together using matching thread and the correct direction of the twill line. On premium Japanese denim, I use tonal thread to match the original stitching. On everyday jeans, a slightly visible repair is more durable and more honest.

If you don't know what your garment is made of, send a close-up photo of the fabric on WhatsApp. I'll identify the weave and tell you honestly what's possible.

An honest test — not every garment is worth repairing

Six questions I ask myself before I take on a repair

Mending is skilled, slow work and it costs money. Before I quote you, I ask myself whether the repair actually makes sense — for the garment, and for you. If the honest answer is no, I'll tell you. Here's the test.

01

Is the surrounding fabric still sound?A small hole in healthy fabric is a good candidate for invisible mending. A small hole in fabric that has already gone thin and grey around the edges will just move — you'll mend it today and it will tear again in three months. If the fabric is fatigued, I say so.

02

Is the garment worth the repair cost?A KSh 3,500 invisible mend on a KSh 4,000 suit jacket doesn't make sense. The same mend on a KSh 45,000 Italian wool jacket is excellent value. I'll always tell you which side of that line your garment falls on. Some clients say "it's sentimental" — that changes the calculation entirely, and I respect it.

03

Will the repair hold under stress?A torn underarm seam will face repeated stress when you raise your arms. A hole in the seat of trousers will face repeated stress when you sit. In these places, the repair has to be structurally reinforced, not just cosmetically hidden. If the fabric can't take that reinforcement, the mend isn't viable — and I'd rather tell you than take money for a repair that fails in a month.

04

Is the damage in a visible or hidden place?A hole on the front chest of a suit jacket needs to be nearly invisible. The same hole on an inner seam that nobody sees can be a straightforward structural repair at a much lower cost. Tell me where the damage is — it changes both the technique and the price.

05

Do I have matching thread or fibre?For luxury fabrics — Italian wool, Japanese silk, cashmere — matching the exact fibre weight and colour is the difference between a repair that disappears and one that screams. I keep a wide stock of threads on hand, and I source specific matching fibres when needed. If matching is impossible, I'll tell you honestly and propose an alternative — a visible but beautiful mend instead of a bad invisible one.

06

Is the garment in my area of expertise?I do not repair leather (the technique is completely different and I'd rather refer you to a leather specialist), and I do not restore heavily damaged vintage couture (that work needs a conservation-level approach). For everything else — wool, cashmere, silk, cotton, linen, knitwear, denim, uniforms, formal wear — I take the job personally.

You won't get a sales pitch when you message me. You'll get a straight answer about what's worth doing — sometimes that means a repair, sometimes it means saving your money.
