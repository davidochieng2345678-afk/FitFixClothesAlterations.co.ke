Two centimetres, whole different garment

Why shorts are the most proportion-sensitive garment in menswear

Trousers are forgiving. A hem that sits slightly high or low on a trouser barely registers — nobody notices a centimetre. Shorts have no such tolerance. The hem line lands somewhere on or near the knee, and where it lands determines whether the wearer looks modern or dated, tall or short, athletic or middle-aged. The same pair of shorts on the same body can look completely different depending on two centimetres of hem. That's the reason shorts alterations are more sensitive than trouser alterations, and why we measure them on the body — every time.

The hem has to land on the knee — not near it

The single biggest rule in shorts alterations: the finished hem should land on the knee, or just above the top of the kneecap. That's the sweet spot. Land it exactly on the knee and the shorts read as balanced — neither sporty nor conservative, just correct. Land it one or two centimetres above the knee and the shorts read as modern and slightly athletic — a good choice for a slimmer client who wants a contemporary look. Land it one or two centimetres below the knee and the shorts start to look dated — they read as "cargo shorts from 2010" or "school uniform" rather than a considered choice. Land it five or more centimetres below the knee and the shorts have effectively become baggy capri pants. A hem that's five centimetres below the knee makes the wearer look shorter and heavier, no matter what their actual proportions are.

There is no universal correct length

Unlike a trouser hem — where the rule is "one break, sitting on the shoe" — a shorts hem has no universal rule that applies to everyone. The right length depends on the wearer's height, leg length, build and the occasion. A tall client with long legs can wear slightly longer shorts and still look proportionate. A shorter client should go shorter at the hem so the proportion stays correct. A slim client can wear a trimmer leg opening and slightly shorter length. A heavier client should keep the length more moderate and the leg opening wider, so the shorts don't bind. This is why we never quote a shorts hem from a photo alone — we need to know the client's height and see the shorts on their body before the cut is planned. Even a two-centimetre error on a shorts hem is visible.

The rise matters as much as the hem

Rise is how high the waistband sits relative to the crotch. On shorts, the rise affects where the hem sits on the leg — because if the waistband sits lower than intended, everything below it drops, including the hem. A shorts hem measured correctly on a pair that sits at the correct rise will look wrong on a pair whose rise is one or two centimetres low. This is why we measure both the rise and the hem together on every shorts alteration. If the rise needs correcting (either raising or lowering), we plan that first and then re-measure the hem from the new waistband position. Doing it the other way around — hem first, rise second — is how a shorts alteration ends up looking off even though the hem measurement was technically correct.

The leg opening decides the silhouette

Shorts legs are usually straight from the widest part of the thigh to the hem, but the width of the leg opening (the circumference at the bottom of the leg) is what determines the silhouette. A narrow leg opening reads as tailored and athletic — good for a slim client. A wider leg opening reads as relaxed and casual — good for a heavier client and for warm-weather comfort. Most off-the-rack shorts are cut with a leg opening that suits an average build, which means they flare on a slim client and bind on a heavier one. Leg slimming (tapering) narrows the opening without disturbing the fit through the thigh. On a heavier client, we sometimes widen the leg opening instead — which is a different job to slimming and priced differently.

Shorts move differently from trousers

Shorts sit at a different point on the body than trousers, and they move differently — they swing more because there's less fabric below the knee to stabilise them. This matters for the seat, the crotch and the pocket placement. On a shorts alteration, we always check the seat curve and the pocket position, because shorts that fit perfectly standing still can look odd when the wearer walks. The back pocket height and angle — which nobody thinks about on trousers — becomes visible on shorts because the leg length is short. This is why we measure shorts in a standing position and then have the client walk a few steps and sit down, all before we make the first cut.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 1,000 shorts hem done right transforms a pair of shorts the client already owns into something they'll wear every weekend. A KSh 1,000 shorts hem done wrong makes the same shorts unwearable — they sit in the wardrobe because the wearer can't bring themselves to put them on. That's the difference between an alteration that pays for itself and one that doesn't. We turn down shorts alterations every month when the honest answer is that the shorts are past the point where altering them makes sense — too much wear, the fabric thinning, the cut already compromised by a previous bad hem. We'd rather tell you that upfront than take your money for a pair that will fail within a season. It's the same standard we hold on chino alterations and jeans alterations.

Not all shorts are the same job

Three shorts tiers — three completely different alterations

A pair of gym shorts and a pair of tailored wool dress shorts require completely different handling. One is a loose knit with an elastic waistband; the other is a structured woven garment with a fixed waistband, welt pockets and sometimes a lining. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be ruined. Here's how we categorise shorts before deciding what technique to use.

Everyday tier

Casual shorts — chino, cargo, denim, sport

Chino shorts in cotton twill, denim shorts in 8–12 oz cotton, cargo shorts in heavier cotton canvas, and sport shorts in knit fabric or synthetic blends. Fabric weight 200–350 gsm. Fixed waistband on most, elasticated on some (gym shorts). Pockets are usually simple patch or slash styles. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 1,500 and KSh 6,000. The shorts most clients want altered.

What we do differentlyCotton twill and denim both fray at a cut edge, so every raw cut is overlocked before hemming. Cargo shorts have a thicker fabric that behaves more like workwear than casualwear, so we use a heavier needle and reinforce the stress points. On denim shorts, we can do a chain-stitched hem to match the original factory finish if the client wants it.

Everyday tier

Tailored casual shorts — mid-weight cotton and blends

Better-crafted shorts from mid-tier brands — Uniqlo, Massimo Dutti, Zara, mid-range Italian cotton. Fabric weight 250–350 gsm, sometimes with 1–2% elastane for stretch. Cleaner construction with slanted pockets, a proper waistband with belt loops and a hook-and-bar closure, sometimes a small internal waistband reinforcement strip. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 5,000 and KSh 15,000.

What we do differentlyElastane-blend shorts pull back after cutting, so we cut with an allowance and use a slightly looser stitch tension so the fabric can still move. On shorts with a curtain waistband (the internal waistband piece that anchors the trouser to the body), any waist take-in has to preserve that structure — otherwise the waistband sits wrong even after the side seams are re-cut.

Premium tier

Tailored dress shorts — wool, linen and structured blends

Formal dress shorts in wool, wool-linen blends, cotton-linen, or structured summer suiting. Fabric weight varies widely — cotton-linen is usually 200–280 gsm, wool dress shorts 250–320 gsm. Properly constructed with a curtain waistband, welt pockets or hand-cut pockets, sometimes a half-lining at the front, functional button fly. Found on tailor-made summer suits, formalwear collections and high-end menswear brands. Common above KSh 15,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier the alteration has to be invisible and the construction has to be preserved. We hand-finish the pocket bags, preserve the curtain waistband structure, and never cut through the front pocket stay. On wool dress shorts, we hand-press the hem over a ham so the fabric keeps its drape. A careless alteration on a KSh 30,000 pair of wool dress shorts is a KSh 30,000 mistake.

If you're not sure which tier your shorts fall into, send a photo of the inside of the waistband and one of the front pocket on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

The five parts of shorts that affect the alteration

What a pair of shorts actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe shorts as "trousers but shorter." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, shorts have five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a pair.

01

The waistband — fixed, elastic or half-elastic

Waistbands on shorts come in three styles. A fully fixed waistband (belt loops, hook-and-bar or button closure) is the standard on chino, cargo and tailored shorts. A fully elastic waistband with a drawstring is standard on gym and swim shorts. A half-elastic waistband (elastic at the back, fixed at the front) is common on casual shorts designed for comfort. Each one is altered a different way. On a fixed waistband, taking in the waist means re-cutting the side seams and reshaping the waistband. On an elastic waistband, taking in the waist usually means shortening the elastic. On half-elastic, the two halves are treated separately.

02

The leg length — the hem, and why it's the whole job

The hem is the finished bottom edge of the leg, and on shorts it's the single most visible tailoring decision. As discussed in the "why shorts are different" section above, where the hem lands — on the knee, slightly above, or slightly below — determines the entire impression of the garment. The hem is usually finished one of three ways on quality shorts: a straight machine hem (single or double needle), a rolled hem (sometimes called a turned hem, where the fabric folds back on itself), or an overlocked finish (on cheaper shorts). When we shorten a shorts hem, we preserve the original finish style so the alteration looks factory-made.

03

The rise — how high the waist sits relative to the crotch

Rise is measured from the crotch seam to the top of the waistband. Standard mid-rise shorts are usually 26–29 cm of rise; low-rise shorts are 23–26 cm; high-rise shorts (rare on casual shorts, common on tailored dress shorts) are 29–32 cm. Rise is critical on shorts because it determines where the waistband sits on the body — which in turn determines where the hem lands on the leg. If the rise is wrong, no hem measurement will fix it. We adjust rise on shorts by taking in or letting out the crotch depth, which is a moderately technical alteration and one that most Nairobi tailors avoid. We do it regularly.

04

The seat and the crotch — where shorts fail first

On any pair of shorts that gets worn regularly, the seat and the crotch are the two places that fail first. The seat sags because the rear panel stretches under load when you sit. The crotch sags because the fabric at the V-joint takes continuous movement. Both are also areas where a bad alteration is visible. When we take in or let out the seat on shorts, we reshape the rear panel along its original curve — we don't just add a dart. On the crotch, we can reinforce the V-joint at the same time as any alteration, at no extra charge, because reinforcing takes minutes while the shorts are open. It adds a year of wear to the shorts.

05

The pockets — and why shorts are the worst place for a bad pocket

Shorts usually have two front pockets (slash or slanted) and two back pockets (patch or welt). Both front and back pockets are more visible on shorts than on trousers because the leg is shorter — there's less fabric below the pocket to draw the eye away. A back pocket that sits too low or too high on a pair of shorts reads immediately. Same with a front pocket that sits too far forward or too far back. When we alter the waist or the seat on shorts, we always check the pocket position before we finish — because if we take in the side seams, the front pocket moves with them, and it can end up sitting too far toward the front of the thigh. Re-positioning pockets on shorts is part of every waist alteration we do.

If a tailor quotes a shorts alteration without asking about the rise, the hem style, or the pocket position, they're about to alter the shorts into something you won't wear. We'd rather ask three questions upfront than see you back in a month with shorts that don't get worn.

The fabric decides the technique

The five short fabrics we work with most often — and how each one behaves

The fabric decides how the alteration behaves under a needle, how it presses, and how it holds shape after the first wash. Here's our honest breakdown by fabric type, the same one we'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Cotton twill — chino and casual shorts

The most common fabric for chino shorts and casual smart shorts. Cotton twill has a diagonal weave that presses beautifully, holds a crease, and takes alteration well. It's also the fabric that shrinks — untreated cotton twill shrinks 1–2% on the first wash, and continues to shrink slightly over the first couple of years. This is why we cut with a small allowance on cotton twill shorts. If the client has already washed the shorts several times, we take that into account in the hem measurement — a hem cut to the exact length they ask for will sit slightly high after the next few washes.

Denim — denim shorts and cut-offs

Denim shorts are usually cut from 8–12 oz denim, which is lighter than jeans denim but still substantial. Denim takes alteration beautifully and holds shape for years. The trade-off is that denim frays aggressively at a cut edge, so every hem we cut is either overlocked or finished with a chain-stitch to match the factory look. On raw denim shorts, the fabric will develop a patina over time — but the chain-stitch hem we put in won't fade at the same rate as the rest of the shorts. This is normal, and it's the reason most jeans and shorts alterations use a slightly heavier thread than the original — the hem thread takes longer to fade than the denim around it.

Cotton-linen blends — warm-weather shorts

The fabric of choice for Nairobi's warmer months and for clients who wear shorts on holiday. Cotton-linen drapes beautifully and breathes well, but the linen content frays at a cut edge if not overlocked immediately. We overlock the raw edge before hemming any linen shorts. Linen also doesn't press as crisply as pure cotton — it has a natural rumple that's part of the appeal. On linen shorts, we don't try to press out the rumple; we work with it. If the shorts have a light lining at the front (common on higher-end linen shorts), we re-set the lining at the new hem line.

Wool and wool blends — tailored dress shorts

The fabric of formal summer suiting. Wool dress shorts are usually cut from a lightweight tropical wool or wool-linen blend — 250–320 gsm. The fabric has a face side and a nap direction, and it responds to steam beautifully. Every alteration on wool dress shorts has to respect the fabric's drape — cutting a wool dress shorts hem flush to the knee without accounting for how the wool falls can leave the shorts looking stiff and slightly wrong. We hand-press wool dress shorts hems over a ham rather than on a flat surface, so the hem follows the natural curve of the leg. On shorts with a lining, the lining is re-set at the new hem line, hand-finished if the original was hand-finished.

Knit and synthetic — sport, gym and swim shorts

Sport and gym shorts are usually knitted rather than woven, which changes everything about the alteration. A knit stretches in every direction, so a hem stitched on a normal machine will pop when the wearer runs. We use a stretch stitch or a cover-stitch on all knit shorts hems, and ballpoint needles so the needle pushes between the knit loops rather than cutting them. On swim shorts — usually a quick-dry nylon or polyester — we heat-seal every cut edge and reinforce the drawstring channel if the waist is being adjusted. On gym shorts with an elastic waistband, replacing the elastic is often the better fix rather than shortening the existing one, because old elastic loses its spring.

What we won't alter

We turn down shorts alterations every month because the honest answer is that the shorts have reached end of life. On shorts where the fabric is broadly thinning across the seat (common on 5+ year old chinos worn hard), any alteration will accelerate the failure. On shorts where a previous hem has already been cut too short and there's no fabric left to let down, the honest answer is usually that the shorts should be retired rather than re-cut into something else. On shorts where the waistband has been compromised by a previous bad alteration, we can sometimes rebuild it — but at KSh 1,500–2,500, that's more than the shorts are worth in most cases. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money. It's the same standard we hold on fabric patching.

A short note from the bench

The two centimetres that decide everything on a pair of shorts

If I could teach one thing to every Nairobi client who ever brings me a pair of shorts, it would be this. On trousers, the hem is a finishing detail. On shorts, the hem is the entire garment. Two centimetres one way or the other changes how the shorts read — sporty, balanced, dated, modern, athletic, heavy. Every shorts hem I cut is a proportion decision, not just a length decision. And that's the difference between a shorts alteration that clients love and one that sits in the wardrobe.

Here's how I think about it. The hem should land on the knee — the top of the kneecap, roughly. That's the reference point. Land it there and the shorts are balanced. They read as neither formal nor sporty. They fit a wide range of occasions — a weekend at home, a casual Friday at the office, a Sunday lunch. Land it two centimetres above the knee and the shorts read as more athletic and modern — but only on a client who's slim and shorter-framed, because the higher hem shortens the visible leg and reads as disproportionate on anyone else. Land it two centimetres below the knee and the shorts start to read as older, more relaxed, more conservative. Land it five centimetres below the knee and the shorts have effectively become something else entirely — they read as baggy capri pants and nobody looks good in those.

This is why I never quote a shorts hem from a photo alone. A photo shows the shorts on a hanger or laid flat. It doesn't show the client's height, their leg length, their build, or how their existing shorts sit on their body. What looks like a hem that needs to come up two centimetres on a photo might look right on the client's actual proportions — or need to come down two centimetres — once I see them. I ask for a photo of the client wearing the shorts, standing, front and side. I ask for their height. And when they collect the shorts from the workshop, I ask them to try them on again before I mark the final hem. That extra step is what makes the difference.

There's a second thing about shorts that clients don't think about — the rise. Rise is where the waistband sits relative to the crotch. On trousers, a wrong rise is annoying but manageable — you wear a belt and ignore it. On shorts, a wrong rise cascades down the entire garment. If the rise is too low, the whole pair sits too low on your hips, which drags the hem lower than you wanted, which makes the shorts look longer than you asked for. If the rise is too high, the waistband digs into your ribs when you sit. Every shorts alteration I do starts with the rise, then moves to the hem. Doing it the other way around is how a shorts alteration ends up technically correct but visually wrong. And visually wrong is what clients actually notice.

The last thing I'll say about shorts is that they're the most wearable garment in most clients' wardrobes and the least altered. Trousers get altered because work demands it. Suits get altered because events demand it. Shorts rarely get altered because clients assume a small wrongness is fine — "they're just shorts, I'll live with them." But the small wrongness is exactly what makes a client not want to wear them. The shorts sit in the wardrobe because the hem is slightly too long, or the waist sags slightly when they sit, or the leg opening is just a bit too wide. Fixing those small wrongnesses turns a pair that gets worn once a month into a pair that gets worn every weekend. That's the work — not making the shorts fit, but making them the shorts you actually reach for.

Make your shorts last between alterations

Eight habits that keep shorts out of the workshop

Shorts take more punishment per square centimetre than trousers. They're worn in warm weather, in the sun, on holidays, at weekends — the exact conditions that stress fabric hardest. Most of the damage we repair in the workshop was preventable. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect an altered pair.

1. Wash cold, wash inside out

Cotton twill, denim and linen all hold their colour and shape far better in cold water. Turning shorts inside out before washing protects the visible surface from friction in the drum — this matters most on denim, where the outer face is the part you see, and on chino shorts where the surface develops a shine if it's abraded too much. Wash whites and darks separately on shorts because the smaller volume of fabric makes colour transfer more likely. Skip fabric softener — it leaves a residue that dulls the fabric's natural finish.

2. Air dry — don't tumble

Shorts are almost always dryer, not washing machine, failures. Tumble drying shrinks cotton twill and denim by 3–5%, and it also weakens elastic waistbands and heat-sealed synthetic edges. On a pair of shorts you've just had hemmed, tumble drying is the fastest way to make the hem sit half a centimetre high. Hang shorts on a line or a rack, or lay them flat to dry. If you're in a hurry, point a fan at them. Never dry shorts in direct sun — the colour fades unevenly, and on linen the sun bleaches in a way that doesn't wash out.

3. Check the hem after every wash, not every month

Hem thread on shorts takes more abuse than the fabric itself — it's the part that catches on everything, and the part that fades first if it's a lighter or darker shade than the shorts. Give the hem a two-second inspection after every wash. If you see the thread starting to come loose or fray at the edge, bring it in — a stitch repair is KSh 400–600 and takes a day. Left for another month, it becomes a re-hem at KSh 1,000. Left for longer, the hem can come apart entirely, and by that point the fabric at the edge has frayed too.

4. Don't over-wash

Shorts don't need washing after every wear, especially in dry weather when they haven't been sweating against the skin. If they don't smell and aren't visibly dirty, hanging them to air for a few hours is enough. Two wears between washes is normal, three is fine in cool weather. Every unnecessary wash is one more cycle of friction, colour loss and (if you tumble dry) shrinkage. Shorts that are washed less are shorts that last longer and stay their original size for years — which means fewer alterations over time.

5. Fold, don't hang, when storing short-term

Shorts that hang by the waistband will eventually develop a stretched waistband and a distorted seat — the same reason you don't hang trousers by the waistband. Fold shorts flat in a drawer or a shelf. If you're stacking several pairs, place them loosely rather than pressing them tightly — the same problem happens with pleats or a pressed crease in cotton twill, which can become permanent if you compress it too much. On linen shorts, folding loosely also helps reduce the natural rumpling that gets worse with tight folding.

6. Rotate between pairs

Cotton twill, denim and linen all need 24 hours to recover their shape between wears. Wearing the same pair of shorts three days in a row stretches the seat, the crotch and the waistband — the three places that fail first. If you own two or three pairs and rotate them, each one gets worn a third as often and lasts roughly three times as long. This single habit roughly doubles the number of years a good pair of shorts stays in good condition.

7. Repair small issues before they grow

A loose buttonhole at the fly is a KSh 300 fix today. Left for three months, it becomes a KSh 600 fly rebuild. A small snag at the hem is a KSh 500 darning job today; left for another season it becomes a KSh 800 patched hem. A slightly worn crotch is a KSh 600 reinforcement job now; left for a year, it's a KSh 1,200 V-joint rebuild. The clients who spend the least on shorts repairs are the ones who message us the first time they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less.

8. Watch for the pocket corner failure

The single most common shorts failure we see is the front pocket corner — the top of the pocket bag where it joins the waistband. On cheap shorts, the pocket bag is held in place with a single stitch at the top corner, and it tears free within eighteen months of regular wear. On well-made shorts, the corner is bar-tacked. When you notice the top corner of a pocket starting to pull free, bring the shorts in. A pocket corner re-stitch is KSh 400 and takes an hour — cheaper than the pocket replacement that follows if you ignore it.

Follow those eight habits and your shorts will hold their fit and colour for years. And when they do eventually need work — a hem, a waist adjustment, a zip replacement — we're one WhatsApp message away.
