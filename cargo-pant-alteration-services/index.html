Who actually asks me for this

Cargo pant alterations are not one job. They're four different jobs.

Over the years I've learned that the person sending me the WhatsApp message changes everything about how I approach the alteration. Here's what I mean.

The tradesperson or site worker

You spend your day on your feet. Your cargo pants need to bend, squat, and climb without splitting at the knees. What you actually want is a hem short enough that you don't trip on it, and a reinforced knee so the fabric lasts more than six months. That's a different job from someone who wears cargo pants to a coffee shop. I reinforce with matching canvas and double-lock the seam — the pants end up stronger than when you brought them in.

The security officer or uniform wearer

You've been issued a pair of tactical cargoes that came in one size and one size only. The waist gapes, the legs flap, the hem drags. You're not allowed to change the design, so we work within it — take in the waist, taper the leg slightly, shorten the hem to regulation length. Nothing flashy, nothing visible. Just so it looks like the trousers were meant for you.

The hiker, camper, or outdoor person

You own a pair of ripstop cargoes that fit well everywhere except the length. You're not going to send them back to the retailer for that. And you want the hem to sit right above your boot — not folded inside, not skimming the ground. I hem with a flat-felled seam so there's no inside chafe on long treks. Nothing rubs.

The student or fashion-first wearer

You bought a pair of cargoes because the cut looked good on someone else. On you, they billow. You want the leg narrowed so they look more like a tapered jogger than a workman's trouser, without losing the two side pockets that made you buy them. That's taper work — a careful taper from mid-thigh down, keeping the pocket bags full-width. It's the trickiest cargo job I do, and it's the one I get asked for most.

The seven points I check on every pair

What a cargo pant actually is, structurally

Cargo pants are not just trousers with pockets. They have their own anatomy — and if you don't respect it, the alteration will look wrong. These are the seven things I look at before I ever touch a pair with scissors.

  1. The waistband and belt loops Cargo waistbands are usually wider and thicker than dress trouser waistbands — often with a hidden elastic section. Taking in the waist means re-stitching through all those layers. If you take in more than about 6 cm, the belt loops end up unevenly spaced, and the whole thing looks hand-made. We cap the take-in at the point where it still looks factory-finished.
  2. The rise and crotch depth Cargo pants generally sit lower than dress trousers. If a pair feels like it's sliding down, the fix isn't usually the waist — it's the rise. Reducing the rise by 2 to 3 cm lifts the whole trouser without shrinking the waist, and it's a much better fix than tightening the belt.
  3. The side cargo pockets This is the cargo pant's defining feature and the reason most alterations go wrong. The pockets have a gusset — a folded fabric panel inside — and if you slim the leg without accounting for that gusset, the pocket puffs out and looks like a balloon. We taper around the pocket, never through it. On some jobs, we re-shape the pocket bag itself.
  4. The inseam and hem finish Cargo pant hems are usually flat-felled — a two-needle stitch that folds the raw edge inside. Shortening while keeping the original hem intact is called "re-attaching the original hem," and it costs more in labour but keeps the trouser looking untouched. Shortening with a fresh hem is faster and cheaper, and for workwear, honestly, just as good.
  5. The knee and thigh reinforcement Better cargo pants have a second layer of fabric at the knee — sometimes internally, sometimes as a visible panel. If you're shortening or slimming, we have to work around that panel so it doesn't shift. If the panel is worn through, this is where you add a patch.
  6. The fly zip and button Cargo zips take more punishment than any other zip on a garment because the fabric is heavy. Most replacements are done badly — a lightweight zip gets forced into a heavy waistband and breaks within weeks. We replace with YKK heavy-duty only. Same for the top button; we use a metal shank button, not a plastic one.
  7. The pocket bags at the front Front pocket bags are usually cotton twill. When we slim a waist, the pocket opening moves, and if we don't re-anchor the bag correctly, the pocket corners will tear within weeks. We reinforce the top corner of every front pocket bag as standard on any waist alteration. Most tailors skip this step. It's the difference between an alteration that lasts five years and one that lasts five weeks.

Fabric matters more than the fit

How I alter each kind of cargo pant fabric

Nobody asks this question until it's too late. The fabric determines how a pair of cargoes moves, presses, and lasts — and how much an alteration will actually cost you in the end. Here's my honest rundown.

Cotton twill

The classic. Ninety percent of the cargo pants that come through my workshop in Nairobi are cotton twill or a twill-polyester blend. It presses beautifully, takes a crease, and forgives a slight alteration error. The downside is shrinkage — a cotton twill pant that's been washed hot will shrink half a size over a year. I always ask how many times the pair has been washed before I quote, because it changes the hem measurement.

Ripstop nylon and ripstop cotton

Ripstop has a grid of reinforcing threads woven through it, which is what makes it tear-resistant. The grid also means the fabric has almost no stretch and cannot be pressed flat — every seam has to be stitched exactly right the first time, because there is no second chance. I use a fine needle and a slightly looser tension on ripstop. If you have a good pair of hiking cargoes, they're almost certainly ripstop.

Heavy canvas and duck cloth

This is the fabric of proper workwear cargoes — the ones that feel stiff for the first month and then soften into the best trousers you own. Canvas is difficult to hem because the needle struggles to penetrate three layers of it, and machine hems on canvas can look puckered. On heavy canvas I do most of the work by hand with a saddler's needle. It takes longer. It also holds forever.

Stretch blends (cotton-elastane)

Modern cargo pants very often have 1–3% elastane in the fabric to give them movement. That changes everything. Elastane means the fabric will pull back slightly after you cut it, so if you cut to an exact measurement, the finished hem ends up a few millimetres short. I always cut an allowance on stretch cargoes and finish with a slightly looser stitch so the hem can move with the fabric.

Tactical nylon and poly-cotton

The fabric used in proper military and security cargoes. It's usually a 65/35 poly-cotton with a ripstop grid, treated with a water-repellent coating. That coating is the tricky part — it burns under heat and turns shiny if you iron it directly. I press through a protective cloth and I don't use high heat on the coating side. Most security cargo alterations are simple — waist and hem — so this is manageable.

Denim cargo hybrids

Some cargoes are made from denim — heavier, more structured, and often with jean-style stitching and hardware. These behave like jeans rather than trousers: they take a chain-stitch hem, they fade at the seams, and they can handle a stronger taper without looking strained. If your cargoes are denim-based, tell me when you message — the alteration technique is different from the start.

If you don't know what fabric your cargo pants are, send a close-up photo on WhatsApp. I'll identify it from the weave and tell you straight what's possible.

Why cargo pants are harder than they look

Cargo pants need a different kind of tailor

If you're used to sending your suits to a smart tailor in Westlands, that's not the tailor who should be handling your cargo pants. Cargo pants are workwear. They need workwear tailoring — a different thread weight, a different needle, a different stitch length. Here's what fourteen years of tailoring taught me about it.

Most Nairobi tailors learned their craft on suits, shirts and dresses. Those garments have a specific weight, drape and behaviour. Cargo pants are a different animal. Cotton twill behaves nothing like wool. Ripstop behaves nothing like cotton twill. Heavy canvas breaks needles that glide through suit fabric all day.

When I trained — at Manix Clothing Store, then Italian Men's Wear, then City Walk Ltd — I learned suit tailoring first. That's the foundation. But the skills that make a good cargo pant alteration are different. Here's what I actually do differently on a cargo job.

  • Heavy-duty thread throughout — We use a bonded polyester thread two weights heavier than the standard garment thread. It doesn't fray, doesn't break under tension, and holds a seam through years of stress. It costs more. It's the reason our alterations last.
  • Reinforced pocket bag anchoring — When we take in a waist, we re-anchor the front pocket bags at the top corner. It's a two-minute extra step most tailors skip. It's the difference between a pocket that tears in six weeks and one that holds for years.
  • Gusset-aware tapering — The cargo side pockets have a gusset inside. Slim the leg without reshaping the gusset and the pocket puffs. We taper around the pocket, and on tight jobs we rebuild the pocket bag itself. Most Nairobi tailors don't even know this is a problem until they see it.
  • YKK heavy-duty zips only — Standard fly zips fail on cargo pants because the fabric is too heavy for the teeth. We replace with YKK heavy-duty — the same zips used on proper workwear. Costs a bit more in materials. Never breaks.
  • Flat-felled seams on hems where needed — For hiking cargoes and outdoor wear, we finish the hem flat-felled so there's no raw edge rubbing against your boot. It's slower work. It matters on a long trek.
  • Stretch-fabric allowances — Cargoes with elastane pull back after cutting. We cut with a known allowance and finish with a looser stitch so the hem still moves with you. Nobody mentions this until the hem buckles. We just do it as standard.

This is the difference between a general alterations shop and a proper cargo pant tailor. It's not marketing language — it's the specific things we do on a job that cost us more time and materials, and that almost nobody else does.

If you want to hire an experienced cargo pant tailor in Nairobi with free collection and you want the alteration done properly rather than cheaply, that's what we offer. If you're looking for the absolute cheapest quote in Nairobi, you'll find it — but you'll come back to us in six months when the hem frays, the pocket tears, and the zip breaks. We'd rather do it once, properly.

Make them last longer between alterations

How to care for cargo pants so you alter them less often

A well-cared-for pair of cargo pants can go five years without a single alteration. A badly-cared-for pair needs one every year. Here are the eight habits I tell every customer when they collect their trousers — the same things I do with my own.

1. Wash cold, wash inside out

Hot water shrinks cotton twill. It also fades the colour unevenly at the seams and pockets — the exact places you don't want to look worn. Turn them inside out before washing and use cold water on a gentle cycle. If your cargoes are ripstop or tactical fabric with a water-repellent coating, hot water strips the coating. Cold wash only.

2. Air dry — never tumble dry

Tumble drying is the single fastest way to shrink a pair of cargo pants. It also weakens the elastic in the waistband if the pair has any. Hang them on a line or the back of a chair and let them dry naturally. If you're in a hurry, hang them in front of a fan. It takes longer. It saves the trousers.

3. Iron from the inside

Iron cargo pants from the inside, or through a thin cloth if the fabric has a coating or print. Direct high heat on the outside fades the colour and can leave shiny marks that never come out. Ripstop and tactical fabrics should never see a hot iron — the coating burns and turns shiny.

4. Don't overfill the cargo pockets

This is the one that catches everyone. Cargo pockets have a weight limit. If you're carrying tools, phones, water bottles and keys in the side pockets every day, the fabric stretches, the pocket bags tear, and the seams at the top of the pocket fail within a year. If you carry heavy gear daily, get a belt pouch or a tool belt. Your cargo pockets will last three times longer.

5. Wash zips closed

Before washing, close every zip — fly, side pockets, everything. An open zip during a wash catches on the fabric inside the drum and bends its teeth. A bent tooth is what causes a zip to feel "stuck" a few weeks later. This one habit prevents most of the zip replacements we do.

6. Rotate between two pairs

If you wear cargo pants daily — to work, to site, to the shop — own two pairs and rotate them. Fabric needs 24 hours to recover its shape and dry out fully from body moisture. Wearing the same pair two days in a row speeds up wear at the knees, seat and hem by a surprising amount.

7. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose stitch on the pocket is a KSh 800 repair today. Left alone, the pocket tears free and you're looking at KSh 1,200 for a replacement, plus the risk that the fabric around it is also weakened. A loose button today is KSh 400. A torn buttonhole tomorrow is a bigger job. Message us when you first notice something small — most small repairs are same-day and come with free pickup.

8. Store them folded, not on a hanger

Cargo pants are heavy. Hanging them from the waistband by a clip stretches the waistband and pulls the trouser out of shape over months. Fold them flat in a drawer or on a shelf. If you must hang, use a bar hanger through both belt loops so the weight is distributed.

Follow these eight habits and your cargoes will hold their shape for years. And when they do eventually need an alteration, we're one WhatsApp message away.
