The material that punishes mistakes

Why leather jacket alteration is the most unforgiving work in a tailor's workshop

Wool recovers. Cotton forgives. Linen frays but can be re-cut. Leather does none of those things. It's a skin — a tanned hide with a nap direction, a grain structure and a memory. When you push a needle through it, that hole stays. When you cut it wrong, that cut is permanent. When you press it too hot, that mark is forever. That's the honest reason so many Nairobi tailors won't take leather work — and the reason we do.

Every needle hole is permanent

On cotton or wool, a tailor can unpick a seam, press out the old stitch marks, and re-stitch along a slightly different line. The old holes disappear into the fabric. On leather, this is impossible. Leather is skin — it has no memory that lets it close back up. Once a needle has gone through, the hole stays visible. That means every leather alteration we do has to be planned before the first stitch — because there is no second attempt. If the seam is going to move two centimetres to the left, it has to move two centimetres on the first pass, or we're going to leave a line of holes running down the side of your jacket.

Leather has a nap and a direction

Suede is the obvious one, but every leather has a surface direction — a fine grain that catches the light differently depending on which way you rub it. Cut or press against that direction and the leather shows a permanent light patch. On black leather this matters most, because a light patch on black leather reads as wear or damage even when the cut itself is perfect. We mark the nap direction with chalk on the inside of the jacket before any work begins, and every new panel we cut is oriented to match.

Leather stretches — it's a skin, not a weave

Woven fabric has threads that hold a shape. Leather is a natural skin, and skins stretch. A sleeve that measures exactly 62 cm on the bench may read 63 cm after a week of wear. This is why we cut leather a fraction longer than the target measurement and let the leather relax before the final stitch. It's also why a leather jacket that fit perfectly when you bought it can feel a size larger after a year — the leather has moved with your body. We take that into account on every alteration, especially sleeve length and body length.

Leather requires its own needles and machines

A leather needle is not the same as a fabric needle. A fabric needle has a sharp point or a ballpoint, and it pushes between the threads of a weave. A leather needle has a wedge-shaped cutting point — it slices a small slit through the leather instead of pushing through it. This matters because a standard needle tears leather, and a torn slit in leather doesn't close back up. We use leather-specific needles, heavier thread, and a walking-foot machine that feeds leather evenly so the top layer doesn't slide against the bottom layer. Standard machines pull leather unevenly and leave the seam wavy.

You can't iron leather the way you iron cloth

A standard iron on leather will scorch it. Leather has a surface finish — a dye, a wax, a top coat — that reacts to heat. Once you've pressed too hot, the leather is left with a permanent shine patch that shows up as a flat spot in the middle of an otherwise soft surface. We press leather through a muslin cloth on a low setting, and on some finishes we use a leather-specific pressing technique that avoids direct heat entirely. On faux leather and bonded leather, the rules are even tighter — the top layer is plastic and can bubble or melt if overheated.

The lining is a second garment

Every leather jacket has a full lining — usually a satin, quilted, or bemberg fabric on the inside. Any alteration that touches the body of the jacket means the lining has to come out first and be re-set at the end. That's why leather body slimming takes significantly longer than trouser work of the same visual scale. When we quote a leather alteration, the lining is factored into the price — because it's a separate garment that has to be handled carefully, re-measured, and hand-finished back into the jacket.

Not all leather is the same

Three leather tiers — three completely different alterations

A KSh 4,000 faux leather jacket from a market stall and a KSh 60,000 Italian lambskin jacket from a boutique require completely different handling. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be ruined. Here's how we categorise leather before deciding what technique to use.

Entry tier

Faux leather, PU leather, bonded leather

A plastic top layer (polyurethane or PVC) laminated to a fabric backing. Common on market jackets, fast fashion, and budget outerwear. The top layer is thin — usually 0.3–0.5 mm — and the backing is a knit or woven fabric. Feels slicker and colder than real leather. The cut edges tend to fray and can peel away from the backing over time. Colour is uniform and easier to match. Common in Nairobi retail under KSh 4,000.

What we do differentlyHeat is the enemy on faux leather — the plastic top layer bubbles or melts. We press through a muslin cloth at the absolute minimum temperature, and on some finishes we don't press at all, working the seam with a bone folder instead. We also avoid heavy stitching on bonded leather because the top layer can lift away from the backing along the stitch line.

Everyday tier

Genuine full-grain and top-grain leather

Real leather — usually cowhide, sheepskin, or goatskin. Common on mid-range jackets from Levi's, Massimo Dutti, Zara, and regional leather manufacturers. Thickness 0.8–1.2 mm. Visible grain, sometimes with natural markings that tell you it's real hide. Develops a patina with age. Colour can vary slightly panel to panel — real leather is never perfectly uniform. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 6,000 and KSh 25,000.

What we do differentlyGenuine leather takes alterations well — but only with the right needle, thread and technique. We use a wedge-point leather needle, a heavier bonded nylon thread, and a walking-foot machine. On top-grain leather, we hand-finish the edges of any new cut so they don't dry out or curl. On full-grain with heavy natural grain, we orient every new panel to match the grain direction of the original.

Premium tier

Designer leather, suede and specialty hides

Italian lambskin, calfskin, deer leather, pebbled goat leather, or suede. Often from Zegna, Boggi, Armani, or premium leather specialists. Thickness 0.5–0.8 mm — thinner than mid-tier leather, but stronger because the fibre structure is denser. Sometimes hand-finished with a signature thread, hand-set hardware, or a hand-cut lining. Suede is a sub-category — the hide has been sanded to a napped surface that shows every mark. Common in Nairobi retail above KSh 30,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier the alteration has to be invisible and the surface has to stay pristine. We hand-finish every seam. We never cut through a canvas or structured back panel. On suede we don't press at all — the nap marks permanently. On lambskin we use the finest available needle and hand-finish the lining edge rather than machine-stitching it. A careless alteration on a KSh 60,000 lambskin jacket is a KSh 60,000 mistake.

If you're not sure which tier your jacket falls into, send a close-up photo of the leather surface and the inside label on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the grain and construction how the alteration should be handled.

The six parts of a leather jacket that affect the alteration

What a leather jacket actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe a leather jacket as "leather and a zip." That description doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a leather jacket has six parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what we look at before we take a needle to a jacket.

01

The main zip and front placket

Every jacket has a front closure — usually a metal zip, sometimes a button placket, occasionally a double-breasted arrangement. On leather jackets, the zip is heavy-duty (YKK or equivalent), sometimes with a decorative pull or a storm flap. When the zip fails, replacing it is a specialist job because the leather front panel has to be opened up, the old zip removed, the new one set, and the placket re-aligned so the two front edges meet cleanly. On asymmetric biker jackets, this is even more delicate because the two front panels aren't symmetrical to begin with.

02

The lining

Every leather jacket has a full lining that insulates, protects the leather, and finishes the inside of the garment. The lining is a separate garment — it has its own seams and its own shape. Any alteration that touches the body of the jacket requires the lining to be removed, altered separately, and re-set. On quilted linings (common on motorcycle jackets), the inner padding has to be preserved. On bemberg or satin linings (common on designer leather), we hand-finish the seams to match the original construction.

03

The shoulder seam — raglan or set-in

Leather jackets use two shoulder constructions. Raglan — the seam runs from the underarm to the collar, with no shoulder seam. Set-in — a traditional shoulder seam that joins the sleeve to the body along the top of the shoulder. Each one affects the alteration differently. On raglan jackets, sleeve shortening can be done from either the cuff or the shoulder, giving more flexibility. On set-in shoulders, sleeve work usually happens at the cuff — the shoulder join is usually reinforced and difficult to re-open on leather.

04

The sleeve and cuff

Sleeves are usually the first thing clients want altered — leather jacket sleeves are notoriously long off the rack. Sleeve shortening is done at the cuff end wherever possible, because cutting into the shoulder join on leather is risky. If the sleeve has a zip cuff, a snap cuff, or a decorative finish at the wrist, we preserve that finish and shorten from a point a few centimetres above it. Sleeve slimming (which we do less often) requires opening the underarm seam and re-cutting from there.

05

The body, waist and hem

The body of the jacket has a front panel, a back panel, two side seams and a hem. Body slimming means taking in from the side seams — usually from armpit to hem, tapering in a way that follows the original shape of the jacket. Length shortening means cutting from the hem. Both are significant jobs because the lining has to come out. On jackets with a belted waist or a shaped hem, the alteration has to preserve the original silhouette — if the jacket is designed with a slight flare, slimming it too aggressively will make the hem curl.

06

The collar and lapel

Leather jacket collars come in several styles — a simple stand collar (very common), a shirt collar, a moto-style snap collar, or a lapel. On biker jackets, the lapel is asymmetric and the collar usually has snaps. Collar adjustments are less common than sleeve work, but when they're needed (a too-tight stand collar, a collar that's lost shape), we open the collar seam, reshape the underlying canvas, and re-set the leather. On suede, collar work is done with the greatest possible caution because the nap shows every mark.

If a tailor quotes a leather alteration without asking about the lining, the zip, or the sleeve construction, they're about to learn why leather doesn't forgive. We'd rather you knew that before you committed.

Every leather demands its own approach

The five leather types we work with most often — and how each one behaves

Leather type decides how the alteration behaves under a needle, how it presses, and how it ages after the first year. Here's what fourteen years of tailoring has taught us, hide by hide.

Cowhide (full-grain and top-grain)

The most common real leather for jackets. Thick (0.9–1.2 mm), dense, and durable. Cowhide takes alterations very well — it has enough body to hold a seam, and it doesn't stretch as aggressively as thinner hides. The trade-off is weight: cowhide jackets are heavier, so sleeve and body work has to account for gravity pulling the leather down. We reinforce the shoulder and hem seams on cowhide alterations so the jacket holds its shape through years of wear.

Lambskin and sheepskin

The softest, most luxurious leather you'll find on a jacket. Thinner than cowhide (0.5–0.8 mm), with a very fine grain and a beautiful drape. Lambskin is what most Italian and designer leather jackets are cut from. It's also the most delicate — a standard needle will tear it, and a hot iron will permanently mark it. We use the finest available needle on lambskin, hand-finish every seam, and never press directly on the surface. A lambskin sleeve shortening takes about three times as long as the same job on cowhide.

Goatskin

Underrated leather that's both durable and lightweight. Goatskin has a natural pebble grain and a slight sheen — it sits between lambskin and cowhide in weight and feel. It's the traditional leather for motorcycle jackets in some markets because it resists abrasion exceptionally well. Goatskin takes alterations beautifully — it has a natural stretch that lets seams sit without puckering, and it holds a press through a cloth better than lambskin does.

Suede

Leather that has been sanded on the flesh side (or, on split suede, on both sides) to create a napped surface. Suede has a velvety texture and a distinct nap direction. The nap is what makes suede so beautiful and so difficult to work with — every touch shows, every cut mark stays, and every pressed area flattens into a permanent shine spot. We do suede alterations entirely by hand where possible, don't press at all unless we absolutely must, and always work with the nap direction. Suede alterations cost slightly more than the same job on smooth leather because of the additional care.

Bonded and faux leather

A plastic top layer (polyurethane or PVC) bonded to a fabric backing. Common on budget jackets and fast-fashion outerwear. The top layer is thin and prone to peeling at cut edges. On bonded leather, we don't do tight slimming alterations — the plastic lifts from the backing when it's stretched. We also don't press with heat, because the top layer can bubble. The right move on bonded leather is usually to stabilise the existing construction and do the minimum alteration the client needs, rather than attempting a full restyle that the material can't take.

What we won't alter

On severely cracked or peeling leather — a jacket where the surface has begun to flake off in patches — we usually advise retiring the garment. The alteration itself may succeed, but the surrounding surface will keep failing, and the client will have paid for a repair on a jacket that has two months of life left. On heavily repaired jackets (multiple previous alterations with visible needle hole lines), we tell the client upfront that another alteration will add to the scars. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money.

If you don't know what your jacket is made of, send a close-up photo of the surface and the inside label on WhatsApp. We'll identify the leather type from the grain and tell you straight what's possible.

A short note from the bench

Why I plan every leather cut three times before I make it once

I once watched a Nairobi tailor ruin a KSh 90,000 leather jacket in about forty minutes. He measured the sleeves, marked them, cut them, stitched them. Then he asked the client to try the jacket on. The sleeves were the right length — but the seam line where he'd cut had left a line of tiny needle holes running down the front of the jacket, because he'd measured once and adjusted the fit twice. On cotton, those holes disappear with a press. On leather, they don't. The jacket was finished. The client paid. And the client never came back.

That's the reason I plan every leather cut three times before making it once. And I want to explain what I mean by planning, because it isn't just "think before you cut." On leather, planning means something very specific. It means I draw the alteration on the inside of the garment with chalk first — every new seam line, every new cut line, and every adjustment to the lining that will happen as a result. I check the drawing against the nap direction of the leather. I check it against the sleeve construction — raglan or set-in — because that determines what's actually possible. Then I walk away from the garment for at least an hour, and I come back and check the drawing again. If it still holds, I make the first cut. If it doesn't, I erase and redraw. That hour costs nothing and saves everything.

The other thing I want clients to understand is that leather stretches. Not much — a few millimetres on a sleeve, a few more on the body — but enough to matter. When I measure a sleeve that needs to lose four centimetres, I cut it to lose three and a half. That sounds wrong, and every client who hears it asks me why. The answer is that leather is a skin. When you wear it, the leather relaxes across the sleeve and it moves. A sleeve that measures exactly the length you asked for on the bench will feel one centimetre long after two weeks of wear. A sleeve that's slightly short on the bench will feel exactly right after two weeks. Every leather tailor knows this; not every Nairobi tailor does.

There's one more thing I want to say about linings, because most clients don't think about them at all until the day the armpit shreds. Every leather jacket is really two garments — the leather and the lining. The lining takes the wear from the inside: friction under the arms, sweat at the back, contact at the seat. So when the leather is still beautiful, the lining can be a mess. A full lining replacement on a leather jacket is KSh 2,500, and it takes about two days of careful hand-work. But it can extend the life of a KSh 60,000 jacket by another ten years. That's not a repair — that's a rescue. And it's the work I'm proudest of, because the client gets to keep the jacket they love instead of buying a replacement.

If you own a leather jacket that isn't quite right — sleeves too long, body too loose, zip sticking, lining tired — send me a photo on WhatsApp. I'll tell you straight what's possible, what it will cost, and whether it's worth doing. Sometimes the honest answer is that the alteration costs more than the jacket is worth, and I'd rather tell you that than take your money.

Make your leather jacket last between alterations

Eight habits that keep leather jackets out of the workshop

Leather is the most durable outerwear fabric there is — a well-cared-for leather jacket will outlive three cloth coats. But leather is also a skin, and it responds to the environment around it. Nairobi's dry season, the rain in April, and the way you store a jacket all matter more than you think. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect an altered leather jacket.

1. Condition once or twice a year

Leather dries out over time. When it dries, it loses flexibility, starts to crack at the stress points (elbows, shoulders, sleeve creases), and becomes more prone to tearing. A good leather conditioner — applied twice a year on a clean jacket, worked in with a soft cloth — restores the oils and keeps the leather supple. Nairobi's dry season and the harmattan-like dust in July and August are the two moments your jacket needs it most. Never use a petroleum-based product; use a purpose-made leather conditioner.

2. Keep it away from sustained heat

Leather hates heat. A jacket left near a radiator, a space heater, or on the back seat of a hot car will gradually lose moisture and stiffen. Once it stiffens, it can crack when it flexes. Never iron a leather jacket directly — the surface coat reacts to heat and leaves a permanent shine patch. If your jacket gets wrinkled, hang it in a warm room and let the natural drape take the wrinkles out.

3. Wipe spills immediately — never scrub

If something spills on your leather jacket, blot it with a soft cloth — do not rub. Rubbing pushes the spill into the leather's grain and often lifts the top finish. If the spill is oil-based, blot with a small amount of cornstarch first, then gently wipe. If water-based, blot with a dry cloth and let it air dry naturally — never with a hair dryer, and never in direct sun.

4. Air dry naturally, hang on wide hangers

If your jacket gets caught in the rain, hang it on a wide wooden or padded hanger and let it dry at room temperature. Never use a hair dryer or a radiator to speed it up. Never use a thin wire hanger — the weight of the wet leather pulls the shoulders out of shape. Once dry, if the leather feels stiff, a light conditioner brings it back.

5. Store on a wide hanger, not folded

Leather jackets should be hung, not folded. Folding a leather jacket puts stress on the same creases every time — the elbow line, the shoulder, the back panel — and over months, those creases become permanent. Use a wide wooden or padded hanger that supports the full width of the shoulders. If you must store the jacket in a bag, use a breathable cotton garment bag — never plastic, because leather needs air and trapped moisture causes mildew.

6. Rotate between jackets

Leather recovers its shape overnight — the skin stretches to your body during wear and relaxes back when you take the jacket off. Wearing the same leather jacket every single day means the leather never gets a chance to recover, and the elbows and shoulders stretch permanently. If you own two or three leather jackets and rotate them, each one holds its shape for years longer than it would if worn daily.

7. Fix zips and hardware before they fail completely

A zip that starts to skip teeth is a KSh 1,500 fix. Left for a month of forcing it closed, the zipper pull can snap, the slider can bend, and the whole zip has to be replaced — often at double the cost because the placket leather is now stressed from the constant forcing. Same for snaps and buttons: a KSh 600 replacement today is far cheaper than a KSh 2,000 collar adjustment down the line. The clients who spend the least on leather repairs are the ones who message us at the first sign of hardware trouble.

8. Clean the lining, not the leather

Most leather jackets get dirty from the inside, not the outside. Sweat, deodorant residue, and body oils attack the lining and the underside of the leather. Wipe the inside of the jacket with a slightly damp cloth every few months. For a fully removable lining, hand-wash in cold water and air dry flat. For a stitched-in lining, spot clean only. And when the lining eventually shreds at the armpits — which is normal for a jacket that's been worn well for years — a KSh 2,500 lining replacement gives the jacket another decade of life.

Follow those eight habits and your leather jacket will outlive the decade you bought it in. And when it does eventually need an alteration — a sleeve shortened, a zip replaced, a lining rebuilt — we're one WhatsApp message away.
