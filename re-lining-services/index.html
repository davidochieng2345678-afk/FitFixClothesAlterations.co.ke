The garment inside the garment

Why a lining fails long before the outer fabric does

A jacket lining is invisible most of the time. You only notice it when it starts to bother you — a pull at the armpit when you raise your arm, a torn seam that catches on your shirt, a shine patch on the back where the lining has worn thin against your spine. By the time you notice, the outer fabric is usually still perfect. That's the whole reason re-lining exists: because the best leather jacket, the best wool coat and the best suit jacket will outlast three or four linings before the outer fabric needs anything.

Linings fail at the armpits first — always

Every jacket lining fails at the armpit before anywhere else. It's the one point in the garment that takes friction from every movement of your arm — lifting, reaching, driving, typing. A lining that touches the skin through a shirt, on a body that sweats slightly, on a garment worn two or three times a week, will wear through the armpit fabric within twelve to eighteen months. On cheaper linings (thin polyester, low-thread-count viscose), it can be as short as six months. On the good side, this means the failure is predictable: if you own a lined jacket and you wear it regularly, the armpits will go first. And because they go first, they're also the cheapest part of the lining to repair. A patch under each armpit costs KSh 800 to KSh 1,200. A full lining replacement costs KSh 2,000 to KSh 4,000. The patch saves the jacket for another two years and costs a third of what a replacement does. Knowing when to patch and when to replace is most of what a re-lining consultation is about.

The lining breathes differently from the outer

On every lined jacket, the outer fabric and the lining move independently. The outer is usually a woven wool, cotton, leather or synthetic. The lining is usually a smoother, lighter fabric — bemberg, satin, cupro, or a fine polyester. The lining is designed to slide against your shirt so that putting on the jacket is easy and so that the outer fabric doesn't catch on what you're wearing underneath. That smoothness is the entire point. Which means: when the lining wears out, the jacket becomes harder to put on, harder to take off, and less comfortable to wear, even though nothing about the outer fabric has changed. This is the second reason people re-line: not because the lining looks bad, but because the jacket no longer slides on the way it used to.

A lining is not one piece — it's a set of panels

A jacket lining looks like one continuous piece of fabric. It isn't. It's cut into panels that mirror the outer garment: a back panel, two front panels (left and right), two sleeve linings, sometimes a yoke at the back, sometimes a pleat down the centre back for movement. Each panel is cut to a slightly different pattern than the outer, because the lining has to be looser than the outer fabric — usually 3 to 5 mm wider on every side, with extra fullness at the elbow and the centre back. When we replace a lining, we don't just copy the old one. We measure the outer garment, we account for the extra fullness in the new panels, and we hand-finish the seams so the lining floats freely inside the jacket. That's why a proper lining replacement takes longer than most clients expect — it's not one garment, it's five or six.

Bemberg, cupro, satin, silk — the fabric matters

The best suit jacket linings in the world are cut from bemberg (also called cupro — it's the same fibre, a regenerated cellulose). Bemberg has a specific feel — cool, slightly crisp, with a soft drape — that no polyester can replicate. It breathes, it doesn't cling, and it ages beautifully. The second-best is silk — lighter and softer than bemberg, but more fragile and more expensive. Satin is a weave, not a fibre, so a "satin lining" can be polyester satin, silk satin or cupro satin; the fibre inside matters more than the name on the label. Viscose is close to bemberg but less durable. Polyester is what most off-the-rack jackets use, and it's fine for a light summer jacket — but on a heavy wool coat or a leather jacket, polyester lining will pill, cling and crack within a few years. When we re-line a jacket, the client chooses the fibre. Our default recommendation for a wool coat is bemberg; for a leather jacket, quilted cupro; for a summer blazer, a lightweight polyester is honest and reasonable.

The underarm shield — a small piece that matters

On most good jackets, there is an extra piece of fabric under the arm called the underarm shield (or "dress shield"). It's usually a matching or contrasting panel set into the lining at the armpit, and it exists to absorb sweat and protect the outer fabric from the moisture that would otherwise reach it. On cheaper jackets, the shield is missing, and the lining itself takes all the sweat damage — which is why the armpits fail so quickly on budget garments. When we re-line a jacket, we always add or replace the underarm shield. It adds KSh 200 to KSh 400 to the job and roughly doubles the number of years the new lining will last before it needs attention again. Most Nairobi tailors skip this step. We don't.

Why this matters to your wallet

A jacket re-lining at KSh 2,000 to KSh 4,000 can extend the life of a KSh 40,000 wool coat, a KSh 60,000 leather jacket, or a KSh 90,000 cashmere overcoat by five to ten years. On a KSh 2,500 market jacket, that same KSh 2,000 job doesn't make sense — the outer fabric will fail before the new lining is fully broken in. Our job is to look at the garment honestly and tell you which side of that line you're on. We turn down re-lining jobs every month because the coat behind them isn't worth the work. We take every job where it is — because a rescued jacket is a garment that stays out of landfill, and because doing the work properly the first time is always cheaper than replacing it. Not all linings are equal

Three lining tiers — three completely different rebuilds

A KSh 2,500 market jacket with a polyester lining and a KSh 90,000 cashmere overcoat with a hand-set bemberg lining require completely different handling. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be damaged. Here's how we categorise linings before deciding what technique to use.

Entry tier

Polyester and basic viscose linings

Mass-produced linings from market jackets, fast fashion and mid-tier retail. Usually a thin polyester taffeta or a basic viscose, 60–80 gsm. Slick, slightly stiff, and prone to pilling where it rubs against the outer fabric. Often glued rather than stitched at the shoulder seam and armhole — a machine-bagged construction that keeps costs down. No underarm shield. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 2,000 and KSh 8,000.

What we do differentlyOn a lined jacket at this tier, we never reuse the original shoulder seam or armhole opening — we re-cut those areas cleanly and hand-finish the new lining at the same points, because the original bagged seam is not strong enough to survive being reopened. We also add an underarm shield to every budget-tier re-lining. Without it, the new lining will fail at the armpits within a year, exactly as the old one did.

Everyday tier

Bemberg, cupro and quality viscose

Real bemberg (cupro) linings — the standard for Italian and British tailored jackets. Also quality viscose and good polyester cupro blends. Weight 80–110 gsm. Cool to the touch, breathes well, doesn't cling, ages beautifully. Set into the garment with a mix of machine seams at the body and hand-finishing at the armhole and the sleeve lining. Common on mid-range suits, wool coats and mid-tier leather jackets between KSh 12,000 and KSh 40,000.

What we do differentlyBemberg is a specialist fibre — it doesn't like pins (they leave marks), it doesn't like sharp needles (they cut the weave), and it doesn't like being pressed flat on a hard surface (it glazes). We use fine ball-point needles, minimal pinning, and press bemberg over a rounded ham rather than a flat ironing board. We also hand-finish the armhole and the sleeve lining wherever the original was hand-finished — because a machine-stitched bemberg armhole will not sit as cleanly as the original, and the client will notice.

Premium tier

Silk, cupro-silk blends and quilted luxury linings

Pure silk linings on designer coats — usually found on Japanese and Italian tailoring, and on some cashmere overcoats and bespoke commissions. Cupro-silk blends, which give the cool handle of cupro with the softness of silk. Quilted linings on high-end winter coats and leather jackets, where a layer of wadding is stitched between the lining fabric and a backing cloth. Weight varies from 60 gsm (silk) to 250 gsm (quilted wadding). Hand-set armholes, hand-finished sleeve linings, sometimes hand-sewn seam allowances visible from the inside.

What we do differentlyOn this tier the re-lining has to be invisible and the construction has to be preserved. Silk is hand-finished through every seam because a machine would mark the fabric along the stitch line. Quilted linings are rebuilt to match the original quilting pattern — we don't just substitute a flat bemberg. On coats with visible hand-stitching at the inside edges (the signature of some Japanese and Italian makers), we preserve the visible stitches so the coat still reads as the garment it was. This takes longer and costs more. It's the difference between a KSh 90,000 coat that stays a KSh 90,000 coat and one that looks home-restored.

If you're not sure which tier your garment falls into, send a close-up photo of the lining fabric on the inside and the label on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the fibre and the construction how the re-lining should be handled.

The five parts of a lining that affect the rebuild

What a lining actually is — piece by piece

Most clients describe a lining as "the inside of the jacket." That description doesn't help anyone doing the work. Structurally, a lining has five parts, and each one changes how the rebuild should be done. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a lining.

01

The body panels — front, back and side

The lining body is cut into panels that mirror the outer garment: two fronts, one back, plus side panels where the outer has them. The lining panels are cut looser than the outer fabric by 3 to 5 mm on every side, because the lining has to be able to move against the outer without pulling. On jackets with a centre-back pleat (a small vertical fold at the middle of the back lining), the pleat exists to give extra room when you reach forward — that's the pleat that tears when the lining gets old, and one of the first signs the lining is finished. We always recreate the centre-back pleat when we re-line a jacket.

02

The sleeve linings

Sleeve linings are separate tubes that run from the armhole to the cuff. On most jackets they're cut on the cross-grain so the fabric gives a little at the elbow, and they're cut slightly longer than the outer sleeve so the lining doesn't pull when the arm bends. On jackets with a visible cuff lining at the wrist (common on suits and trenchcoats), the sleeve lining is usually turned back and tacked to the outer cuff — that fold is what shows a sliver of colour at the wrist when the jacket is worn. When we re-line, we match that cuff fold to the original. On jacket sleeves where the outer cuff is closed (as on most wool coats and leather jackets), the sleeve lining runs to the cuff seam.

03

The armhole finish — machine-bagged or hand-set

There are two ways a lining is attached at the armhole. Machine-bagged is what most off-the-rack jackets use: the lining is stitched into the armhole by machine, usually from the inside, sometimes through the outer fabric as well. Hand-set is what better jackets use: the lining is turned under at the armhole and slip-stitched by hand, with nothing showing on the outer side. When we re-line a hand-set jacket, we hand-set the new lining at the same point — because re-setting it by machine would leave visible stitch marks on the outer armhole that weren't there before. This is the single most technical part of any re-lining, and it's the reason a proper bemberg re-lining on a hand-finished jacket costs more than a polyester re-lining on a mass-produced one.

04

The hem and the hem swing

The lining doesn't run all the way to the bottom of the garment. It stops 2 to 4 cm short of the outer hem — that gap is called the "hem swing" and it exists so the lining doesn't show below the outer coat hem when you walk or sit. On a coat, the lining hem is usually hand-tacked to the outer hem at a few points so it doesn't drop below. On a suit jacket, the lining hem floats free. When we re-line, we match the original hem swing exactly — too short and the lining pulls inside the coat, too long and it drops below. Both look wrong, and both are the first things a good tailor notices on someone else's alteration.

05

The underarm shield and the inner pocket

Most quality jackets have an underarm shield set into the lining at the armpit — an extra panel designed to absorb sweat and protect both the lining and the outer fabric. It's usually in a contrasting or matching fabric, and it's the piece that keeps the armpit from failing within a year. On jackets that have an inner chest pocket, the pocket bag is set into the lining on the left side (usually) and is a small separate structure that has to be rebuilt along with the new lining. We replace both the shield and the inner pocket on every re-lining unless the client specifically asks us not to — the shield costs us a few minutes and about 20 cm of lining fabric, and it roughly doubles the life of the new lining.

If a tailor quotes a re-lining without asking about the armhole construction, the hem swing, or the underarm shield, they're about to do a job that will look fine for six months and wrong after that. We'd rather you knew that before you committed.

The fibre decides everything

The five lining fibres we work with most often — and how each one behaves

The label on a lining usually says "100% polyester" or "cupro" or "silk" — but the fibre underneath the name matters more than the name itself. Here's what fourteen years of tailoring has taught us, fibre by fibre.

Bemberg / cupro (regenerated cellulose)

The gold standard for jacket linings. Bemberg is made from cotton linter — the short fibres that are left over when cotton is processed — regenerated into a smooth, fine filament. It feels cool to the touch, breathes like cotton, drapes beautifully, doesn't cling, and doesn't pill. It's also the fibre most likely to survive ten years of wear inside a suit jacket or coat. Bemberg hates pins (it marks), hates sharp needles (they cut the weave), and hates direct heat (it glazes). We use fine ball-point needles and press over a ham. This is the lining we recommend by default for wool coats, cashmere overcoats, and mid-tier suits.

Silk

Lighter, softer and more luxurious than bemberg. Silk linings are found on the most expensive tailored garments — bespoke suits, high-end cashmere overcoats, and some leather jackets. Silk takes dye beautifully and has a specific sheen that no synthetic can replicate. The trade-off is fragility: silk wears through faster than bemberg in high-friction areas, is more easily damaged by sweat and deodorant, and needs to be hand-washed or dry-cleaned carefully. On a garment where the lining will only be touched lightly (a coat worn over a suit), silk is beautiful. On a garment worn against the skin (a light summer jacket), silk will not last as long as bemberg.

Viscose

The most common "nice" lining fibre in mid-tier retail. Viscose is also a regenerated cellulose, but it's made differently from bemberg and ends up softer and less durable. It's cheaper, it's widely available, and it's honest about what it is — a good lining for a summer jacket or a mid-range blazer that will be worn lightly. Viscose doesn't handle repeated washing well and can shrink if it gets wet, which is why we advise dry-cleaning any garment with a viscose lining. For a client who wants a good lining without paying for bemberg, viscose is usually the right answer.

Polyester

The workhorse lining fibre of the modern wardrobe. Cheap, tough, washes well, doesn't shrink, doesn't fade. On a light casual jacket (a bomber, a utility coat, a light blazer worn a few times a month), polyester lining is entirely reasonable and lasts for years. The trade-off is that polyester clings, doesn't breathe well, and pills against wool or coarse outer fabric — which is exactly why polyester linings in wool coats fail faster than bemberg. On a heavy coat or a leather jacket, we steer clients toward bemberg or a quilted cupro, because polyester lining in a heavy garment is a false economy.

Quilted cupro and wadded linings

On winter coats, leather jackets and some overcoats, the lining is quilted — a layer of wadding (usually polyester or wool) stitched between the lining fabric and a thin backing cloth. Quilted linings are what make a leather jacket warm in winter and what make a wool overcoat work in a Nairobi cold season. Rebuilding a quilted lining is a more involved job than a flat lining because the quilting pattern has to be recreated — either the classic diamond quilting, the horizontal channel quilting, or the diagonal chevron pattern. We match the original pattern wherever possible, and where the original is a synthetic quilting that the client wants to upgrade, we can rebuild in a wool-wadded quilt for extra warmth. Quilted re-linings cost slightly more than flat linings because there is more than one layer of fabric to handle.

What we won't use

We don't use thin "economy" polyester taffeta as a substitute for bemberg on a wool coat. We don't use glue-bonded linings on any garment — bagged construction is what holds a lining, and glued linings are what makes them fail. And we don't substitute a lighter weight lining for a heavier one on a coat, or a heavier one for a lighter one on a summer jacket — the weight of the lining has to match the weight of the outer, or the garment behaves wrong when worn. If we can't source the right fibre for the job, we'll tell you honestly before we take the order rather than doing the work with a compromise. It's the same standard we hold on blazer alterations.

If you don't know what your lining is made of, send a close-up photo of the fabric inside the garment plus a photo of the inside label on WhatsApp. We'll identify the fibre and tell you straight what's possible.

A short note from the bench

Why I put an underarm shield in every jacket I re-line

If you've ever had a jacket lining fail, it failed at the armpit. Not at the shoulder, not at the hem, not at the sleeve — at the armpit, first, always. That's not a coincidence. The armpit is the one place in a jacket that takes friction on every single movement of your arm. Reaching for a cup of tea. Opening a car door. Typing. Sitting down. Every one of those movements rubs the lining against itself, against the outer fabric, and against whatever you're wearing underneath. No other part of the jacket takes that kind of punishment. And the sweat and deodorant that reach that area — even through a shirt — are what finally kill the fibres.

Which is why a proper jacket has an underarm shield. It's a small piece of fabric — usually a matching or lightly contrasting panel — set into the lining right at the armpit. It's designed to sit between the lining and the outer fabric, and it takes the sweat so the lining doesn't have to. On a good suit jacket from a maker who cares, the shield is always there. On a mass-produced jacket from a Nairobi wholesaler, it's almost never there — because a shield costs about 20 centimetres of lining fabric and eight minutes of labour, and on a garment that retails at KSh 3,500 that adds up over a production run of ten thousand.

When I re-line a jacket, I always add an underarm shield — even if the original didn't have one. It adds KSh 400 per side to the job, which comes to about KSh 800 for both armpits. What does that KSh 800 buy? Roughly three to five extra years of life from the new lining before the armpits fail again. On a jacket whose owner paid KSh 40,000 for it, that's the best KSh 800 they will ever spend on that garment. On a jacket whose owner paid KSh 8,000, it's still the best KSh 800 — because it saves a KSh 2,000 re-lining two years earlier than would otherwise be necessary. Most Nairobi tailors skip the shield because it's fiddly and because the client won't notice the absence. They're right that clients don't notice — until the lining fails again two years later, at which point the client doesn't come back.

The other thing I want clients to know is about the fibre. When I ask what a client wants in their re-lining, most of them say "the same as it was." Sometimes that's the right answer — on a light summer jacket, a polyester lining is fine and cheap, and it will outlast the jacket if the jacket is worn lightly. But when a jacket already failed once with a polyester lining against a wool outer, putting polyester back in is asking for the same failure. The right fibre for a wool coat is bemberg or cupro — cool, breathable, no cling, and it doesn't pill against wool. The right fibre for a leather jacket is a quilted cupro, because leather is a skin and needs to slide against something smooth. The right fibre for a summer blazer is honestly a lightweight polyester, because the client is not going to wear it hard enough for the fibre to matter.

Last thing. I turn down re-lining jobs every month. Not because I can't do them — because the honest answer is that the garment isn't worth the work. If the outer fabric is failing alongside the lining, or the canvas has separated on a wool coat, or the leather is already cracked at the elbow and the collar, then a new lining is going to look strange inside a garment that's falling apart around it. I'd rather tell you straight that buying new is the better move than take KSh 3,000 from you for a re-lining on a KSh 4,000 jacket. The clients who come back to me are the ones I've told that to.

Make your lining last between replacements

Eight habits that keep jacket linings out of the workshop

A jacket lining has a finite life — that's the nature of the fibre and the friction. But how long that life lasts depends on four things you control: how you wash the jacket, how you store it, what you wear under it, and how quickly you fix small problems. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect a freshly re-lined garment.

1. Dry clean, don't wash — especially viscose and bemberg

Bemberg, cupro, viscose and silk linings all shrink or glaze when they meet water and heat. Home washing is the single fastest way to kill a good lining. If a jacket has a bemberg or viscose lining, dry clean it — twice a year at most, and only when it actually needs cleaning. Between cleans, air the jacket on a hanger for an hour after every wear, which lifts most of what accumulates. If you must spot clean, use a slightly damp cloth and never rub — dabbing only, and dry flat.

2. Wear a smooth shirt under the jacket, not a rough one

The lining takes friction from whatever is under it. A smooth cotton or silk shirt slides easily; a rough knit, a textured linen, or a wool jumper drags against the lining and wears it out faster. If you wear the same jacket over the same rough shirt five days a week, the lining will fail at the armpits and the centre back within eighteen months. Rotate your shirts and wear the smoother ones with lined jackets — this one habit extends lining life by years.

3. Don't hang a jacket by the hood or by a single hook

Lined jackets — especially wool coats and leather jackets — should hang from the shoulders on a wide wooden or padded hanger, never from a single narrow hook at the collar. A hook concentrates the whole weight of the jacket at one point and pulls the lining out of alignment at the shoulder seam, which is where a lining failure often starts. Wide hangers distribute the weight evenly across the shoulders and keep the lining sitting where it was sewn to sit.

4. Give the jacket a day off between wears

Every lining recovers its shape overnight. The fibres relax, the moisture evaporates, and the fabric returns to its original dimensions. Wear the same jacket five days a week and the lining never gets that recovery — the armpit fabric stays stretched, the back stays damp, and the fibres fatigue faster. If you own two or three lined jackets and rotate them, each one lasts roughly twice as long. This single habit is worth more than any repair we can do.

5. Air the jacket after every wear before it goes back in the cupboard

A lined jacket absorbs moisture from your body, your breath and the outside air every time you wear it. If it goes straight back into a closed wardrobe, that moisture stays in the lining and slowly degrades the fibre — which is exactly why linings from jackets stored badly always smell slightly stale. Hang the jacket on a hook or a chair back for an hour before it goes into the cupboard. Long enough for the moisture to evaporate and the lining to recover its shape.

6. Don't store a jacket in a plastic bag

Plastic traps moisture against the lining and creates exactly the warm, humid environment that mildew and moths love. Use a breathable cotton or canvas garment bag — the same rule as for wool coats. If you've been storing a lined jacket in a dry-cleaner's plastic bag between wears, take it out today. Every month in plastic increases the risk of mould damage that will show up as dark spots along the lining within a season.

7. Fix an armpit tear the moment you notice it

An armpit patch is KSh 800 today. Left for six months, the tear grows along the grain, the fabric around it weakens, and the fix becomes a KSh 1,200 patch or, worse, a KSh 2,000+ full re-lining. The clients who spend the least on lining repairs are the ones who message us the first time they notice a small pull at the armpit. Every repair on a lining is cheaper when done early, and every lining replacement that gets postponed is one that was avoidable.

8. Choose the right fibre when you re-line — and never compromise

When we re-line a jacket, the fibre you choose determines how long the new lining lasts. Bemberg lasts longer than polyester against a wool outer. Quilted cupro lasts longer than a flat lining inside a leather jacket. A lightweight polyester is fine for a summer blazer but wrong for a winter coat. If your first re-lining failed faster than you expected, it's usually because the fibre was the wrong choice — most often, polyester where bemberg should have been used. Choosing correctly the second time roughly triples how long you'll go before the next re-lining.

Follow those eight habits and your lining will hold its shape for years longer than it would otherwise. And when it does eventually need repair or replacement, we're one WhatsApp message away. See our fabric patching and darning & invisible mending pages for the small-repair side of the work.
