The highest-skill suit alteration

Why shoulder reduction is the one alteration most Nairobi tailors refuse

Every tailor who works on suits knows the same thing: the shoulder is the anchor. If the shoulders fit, everything else on the jacket can be adjusted to fit the wearer. If the shoulders don't fit, no amount of other work will make the jacket look right. That's the reason shoulder reduction is a specialist job — and the reason most Nairobi tailors will tell a client honestly that they can't do it, rather than take the money and risk ruining the jacket.

The shoulder seam is the one seam you can't hide

When we slim the body of a jacket, the new seam runs parallel to the old one and hides it — the old needle holes sit under the new seam and nothing shows. When we shorten a sleeve, the same applies — the old cuff marks sit under the new cuff and nothing shows. The shoulder seam is different. When we reduce the shoulder width, the shoulder seam has to move inward by 1 to 2 centimetres. The old seam line is now visible as a faint row of needle holes inside the new seam, closer to the neck. On dense, felted wool — the classic suit cloth — the holes close up within a few days because the fibres swell. On looser or lighter cloth, they don't close as well, and on a very pale suit they can show as a faint stitched line just inside the new shoulder. This is why shoulder reduction should never be the first alteration attempted on a suit — and why the client has to accept the small risk before we start.

The sleeve head has to be re-set by hand

The sleeve head is the part of the sleeve that joins the shoulder — a curved, gathered piece of cloth that sits over the top of the shoulder and lets the sleeve roll naturally as the wearer moves. When we reduce the shoulder width, the sleeve head has to come out entirely, and then be re-set by hand at the new position. This is a hand job — a machine cannot reproduce the soft roll of a properly set sleeve head because the head needs deliberate, uneven "ease" (a small amount of extra fullness) that a machine can't distribute. On a canvased jacket, the shoulder canvas inside also has to be trimmed and re-stitched by hand to match the new width. A shoulder reduction on a canvased jacket takes four to six hours of bench work, most of it with a hand needle.

The padding has to be re-cut or replaced

Most suit jackets have some shoulder padding — usually a thin layer of wadding or foam that gives the shoulder a little structure. On a well-made jacket, the padding is shaped to match the shoulder curve. When we reduce the shoulder width, the padding has to be re-cut to the new shape — sometimes with the same padding material, sometimes replaced entirely if the original was bonded and won't re-cut cleanly. On jackets with very light padding or no padding at all (common on Italian and Neapolitan tailoring), this step is quick. On heavy-padded British-style jackets, the padding replacement is a significant part of the job and adds to the price.

The armhole changes with the shoulder — always

The armhole is the opening in the jacket where the sleeve is set. When you reduce the shoulder width, the armhole has to move inward with it — otherwise the armhole sits too far out and the sleeve hangs wrong. On some jackets, the armhole can be re-cut to the new position cleanly. On others — especially jackets with hand-set sleeve heads and finished armholes — re-cutting is not possible without leaving visible marks on the surrounding cloth. We check this on every shoulder reduction before we agree to the job. If the armhole can't be adjusted cleanly, we tell the client that the shoulder reduction isn't worth doing on that particular jacket.

There is a hard limit on how much you can reduce

Shoulder reduction is not like slimming — you can't take in 5 centimetres and expect the jacket to still look like a jacket. On a standard suit, the maximum clean reduction is about 1.5 to 2 centimetres per side (3 to 4 centimetres total). Reduce more than that and the armhole has to move so far inward that the sleeve ends up too narrow for the arm, and the chest panel no longer fits properly around the body. On a heavily structured jacket or one with reinforced shoulders, the limit is often lower — sometimes just 1 centimetre per side. We measure the actual limit on every jacket before committing to the work, and we tell the client upfront if the jacket can only take a smaller reduction than they want. A smaller reduction that keeps the jacket wearable is better than a larger one that ruins it.

Why this matters to your wallet

A shoulder reduction at KSh 3,500 is the most expensive single jacket alteration we do — more than sleeve shortening, more than body slimming, more than a hem. That's not because we're expensive; it's because the work is genuinely that slow. It takes four to six hours of hand-bench time. It also has a hard risk: if it's done wrong, the jacket can't be un-done. On the jackets that can take it, the result is transformative. It's the same standard we hold on suit alterations and blazer alterations.

Not all suit shoulders are the same

Three shoulder tiers — three completely different jobs

A fused suit jacket with no canvas and a full-canvased Italian jacket with hand-set shoulders require completely different handling. If a tailor treats them the same way, one of them will be ruined. Here's how we categorise suit shoulders before deciding what technique to use.

Entry tier

Fused shoulders — mass-market suits

Most suits from market suppliers, fast-fashion and mid-tier retail. The shoulder area is fused — a layer of heat-activated adhesive holds the interlining to the front panel rather than a floating canvas. Padding is often a thin layer of bonded foam, sometimes shaped to the shoulder curve but usually a single piece. No hand-work at the shoulder. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 8,000 and KSh 20,000.

What we do differentlyOn a fused shoulder, the padding can be re-cut but not always re-shaped — the foam is bonded and can't be steamed into a new curve the way a horsehair pad can. On this tier, we usually replace the padding with a new piece rather than trying to reuse the original. The shoulder seam is machine-stitched at the new position, and the visible old seam marks often close well on dense poly-wool blends. Fused shoulders usually take a 1 to 1.5 cm reduction per side cleanly.

Everyday tier

Half-canvased shoulders — better suits

Suits from SuitSupply, Boggi, mid-range Italian and better regional makers. The chest is canvased (a floating horsehair layer), and the shoulders are half-canvased. The sleeve head is set by hand, sometimes with a small amount of deliberate "ease". The shoulder padding is a shaped horsehair or wool pad. The armhole is hand-finished on better examples. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 25,000 and KSh 60,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier the canvas has to be hand-trimmed and re-set at the new shoulder width. The sleeve head is re-set by hand — no machine work at the shoulder. We re-shape the horsehair padding to match the new shoulder curve by pressing it over a ham rather than replacing it. The visible old seam line usually closes within a week on quality wool — the cloth swells back. A 1.5 to 2 cm reduction per side is possible if the armhole can be adjusted cleanly.

Premium tier

Full-canvased shoulders — the highest tier

Full-canvased jackets from Italian, British and Japanese makers — Zegna, Canali, Brioni, Huntsman, Ring Jacket. Hand-set sleeve heads with a specific, deliberately uneven "ease". Hand-stitched shoulder canvas. Hand-finished armholes. Shaped horsehair padding that's press-formed to the wearer's shoulder curve. The shoulder is the single most important hand-finished element of the jacket. Common above KSh 90,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier every part of the shoulder work is hand-finished. We re-set the sleeve head by hand at the new position and match the original "ease" — a small, deliberate unevenness in the sleeve head that machine work cannot reproduce. We hand-trim the canvas, hand-press the padding over a ham, and hand-finish every seam. This takes a full day, sometimes more. On a KSh 90,000 jacket, shoulder reduction is the most demanding alteration we do — and the one we're proudest of when it goes right.

If you're not sure which tier your suit falls into, send a photo of the inside front panel (canvas is often visible behind the lining) and one of the shoulder line from the front and back on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the reduction should be handled.

The five parts of the shoulder that affect the reduction

What a shoulder reduction actually involves — piece by piece

Most people think of shoulder reduction as "take in the shoulder seam." That description misses the four other parts of the jacket that change when the shoulder changes. Structurally, a shoulder reduction involves five parts of the jacket, and each one has to be handled correctly. Here's what we look at before we take scissors to a jacket.

01

The shoulder seam — the visible join between the sleeve and the body

The shoulder seam runs from the collar down to the armhole, on top of the shoulder. On a well-made jacket, the seam sits exactly on the edge of the wearer's natural shoulder bone — not short of it, not past it. When the jacket's shoulders are too wide, that seam sits past the natural shoulder, which makes the whole jacket look oversized even if the body fits. Reducing the shoulder width means moving that seam inward by 1 to 2 centimetres. The old seam line, as noted above, may leave faint needle-hole marks inside the new seam. On dense wool, they close within a few days. On lighter cloth, they may not.

02

The sleeve head — the curved join that lets the sleeve roll

The sleeve head is the curved top of the sleeve where it joins the shoulder. On a well-made jacket it's set with deliberate "ease" — a small, uneven amount of extra fullness that lets the sleeve roll naturally as the wearer moves. Machine-set sleeve heads look flat by comparison. When we reduce the shoulder width, the sleeve head has to be removed entirely and re-set by hand at the new position. This is the single most time-consuming part of a shoulder reduction, and it's what separates a shoulder reduction that looks factory from one that looks altered. There is no shortcut for this step.

03

The shoulder canvas and padding — the internal structure

On any canvased jacket, there's a piece of horsehair canvas inside the shoulder that gives it shape. There's also usually a layer of padding — sometimes thin, sometimes substantial — that gives the shoulder its structure. When we reduce shoulder width, both have to be trimmed or reshaped. The canvas is hand-trimmed to match the new width. The padding is either re-cut (if it's a shaped horsehair pad) or replaced (if it's a bonded foam pad that can't be re-shaped). On a heavy-padded British-style jacket, this is a substantial piece of work. On a lightly padded Italian jacket, it's quick.

04

The armhole — the opening where the sleeve is set into the body

The armhole is the curved opening in the jacket where the sleeve is attached. When the shoulder width is reduced, the armhole has to move inward with it. On some jackets, the armhole can be re-cut to the new position cleanly. On others — especially jackets with hand-finished armholes and fragile cloth — re-cutting is not possible without leaving visible marks. We check this before agreeing to any shoulder reduction. If the armhole can't be adjusted cleanly, we tell the client the job isn't worth doing on that jacket, and we mean it.

05

The shoulder slope — the angle from the neck to the shoulder point

Every wearer's shoulders slope at a slightly different angle. Some people have flat shoulders (almost horizontal), some have steeply sloping shoulders, and most are somewhere in between. A well-made jacket's shoulder slope matches the wearer's. On an off-the-rack suit, the slope is cut for an average and fits almost nobody exactly. Shoulder slope correction is a separate alteration from width reduction — it changes the angle of the shoulder line, not the width. On a canvased jacket, slope correction means re-shaping the shoulder canvas and re-setting the sleeve head at the new angle. Same time and skill as a width reduction, and often done at the same time if both are needed.

If a tailor quotes a shoulder reduction without asking about the armhole, the sleeve head, or the shoulder slope, they're about to do a job that will look right for a week and wrong for a decade. We'd rather ask five questions upfront than take on a job we can't finish properly.

The cloth decides the outcome

The five suit cloths we reduce shoulders on most often — and how each one behaves

Shoulder reduction is more sensitive to cloth than any other suit alteration, because the visible outcome depends on how well the needle holes close up after the seam moves. Here's our honest breakdown by cloth type, the same one we'd give you on WhatsApp before quoting.

Worsted wool — the classic business suit

The best cloth for shoulder reduction. Worsted wool is tightly woven, dense, and the fibres swell back after a needle passes through — which means the old shoulder seam holes close up within a few days. On a KSh 40,000 worsted suit, a shoulder reduction is genuinely invisible three weeks later. The trade-off is that worsted holds a press line — so when we re-shape the sleeve head over a ham, we have to be careful not to press a hard line into the cloth. We steam-press over a ham and let the sleeve head settle into its new curve naturally. This is the cloth we'd recommend shoulder reduction on above all others.

Fresco and hopsack — summer cloths

Open-weave wool cloths that breathe. Fresco is tightly twisted; hopsack is a looser basket-weave. Both have visible texture that distracts the eye from small marks at the old shoulder seam — which is helpful for shoulder reduction. The trade-off is that open-weave cloth doesn't swell back as much as tightly woven worsted, so needle holes from the old seam stay visible longer. On a fresco or hopsack jacket, we accept that the old shoulder line may show as a faint shadow for two to three months after the reduction. It usually fades to nothing after that. Send a close-up of the cloth before we start so we can tell you how visible the shadow will be.

Flannel and brushed wool

The best cloth for hiding a shoulder reduction — because the brushed surface has a nap that distracts the eye from any seam marks. Flannel also has natural give, which helps the sleeve head settle into its new curve. The trade-off is that flannel shows wear faster than worsted at stress points (elbows, shoulders), so on a flannel jacket that's already been worn hard, the shoulder area may be thin before we even start. We check the fabric thickness at the shoulder before agreeing to a reduction. If it's already worn thin, a shoulder reduction will accelerate the failure.

Wool-silk and wool-cashmere blends

The cloth of premium suits. Wool-silk blends give a slight sheen and beautiful drape; wool-cashmere blends are softer and warmer. Both are more delicate than pure wool and more expensive. On a wool-silk suit, needle holes are more likely to show after a shoulder reduction — silk doesn't swell back the way wool does. On a wool-cashmere suit, the cashmere content responds beautifully to steam but hates direct heat, so we work with a lot of steam and very little iron at the shoulder. On this tier, shoulder reduction should only be attempted if the client genuinely needs it — and the honest answer is sometimes that the small shadow at the old seam is a trade-off the client has to accept.

Poly-wool blends — budget and mid-tier suits

Common on entry-tier and mid-tier suits. The polyester content makes the cloth more resistant to wrinkling but less forgiving under a needle — poly fibres don't swell back like wool, so old seam holes are more visible after a shoulder reduction. On a poly-wool suit, the reduction is still possible but the old seam marks may never fully disappear. The other limitation is that poly-wool cloth can't take the same level of hand-shaping as pure wool — the sleeve head "ease" is more resistant to being hand-formed. This affects the final sleeve-head roll. On a poly-wool suit with a fused shoulder, shoulder reduction is often not worth doing — the cost approaches the value of the jacket.

What we won't reduce

We turn down shoulder reduction jobs every month. On a heavily padded British-style jacket with a shallow armhole, the reduction can't be done cleanly because the armhole can't be re-cut — the padding and canvas have been engineered to sit exactly where they are. On a suit jacket whose shoulder has already been re-set by a previous tailor (visible as a second row of needle holes at the shoulder seam), a second reduction moves into compromised cloth. On a pale-coloured lightweight summer suit, the old seam marks will show even after a year. If we can't do a job properly, we don't take the money. It's the same standard we hold on suit jacket lengthening.

A short note from the bench

Why I turn down shoulder reduction jobs more than any other alteration

Of every alteration I do on a suit — sleeve shortening, body slimming, jacket length, trouser work — shoulder reduction is the one I say no to most often. Not because the work is beyond me. I've been doing shoulder reductions for fourteen years. I say no because the shoulder is the one part of a jacket that cannot be un-done. Every other alteration, if it goes wrong, can usually be corrected. Shoulder work can't. Once you've moved the shoulder seam inward, the old seam line is inside the new one. If the armhole has been re-cut and the sleeve head re-set wrong, there's no way to bring it back. The jacket is permanently compromised.

There are three specific situations where I turn the job down. The first is when the armhole can't be re-cut cleanly. Shoulder reduction moves the shoulder seam inward by 1 to 2 centimetres, which means the armhole has to move inward with it. On most suits, that's fine — the armhole is a machine-set circle that we can re-stitch at the new position. But on jackets with hand-finished armholes, or fragile cloth that marks easily, or a shallow armhole that's already close to the edge of the jacket body, re-cutting the armhole leaves visible marks on the surrounding cloth. On those jackets, the honest answer is that the shoulder reduction isn't worth doing. I'd rather tell the client that upfront than hand them back a jacket with visible marks at the armhole.

The second situation is when the client asks for more reduction than the jacket can take. Most jackets can only be safely reduced by 1.5 to 2 centimetres per side. Beyond that, the armhole has to move so far inward that the sleeve ends up too narrow for the arm, and the chest panel no longer fits properly around the body. On some heavily structured jackets, the limit is even lower — sometimes just 1 centimetre per side. When a client asks me to reduce their shoulders by 3 or 4 centimetres, I have to tell them honestly that the jacket can't take it. Sometimes they accept a smaller reduction and are happy with the result. Sometimes they decide not to have the alteration done at all. Either way, I'd rather they knew the limit before we started than found out after.

The third situation is when the cloth is too fragile or the previous alteration has already compromised the shoulder. Pale lightweight summer suits show old seam marks even after a year. Jackets that have already had a shoulder reduction attempted by a previous tailor have a row of needle holes at the original seam position — a second reduction moves into compromised cloth and shows both rows of holes. And on a suit jacket with moth damage along the shoulder, a shoulder reduction makes the damage more visible because the surrounding cloth is now doing more work. On those jackets, I explain the situation and I let the client decide. If they choose to go ahead anyway, we plan the work carefully together. If the honest answer is "don't do it," I say so.

The clients who get the best shoulder reductions from me are the ones who understand the trade-offs. On a KSh 40,000 worsted wool suit, the reduction is invisible three weeks later — the needle holes close, the sleeve head settles, the whole jacket looks like it was made for the wearer. On a pale lightweight summer suit, the old seam marks may stay faintly visible for a year. On a very heavily padded jacket, the reduction may not be worth doing. I don't hide these trade-offs. I tell the client what the jacket can take, what the risks are, and what the result will look like in six months. And then we decide together whether to go ahead. That's the standard — and it's the reason clients bring me their best jackets instead of their cheapest ones.

Make the re-set shoulder last

Eight habits that protect a re-set shoulder

A shoulder reduction moves the shoulder seam inward by 1 to 2 centimetres and re-sets the sleeve head by hand. Both are delicate — the sleeve head has more hand-work than any other part of the jacket, and the canvas inside the shoulder has been trimmed and re-anchored. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect a shoulder-reduced jacket.

1. Give the jacket a week before the first wear

After a shoulder reduction, the sleeve head needs seven to ten days to settle into its new curve. The hand-stitching relaxes slightly, the canvas inside the shoulder re-sets to the new shape, and the cloth around the new shoulder seam recovers its natural grain. Wear the jacket briefly during that week — try it on twice, stand in it for a few minutes — so the cloth adjusts to being worn at the new width. Don't wear it to an event until that week is done. On a heavy jacket, give it ten days. On a lightweight summer jacket, seven is fine.

2. Never hang it by a single hook at the collar

After shoulder reduction, the entire weight of the jacket is supported by the shoulders that have just been re-set. On a thin wire hanger, or hanging from a single hook at the collar, the weight concentrates at a single narrow point at the neck — which stretches the newly re-set shoulder seam and can pull the canvas out of shape. Always use a wide wooden or padded hanger with a shaped shoulder. The hanger should support the full width of the jacket shoulder, not just a narrow strip. This is the single most important habit for protecting a re-set shoulder.

3. Let the jacket rest 24 to 48 hours between wears

After shoulder reduction, the hand-set sleeve head and the re-anchored canvas need more recovery time than a factory jacket. The hand-stitching relaxes slowly after being worn — 24 hours minimum, ideally 48. Wear the same jacket three days in a row and the sleeve head stays compressed, the shoulder seam stays under load, and the whole shoulder area never fully relaxes. Rotate between two jackets — the shoulder-reduced one and another — and each one lasts roughly twice as long.

4. Brush the shoulder seam after every wear

The shoulder seam collects dust more than any other seam on a suit because it's the highest point on the jacket. Dust works into the new shoulder seam and, over time, can make the seam line more visible. A soft clothes brush, used along the shoulder seam after every wear, removes dust before it settles. Brush in the direction of the grain — with the nap, then against, then with again. Two minutes per jacket. This is especially important in Nairobi during the July and August dust season.

5. Steam, never press, the shoulder area

Never press a shoulder-reduced jacket with a home iron. The re-set sleeve head has deliberate, hand-worked "ease" that a flat iron will destroy. The canvas inside the shoulder will flatten and lose its shape under direct heat. If the shoulder area looks wrinkled, hang the jacket in a steamy bathroom for ten minutes. The steam relaxes the cloth without pressing it flat. If the shoulder area genuinely needs a real press, bring it to us — we press over a rounded ham, which is the only safe way to press a curved shoulder line. We'll do this once for free on any jacket we've shoulder-reduced.

6. Check the sleeve head after every few wears

The sleeve head is where the sleeve joins the shoulder, and it's the part of the jacket that takes the most movement stress. After every few wears, glance at the sleeve head: does it still have a soft roll, or has it gone flat? Is the hand-stitching still even, or has a thread loosened? A single loose thread at the sleeve head is a KSh 500 touch-up today. Left for a month, it becomes a KSh 2,500 full sleeve head re-set. Sleeve head damage is always cheapest to fix the moment it appears — and on a shoulder-reduced jacket, the sleeve head is younger than the rest of the jacket, so it needs a bit more attention during the first year.

7. Keep the shoulder dry — never let it get soaked

Wool shrinks when it gets wet and dries without being re-shaped. On a shoulder-reduced jacket, the shoulder area has more tension than the rest of the jacket — the canvas is doing more work, the sleeve head is under load. If the shoulder gets soaked in the rain and dries naturally, the wool can shrink back slightly, which pulls the shoulder seam and distorts the re-set sleeve head. Dry the jacket flat or on a wide hanger indoors — never near a radiator, never in direct sun. If the shoulder does get soaked, let it dry naturally, then bring it to us for a re-steam and re-shape, which we do free on any jacket we've shoulder-reduced.

8. Fix small shoulder issues before they grow

A single loose stitch at the shoulder seam is a KSh 500 fix today. Left for three months, it becomes a KSh 2,500 sleeve head re-set. Left longer, the shoulder seam can start to gape and the whole shoulder reduction has to be re-done. Same for the sleeve head: a small pull at the sleeve head corner is a KSh 500 touch-up now, a KSh 2,500 re-set if you wait. The clients whose shoulder-reduced jackets last the longest are the ones who message us the moment they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less.

Follow those eight habits and the shoulder reduction will hold its new shape for a decade. And when it does eventually need work — a sleeve adjustment, a lining refresh, a re-press — we're one WhatsApp message away.
