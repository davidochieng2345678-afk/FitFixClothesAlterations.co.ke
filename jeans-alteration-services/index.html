The fabric that remembers everything

Why jeans are harder to alter well than any other trouser

Most tailors treat jeans the same way they treat dress trousers — measure, cut, hem, done. That's the wrong approach for denim, and it's why so many altered jeans end up looking like altered jeans. Denim is not just heavy cotton. It's a fabric that fades on a schedule, stretches where the knee has bent a thousand times, and softens in specific ways that can't be faked or forced. Here's what that means in practice.

Denim has a fade pattern that shouldn't be cut

Every pair of jeans that's been worn for a year or more has a fade pattern — the knees are lighter, the thigh and seat are worn in a specific pattern, and the hem has taken on the wear of the shoes you wear most often. That fade pattern is what makes a pair of jeans yours. When we hem a pair of jeans, the first question I ask is: what happens to the fade pattern if we cut from the bottom? On a pair with light fading at the bottom, cutting removes the fade and the trouser looks fresh at the hem but worn everywhere else — a mismatched look that clients notice immediately. That's why we offer original hem preservation as the default on any jeans that have been worn for more than six months.

Denim has almost no seam allowance — and that changes everything

Dress trousers typically have 2 to 4 cm of hidden seam allowance in the side seams, which lets a tailor let out the waist by one size. Denim usually has 1 to 1.5 cm at most. This means the range of waist alterations on jeans is much smaller — usually 2 to 4 cm take-in, and very rarely anything let out. If a client asks us to let out a pair of jeans that has already been worn hard, we have to check the inside seam allowance before quoting. Sometimes it's there. Often it isn't. When it isn't, the honest answer is that the jeans can't be saved at the waist, but they can still be hemmed, slimmed or repaired.

The heaviest denim needs a walking-foot machine

Heavy denim — 12 oz and above — cannot be sewn on a standard domestic machine without problems. The needle struggles through the fabric, the stitch tension goes uneven, and the hem line ends up visibly crooked. On selvedge denim (14 oz and above), a standard machine will actually damage the fabric by forcing the needle through compressed layers. We use a walking-foot industrial machine on all denim heavier than 10 oz, which feeds the fabric evenly from above and below at the same time. It's the only way to get a factory-standard hem on heavy denim. Most Nairobi alterations shops don't have one.

Selvedge denim has to be handled differently again

Selvedge denim is woven on shuttle looms that produce narrower fabric with a finished edge — the "self-edge" that gives the fabric its name. That edge runs along the outside seam of a selvedge jean, and it's visible when you cuff the hem. On a selvedge jean, hemming has to preserve that self-edge, otherwise the entire point of the denim is lost. We handle selvedge hemming by hand, using a technique that reproduces the original chain-stitch line and keeps the selvedge finish intact. Not every tailor knows this — most will just run it through a machine and hope you don't notice.

Chain-stitch hems matter on quality denim

Original jean hems are usually chain-stitched — a specific stitch pattern that has a slight rope at the edge and produces the fade rings you see on the hems of well-worn denim. When we shorten a hem without preserving the original, the natural look is lost. We offer chain-stitch finish as an option — usually on mid-tier and premium denim — because it's the difference between a hem that looks like it came from the factory and a hem that looks altered. It takes longer and costs a little more, but on a KSh 8,000 pair of denim it's worth it.

Why this matters to your wallet

We charge KSh 1,000 to hem a pair of jeans with a standard overlock finish. A tailor working without denim experience might charge KSh 500 — and shorten it wrong, so the fade no longer matches, or the machine marks the fabric, or the hem twists after the first wash. On a pair of KSh 3,000 jeans that's annoying. On a pair of KSh 15,000 selvedge denim, it's a KSh 15,000 mistake. Doing it right the first time is cheaper than doing it twice. It's the same standard we hold on trouser alterations and chino alterations.

Read the denim before you book

Three denim weights — three completely different alterations

Lightweight fashion denim, mid-weight everyday jeans, and heavy selvedge denim require completely different approaches. If a tailor treats them the same, one of them will end up looking wrong. Here's how I categorise denim before I decide what technique to use.

Lightweight

Fashion and stretch denim (8–10 oz)

Mass-produced fashion jeans from high-street retailers and fast fashion. Fabric weight 8–10 oz with 1–3% elastane for stretch. Sometimes stonewashed or chemically treated. Waistbands often have elastic panels at the sides. Very common in Nairobi, especially on women's jeans and lighter men's cuts.

What we do differentlyLightweight stretch denim pulls back after cutting, so we cut with a small allowance. Waist adjustments on stretch denim need slightly looser tension so the elastane can still move. Hem finishes are usually simpler — machine straight or overlock — because the fabric doesn't take a chain-stitch clearly.

Mid-weight

Everyday denim and premium stretch (10–13 oz)

Uniqlo, Levi's, Diesel, Wrangler, Nudie and mid-range Italian denim. Fabric weight 10–13 oz with 0.5–2% elastane. Heavier weave with a visible twill line and better fade retention. Traditional flat-felled seams throughout, sometimes with rivets at the pocket corners. The most common denim in Nairobi for daily wear.

What we do differentlyMid-weight denim takes a hem well but fades fast at the cut line if the finish isn't right. We use a walking-foot industrial machine and match the original thread weight so the fade pattern continues evenly. Original hem preservation is the standard finish on this weight because the fade at the hem is usually worth saving.

Heavy-duty

Heavy and selvedge denim (13–17 oz)

Japanese selvedge denim, Iron Heart, Momotaro, Samurai, and heavy-duty work denim. Fabric weight 13–17 oz, sometimes unwashed raw denim. Chain-stitch hems with the original rope look, copper rivets, hidden back-pocket rivets, and lined back pockets. Expensive, hard-wearing, and irreplaceable for collectors.

What we do differentlyHeavy denim requires a walking-foot machine on every seam. We hand-finish chain-stitch hems to reproduce the original factory look. Selvedge denim hemming preserves the self-edge. All alterations on this tier are done by me personally — not handed off — because a mistake on selvedge denim shows for years.

If you're not sure what weight your denim is, send a photo of the label and a close-up of the fabric on WhatsApp. I'll identify the weight from the twill and tell you honestly which technique applies.

The five parts of a jean that affect the alteration

What a pair of jeans actually is — piece by piece

Most people describe jeans as "denim trousers." That doesn't help anyone doing the alteration. Structurally, a jean has five parts, and each one changes how the work should be done. Here's what I look at before I take scissors to a pair of denim.

01

The denim weave and weight

Jeans are woven in a twill pattern — a diagonal weave that gives denim its strength and its distinctive surface. Twill is directional; it runs bottom-left to top-right on standard denim, and bottom-right to top-left on reverse twill. When we cut for a hem or a slimming alteration, the cut has to follow the twill direction, otherwise the new seam reads against the grain of the fabric. Weight determines the needle and thread — 8 oz denim uses a fine needle; 14 oz selvedge denim needs a size 100 or 110 needle and a walking-foot machine.

02

The waistband and its construction

Jean waistbands are usually 4 to 5 cm wide and cut on a slight curve so they follow the natural waistline. They're constructed with an internal facing and, on quality jeans, a hidden support strip. When we take in a waist, we re-shape the band, reposition the belt loops, and re-anchor the top button — all three steps. If we cut corners and skip the belt loop repositioning, the loops end up unevenly spaced and the alteration shows immediately.

03

The front pockets and coin pocket

Standard jeans have two front pockets plus a coin pocket inside the right front. The pockets are usually cut from lighter cotton than the denim and riveted at the corners. When we slim a waist, the front pockets shift slightly and their openings tighten. If we don't re-anchor the pocket bag at the top corner, the pocket tears free within a year. On every waist alteration, we re-anchor the pocket corners and reinforce the coin pocket stitching. Small detail, and it's the reason our alterations last.

04

The back yoke and back pockets

The back yoke is a horizontal panel of denim that sits just below the waistband and gives jeans their distinctive shape. The back pockets are usually cut with a slight angle and stitched with a decorative pattern. When we slim a jean at the seat, we have to re-shape the yoke — otherwise the back of the trouser flattens and the pockets sit wrong. Yoke adjustments are one of the most technical denim alterations, and only work on jeans that still have good fabric at the back.

05

The hem — chain-stitch, single-stitch or overlock

Original jean hems are usually chain-stitched — a specific type of stitch that produces a visible rope at the hem and, over time, the fade rings that make worn denim distinctive. When we shorten a hem, we reproduce the original stitch type — chain-stitch for chain-stitch, single-stitch for single-stitch, and overlock for overlock. Matching the finish is the difference between a hem that looks factory and one that looks altered. On selvedge denim, we preserve the self-edge as well.

If a tailor quotes you a jeans alteration without asking about the twill direction, the hem type or the pocket construction, they're guessing — and the alteration will show in the fade pattern within a year.

Choose your hem finish

Three hem finishes — and how to know which one your jeans need

The hem finish is the most visible part of any jeans alteration. Choose wrong and the hem stands out from the rest of the trouser. Choose right and nobody — not even you — will be able to tell the jeans were altered. Here's what each finish is actually for, and how to know which one you want.

Overlock (ON) finish

An overlocked hem is the modern, tidy, mass-produced finish — a serged edge that encloses the raw fabric so it can't fray. On jeans, overlock is used on almost every budget pair because it's fast and clean. When we re-hem with overlock, we reproduce the original stitch count and thread colour so the hem blends with the rest of the trouser. Overlock works best on lightweight and mid-weight stretch denim and on jeans you wear hard. It doesn't produce the visible rope of a chain-stitch, but on fashion denim it's the right call. Overlock hemming is KSh 1,000.

Machine straight finish

A machine straight hem uses a single lockstitch run along the folded hem — the classic hem finish you'd see on a pair of smart-casual trousers rather than on jeans. On jeans, it's less common but works well on straight-leg and boot-cut cuts where the original hem was a plain straight line. The stitch is denser than overlock and produces a cleaner, more formal look. Machine straight hemming is KSh 1,000 and takes slightly longer than overlock on heavy denim because the machine has to run at lower speed.

Original hem preservation

Original hem preservation is the technique that keeps the fade pattern intact. We detach the original hem — carefully, stitch by stitch — shorten the leg from underneath, and then reattach the original hem so the outer fade, wear and stitching are all preserved. From the outside, the jean looks like it came from the factory with the right leg length. Nothing about the fade pattern is altered; only the invisible portion of the hem is changed. This is our default finish on any pair of jeans worn more than six months, and it's the only finish we recommend on selvedge denim. From KSh 1,200 depending on leg length and hem complexity.

How to choose

Here's the rule of thumb I give every client who messages me about hemming.

If your jeans are under six months old and the fade is still uniform, overlock or machine straight is fine. Pick overlock for a modern look, machine straight for a classic look. Both are KSh 1,000 and both are clean, durable finishes.

If your jeans have any fade at the hem — the tell-tale lighter ring just above the original stitch line — original hem preservation is the right call. It's KSh 1,200 and takes the fade complication away entirely. On a pair of jeans you've owned for years, this is the finish that keeps the whole trouser looking consistent.

If your jeans are selvedge — with the visible self-edge running up the outside seam — original hem preservation is not optional. It's the only finish that preserves the character of the denim. We hand-finish the hem to reproduce the original chain-stitch, using a technique that takes about twice as long as a machine hem but preserves the rope at the hem edge.

What about keeping some length?

When we shorten jeans, we usually cut from the top of the hem allowance rather than from the bottom of the leg. This means we're cutting into the fabric just above the original hem, which can be reused or discarded depending on the finish. On original hem preservation, the original hem is detached and reattached — the fabric we remove is the inner portion. This means the visible length of the trouser is fully preserved in the fade and stitching, and only the hidden portion changes.

What about re-hemming a pair already hemmed once?

If a pair of jeans has already been hemmed — by us or by another tailor — the second hem is usually done with less fabric to work with. We can still shorten by 1 to 2 cm cleanly. Shortening by more than 3 cm on an already-hemmed jean is technically possible but you lose the fade and end up with a mismatched hem. We tell clients straight when a pair has been shortened too much already to shorten again properly.

Before you book — do your jeans actually need altering?

Seven checkpoints that tell you if denim is worth altering

Most clients message saying "my jeans don't fit right." That's not enough for me to quote accurately, and it's not enough for you to decide what to ask for. Here's the checklist I use on every pair of denim in the workshop. Run through it with the jeans on. If three or more come back positive, an alteration is worth doing. If one or two, you may be able to live with it.

01

The hem drags on the ground when you walk barefoot

Stand barefoot on a hard floor and look at where the hem ends. If the hem touches or drags on the floor, the leg is too long — and it will wear out quickly against shoes. If the hem sits more than 3 cm above the top of your foot, the leg is too short to shorten but might be too short overall. Standard denim hemming with overlock or machine straight finish is KSh 1,000. Original hem preservation is KSh 1,200. On selvedge denim, hand-finished hemming starts at KSh 1,500.

02

There's more than two fingers of gap at the back of the waist

Put the jeans on and slide your hand into the back of the waistband. If you can fit more than two fingers vertically, the waist is too big and needs taking in. Denim waist adjustments are limited — usually 2 to 4 cm because denim has very little seam allowance compared to dress trousers. Standard waist take-in is KSh 1,500. Because we re-space the belt loops and re-anchor the top button as part of the job, the alteration looks factory-standard after completion.

03

The leg opening is much wider than your shoe

Look down at your foot. If the jean opening extends well past the sides of your shoe — a "boot cut" opening on a slim shoe — the leg is too wide for a modern look. Two options: bottom hem reshaping, which narrows the opening only (KSh 1,000), or full leg slimming, which narrows the whole leg from the knee down (KSh 1,500). On heavy denim, slimming takes longer because we use a walking-foot industrial machine for even feeding.

04

The seat sags even when the waist is correct

Turn sideways to the mirror. If the fabric behind your thighs sags into a loose curve rather than following the natural line of your seat, the yoke is too deep for your body. Yoke adjustments — reshaping the back panel of the jeans — are more complex than waist take-ins. We adjust the yoke and re-shape the back pockets slightly so they sit correctly. From KSh 1,800. This only works on jeans where the seat fabric is still strong; if it's gone thin, the alteration won't hold.

05

The knees feel tight when you sit or bend

Sit down and lean forward. If the fabric strains across your knees — a visible pull line across the front of each knee — the jeans are too narrow at the knee for your body. This can sometimes be eased by loosening the knee darts or by opening the outside seam slightly at the knee area. Easing a tight knee is KSh 900. If the knee is too tight because the jeans were cut with a strong taper at the factory, sometimes the honest answer is that they can't be saved — the fabric simply isn't there.

06

The zip drags or jumps teeth

Zip the fly up and down a few times. If the zipper drags, skips teeth or gets stuck partway, the zip has failed. On denim, a broken zip is not something to live with — it will fail completely within a few months, and if it fails while you're wearing them, you can't zip the fly. We replace with a heavy-duty YKK metal zip matched to the original colour. Same-day often possible if you message before midday. KSh 800.

07

The knees, crotch or seat have thinned or worn through

Hold the fabric up to a light. On jeans worn more than three years, the knees, crotch and seat are the first areas to thin. If the fabric has gone thin but hasn't opened yet, internal reinforcement from KSh 1,200 will add two to three years of wear. If a hole has already opened, we patch with matching denim — from KSh 1,200 for knee reinforcement, KSh 1,200 for crotch reinforcement. See our fabric patching page for the wider repair service.

Run through all seven. If three or more come back positive, send me a photo on WhatsApp — front, back, and one of you wearing them side-on — and I'll tell you honestly which alterations will make the biggest difference. You don't have to fix everything at once. Often the right answer is to fix one thing and live with the rest.

An honest decision — alter, or retire?

When a pair of jeans is worth altering, and when you should just buy new

I turn down denim jobs every month. Not because I can't do them — because the honest answer is that a KSh 1,500 slimming job on a KSh 1,800 pair of market jeans doesn't make sense. Here's the test I use before I quote you. If your jeans fail more than two of these, buy new.

✓

Was the denim good quality to begin with?A KSh 1,800 market pair of jeans has a short remaining life regardless. The fabric thins faster, the seams pull earlier, and every alteration ages the garment faster than it would a KSh 6,000 pair of premium denim. On market denim, we often tell clients honestly that a slimming or waist job won't be worth it — but a simple hem or zip replacement still makes sense. On Italian, Japanese and premium mid-tier denim, almost every alteration is worth it because the fabric has years left.

✓

Is the denim still heavy or has it gone floppy?Good denim has a firm hand feel — you can feel the weight in your hand. Over-washed denim that has lost its body is a warning sign: it will sag at the seat within a year of any alteration, no matter how well we take in the waist. Run the fabric between your thumb and fingers. If it feels supple and heavy, the alteration will hold. If it feels loose and papery, the alteration won't last.

✓

Are the knees and crotch still intact?These are the two areas that always fail first on denim. If either has already thinned or worn through, the alteration may still be worth doing — but we tell you upfront that the repair and the alteration together cost more, and that the fabric there will still be the weakest point. On jeans where the knees are thin and the seat is intact, a knee reinforcement plus hemming is a sensible combined job. On jeans where the crotch has worn through at the V-joint, sometimes the fabric simply isn't strong enough for the alteration to hold.

✓

Will the total cost be under 35% of a new pair?On a KSh 4,500 mid-tier pair, we keep total alteration costs under roughly KSh 1,500 to be worth doing. On a KSh 15,000 pair of Japanese selvedge denim, we can go up to KSh 5,000 and it still makes sense. The percentage stays the same; the numbers scale with the garment. On market denim below KSh 2,000, we usually cap alteration spending at KSh 700 — beyond that, replacing makes more sense.

✓

Have they already been altered before?Once-altered jeans are harder to alter again. If the hem has already been shortened, we can usually shorten once more by 1 to 2 cm cleanly. If the waist has already been taken in, the seam allowance is usually gone and we can't take it in further without opening the original construction. If the jeans have been altered twice already, the honest answer is often that they've reached the end of their alteration life — even if the fabric itself still has years left.

✓

Do they have meaning beyond the price tag?A pair of jeans your father wore, a pair of denim from a specific trip, a pair of workwear with a story — the calculation changes entirely. On sentimental garments, we do the alteration no matter what the numbers say, because the value isn't in the fabric. Send a photo and tell me the story. The quote is built around the meaning, not the price tag. On vintage denim we use different techniques — hand-finishing throughout, chain-stitch hems by hand, matching thread drawn from inside the garment.

You won't get a hard sell when you message me. If the honest answer is "buy new," I'll tell you that straight. Better that than charging you for an alteration that stops looking right in six months. It's the same standard we hold on fabric patching and zipper replacement.

A short note from the bench

Why selvedge denim is the last thing a tailor should rush

Most people who own selvedge denim know exactly what they're buying. They know it's woven on a shuttle loom, that the weft is a single continuous thread, and that the self-edge running up the outside seam is the signature of genuine selvedge weaving. They also know that a hem done wrong erases the entire point of owning selvedge denim — because the self-edge gets cut off, or the chain-stitch gets replaced with a straight stitch, or the fabric gets marked by a machine that shouldn't be anywhere near it. I want to explain what a proper selvedge hem actually involves, because most Nairobi tailors don't do it, and clients who own selvedge denim need to know what to look for.

The first thing is the self-edge itself. On a selvedge jean, the outside leg seam has a finished edge — usually with a coloured thread woven into it (the "selvedge ID"). That edge is visible when you cuff the hem. If the hemming job cuts the trouser leg at the standard point and re-hems it, the self-edge is preserved because it runs the whole length of the leg. It's the hem that has to preserve the edge, not the leg length. So we cut from the top of the hem, not the bottom, and leave the self-edge untouched.

The second thing is the chain-stitch. Original selvedge denim hems are chain-stitched — a specific stitch pattern that produces a slight rope at the hem edge. Over time, the rope creates a distinctive fade ring, and collectors recognise the fade ring as the mark of a properly-finished hem. Reproducing this chain-stitch on a shortened hem requires a specific machine or careful hand-work. On a workshop bench, I do these hems by hand, one stitch at a time. It takes about twice as long as a machine hem. It also produces a hem that a denim collector would accept as correctly finished.

The third thing is the roping effect. Because the chain-stitch pulls the fabric slightly as it's stitched, the hem edge has a natural rope — it doesn't sit flat like a machine straight hem. That rope is what produces the fade ring after the first twenty or thirty washes. If a hem is done with a flat stitch instead of a chain-stitch, the fade ring never forms and the hem looks machine-made, not selvedge. On a pair of KSh 15,000 selvedge denim, that difference is worth hundreds of shillings of extra work.

The fourth thing is what we don't do. We don't put selvedge denim through the standard sewing machine. We don't use polyester thread on denim. We don't press the hem flat with a hot iron. Each of those things — machine-stitching heavy denim, polyester thread on cotton denim, hot pressing — leaves a permanent signature that an experienced eye picks up immediately. A hem done properly on selvedge denim is worth the extra time. A hem done badly is a permanent reminder on an expensive pair of jeans.

If you own selvedge denim and you're not sure whether the tailor you're considering uses the right technique — send me a photo of your jeans and I'll tell you honestly what a proper selvedge hem on your pair would involve. Free quote, no obligation. And if the honest answer is that you should send them to a specialist in Tokyo or Kyoto, I'll tell you that too. Nairobi has very few tailors who work with selvedge denim properly. If you're reading this page, you've found one of them.

Make denim last between alterations

Eight habits that keep jeans out of the alteration pile

Denim is the most forgiving fabric in a wardrobe — but only if you treat it right. Here are the eight habits I teach every client when they collect an altered pair of jeans. The same ones I follow with my own.

1. Wash less, air more

Denim doesn't need washing after every wear. The CEO of Levi's famously said he never machine-washes his jeans — and he's right. Every wash strips indigo, softens the fabric, and shortens the life of the stitching. If your jeans don't smell and aren't visibly soiled, hang them to air for a few hours. Two to three wears between washes is normal. Five or six is fine if you rotate with other pairs. Less washing means longer fade, better fit retention, and fewer alterations.

2. Wash cold, wash inside out

When you do wash, cold water only. Hot water relaxes the denim fibres and shrinks the fabric — usually by half a size on the first hot wash and more after repeated exposure. Turn the jeans inside out before washing so the fade on the outside surface is protected from friction. Skip the fabric softener entirely — it coats the fibres and stops them absorbing indigo the way they should. Wash with like colours.

3. Never tumble dry

Tumble drying is the fastest way to shrink a pair of jeans, distort the waistband, and weaken every stitched seam. It also softens the fabric in ways that make future alterations harder. Air dry instead — hang on a line, a rack, or over the back of a chair. If you're in a hurry, point a fan at them. The one exception: raw denim, which should be hung to dry indoors to avoid direct sunlight (which fades the fabric unevenly).

4. Spot clean small marks

A small grease spot or a food spill doesn't require a full wash. Blot with a damp cloth (cold water), let it dry, and move on. Full washes should be reserved for jeans that genuinely need them. If you must wash a small area, wash only that area — a spot clean is much kinder to the denim than a full wash cycle. This habit alone will extend your jeans' life by years.

5. Rotate between two or three pairs

Denim needs 24 hours to recover its shape between wears. Wear the same pair every day and the knees stay bagged, the seat stays stretched, and the waistband loses its stiffness. If you own three pairs and rotate them, each one gets worn a third as often — which roughly triples its life. The clients who spend the least on jeans alterations are the ones who rotate their denim.

6. Don't over-stuff the pockets

Front pockets and back pockets have a weight limit. A wallet, phone, keys and coins add up — and repeated stress on the pocket bag pulls the pocket corner loose and stretches the front pocket opening. If you carry a lot daily, use a small bag instead. This is one of the main reasons jeans tear at the pocket corner within two years, and it's entirely preventable.

7. Fix small issues early

A loose thread at the hem, a small pull at the pocket corner, a thin patch at the knee — all of these are KSh 300 to KSh 600 fixes when caught early. Left alone, they become KSh 1,200+ repairs because the surrounding fabric fails alongside the original damage. Message us the moment you notice something small. Most small denim repairs are same-day if you get to us before midday.

8. Store folded, not on a hanger

Hanging jeans by the waistband puts the whole weight of the trouser on two belt loops — which stretches them and eventually pulls them loose. Fold the jeans flat in a drawer or on a shelf. Stack them loosely rather than tightly — creases at the knees from tight stacking become permanent over time. If you have a raw denim pair you're not wearing for months, fold them and store with a cedar block to deter moths (moths eat wool, not cotton, but cedar also keeps the drawer fresh).

Follow those eight habits and your denim will hold its fit for years. And when it does eventually need an alteration, we're one WhatsApp message away. See our fabric patching page if the care has slipped and repair is needed.
