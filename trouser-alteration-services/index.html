The most-often-altered garment in menswear

Why a trouser alteration is mostly a fit problem — not a length problem

Almost everyone who walks through our door with a trouser problem says the same thing: "they're too long." Some of them are right. But nine out of ten times, the length is only the last of three or four things wrong with the fit — and if we shorten the hem without fixing the rest, the trouser ends up looking like a shorter version of the same bad fit. Here's what we actually look at when a pair of trousers lands on the bench.

The seat and hip — the fit you feel before you see it

The seat is the section of the trouser between the waistband and the crotch, and it's where most bad fits come from. If the seat is too full, the trouser bags around the back of your thighs and the back of the trouser leg doesn't fall straight. If the seat is too tight, the fabric pulls across your pockets and the front crease warps. Neither of those problems is visible from the front, but both are obvious from behind. Seat and hip adjustment is a KSh 1,500 job, and it's the alteration that changes how the trouser actually behaves on your body — not just how it looks in the mirror. If you've ever had trousers "fit fine" but still feel wrong, this is almost always the reason.

The rise and crotch V-joint — the two settings that decide comfort

The rise is the vertical distance between the waistband and the crotch seam. If the rise is too short, the waistband digs into your stomach when you sit and the crotch line rides high. If it's too long, the crotch sits low and the whole trouser sags. On well-cut trousers, the rise is set at the factory for a specific body and cannot be fixed by sliding the waistband up or down. The crotch itself has a V-joint — a small section of stitching where four panels of fabric meet — and this is where most tailors refuse to work because it's the hardest point on a trouser to alter cleanly. We do both. Rise adjustment and V-joint work are separate specialist services, priced from KSh 1,800.

The leg taper — where slim becomes silhouette

Leg taper is the alteration that decides whether the trouser reads as classic, slim or skinny. The thigh is set at the widest point of the leg, and from there the trouser narrows toward the hem. If the taper is too aggressive, the trouser clings to the calf and the front crease disappears. If the taper is too loose, the trouser looks boxy at the ankle and long over the shoe. Most modern trousers come off the rack with a taper that suits one body type and looks wrong on every other. Leg taper is KSh 1,500 and is done from both side seams and the inner leg seam — cutting from the side only gives a lopsided taper that reads wrong from the back.

The waistband, the pocket and the belt loops

Every alteration on the waist of a trouser has to think about three things: the waistband itself, the two front pockets, and the belt loops at the sides and back. Take in the waist without touching the pockets, and the pockets pull open — which you can see from ten metres away. Take in the waist without re-spacing the belt loops, and the belt sits unevenly around the trousers. Same for the back pocket on chinos and dress trousers — the back pocket line is a visible design element, and if the seat is altered without re-setting the pocket line, the pockets sit visibly off-centre. Waist adjustment on a trouser is KSh 1,500 — but it takes longer than the hem does, because all three of those details have to be re-set properly.

The hem — the last ten percent of the job

The hem gets all the attention because it's the alteration everyone asks for. But the hem is the last ten percent of a good trouser fit, not the first ninety. There are four standard bottom finishes — machine straight, turn-up (cuff), overlock and blind hem — and each one belongs with a specific trouser. Machine straight is for dress trousers worn with a jacket. Turn-up is for heavier trousers like flannel or tweed, and it puts a little weight at the hem so the trouser hangs better. Overlock is the casual finish for chinos and lighter cottons. Blind hem is for suit trousers where the stitching has to be invisible from the outside. Choosing the wrong one makes the trouser look off at the ankle even if the length is perfect.

Why this matters to your wallet

A KSh 1,000 hem on a pair of trousers that fit perfectly everywhere else is money well spent. The same KSh 1,000 on trousers with a bad seat, a low rise and a sagging crotch is money wasted, because the trouser will still look wrong at the waist and the back — just shorter. The clients who get the most out of trouser alterations are the ones who tell us what's wrong, not just what they think needs fixing. Send us photos of the front, the back and the side of the trouser while you're wearing it, and we'll tell you honestly which alterations will fix the actual problem. That's the same standard we hold on jeans alterations, chino alterations and trouser rise adjustment.

Not every trouser is the same job

Three trouser tiers — three completely different alterations

A pair of KSh 1,500 market chinos and a pair of KSh 25,000 wool suit trousers are not the same alteration. One is a casual cotton pant that can be worked on with a standard machine stitch. The other is a precision-cut wool trouser where every seam affects how the trouser falls from the knee to the ankle. Treating them the same way is how a good trouser gets ruined. Here's how we categorise trousers before deciding which technique to use.

Everyday tier

Chinos, casual cotton and market trousers

Mass-produced chinos, cotton casual trousers and market trousers. Fabric weight 200–320 gsm, usually cotton twill or a cotton-poly blend. Bottom finish is usually a simple machine straight hem or overlock. Waistband is a basic 3 cm band. Belt loops are simple straight loops. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 1,500 and KSh 5,000. The everyday trouser.

What we do differentlyOn these we work pragmatically — the goal is to make the trouser fit, not to restore structure that wasn't there. Standard machine stitch on plain cotton seams, overlock finish at the hem, medium heat press with a muslin cloth. Where the rise is too low or the seat too full, we fix it at a sensible price point, but we tell the client upfront that a KSh 1,500 chino is worth a KSh 1,000 alteration, not a KSh 3,000 one.

Mid tier

Jeans, better chinos and poly-viscose dress trousers

Better-crafted casual and dress trousers — selvedge and heavyweight denim, better chinos from mid-tier retail, poly-viscose dress trousers. Fabric weight 280–420 gsm. Chain-stitched hems on jeans, real turn-ups on flannel or tweed. Pockets with proper felling. Belt loops re-inforced. Common in Nairobi retail between KSh 5,000 and KSh 15,000.

What we do differentlyOn this tier we treat the trouser like what it is — a piece of clothing worth altering carefully. Chain-stitch hems on jeans are preserved or re-done with a chain-stitch machine rather than a lock-stitch. Turn-ups on heavier fabrics are re-set at the same width. Waist take-in preserves the front pocket openings and re-spaces the belt loops. Seat adjustments re-set the back pocket line. Every step is measured, not estimated.

Premium tier

Wool suiting, bespoke and high-end dress trousers

Suit trousers and dress trousers from high-end makers — pure wool, wool-silk, high-twist wool, sometimes with a canvas front and hand-finished details. Fabric weight 260–400 gsm. Waistband may be curtained or partially lined. Pockets are jetted, sometimes hand-finished. Rise, crotch V-joint and seat are cut to a specific body. Suit trousers are sold as part of a set and cannot be replaced.

What we do differentlyOn this tier every alteration is done by hand where the original was hand-finished. Waist and seat adjustments preserve the curtain and the jetting. Crotch V-joint work is done by hand, checking the four-panel match three times before closing the seam. Hems are blind-stitched with matching thread. Where a hem would need a press line that would destroy the drape, we let the trouser hang for 24 hours before the final check — because wool relaxes after the alteration, and a trouser measured straight off the bench is not the trouser you get back.

If you're not sure which tier your trouser falls into, send a close-up photo of the waistband, the inside hem and the pocket construction on WhatsApp. We'll tell you from the construction how the alteration should be handled.

English

"Will altering my trousers damage the fabric?"

No — as long as the alteration is done with the fabric in mind. The three most common ways a trouser alteration damages the fabric are: pressing too hot (leaves a permanent shine on wool and poly-viscose), cutting too close to the edge of the seam allowance (leaves no room for a future alteration), and stitching through a fused interlining (breaks the bond and creates a bubble). We press every trouser through a muslin cloth at medium heat, we keep 1.5 cm of seam allowance intact where possible, and on fused trousers we avoid any stitch line that would break the bond. If a job genuinely can't be done without risking damage, we tell you upfront rather than take the money.

A short note from the bench

The three things that decide how a trouser fits — and none of them is the hem

I have been altering trousers in Nairobi for fourteen years. In that time I've shortened about six thousand pairs. And here's the honest thing I tell every client who walks in convinced they need the hem redone: the hem is the last ten percent of the job. The first ninety percent is the seat, the rise and the V-joint — and almost nobody asks about those because you can't see them in a mirror.

Start with the seat. The seat is the section between the waistband and the crotch, and it's where most trouser fits fail. A seat that's too full bags at the back of the thigh. A seat that's too tight pulls the fabric across the pockets — and you can see that pull from the other side of the room, even though nobody can name why the trouser looks wrong. Nine out of ten trousers I see at the bench have a seat problem the client didn't know was there. When I fix the seat, the trouser suddenly reads correctly, and the client usually says "how did you do that?" — the answer is: I didn't do much. I just took in the seat by two centimetres at the back rise and re-set the pocket line. The rest of the trouser was fine all along.

Then the rise. Rise is the vertical distance between the waistband and the crotch seam. If the rise is too short, the waistband digs into your stomach every time you sit down — you get red marks at the navel. If the rise is too long, the crotch hangs low and the trouser sags. Both of those problems feel like "the trousers are uncomfortable" to the wearer, and both of them get blamed on the waistband or the fabric. Rise is not something you can fix by sliding the waistband up or down. It's cut at the factory for a specific body, and the only way to change it is to open the waistband, re-cut the vertical distance, and re-set the band. That's a KSh 1,800 job, and it's the alteration that turns an uncomfortable trouser into one you actually reach for in the morning.

And the V-joint. The V-joint is the point at the crotch where four panels of fabric meet — two front, two back. When the match is off by even half a centimetre, every step you take pulls the fabric in a slightly different direction, and the trouser fights you. That's what causes the rubbing between the legs, the wrinkles that radiate outward when you walk, and the feeling that the trouser is trying to go somewhere without you. Very few Nairobi tailors will touch the V-joint. It's a hard job. You open the crotch, re-cut the four panels, and re-match them — then you check the match three times before you close the seam, because once you've closed it you can't easily open it again without damaging the fabric. We do it. It starts at KSh 2,000. It takes three to five days. It's worth every shilling.

Now the hem — the part everyone focuses on. There are four standard bottom finishes: machine straight, turn-up, overlock and blind hem. Machine straight is for dress trousers worn with a jacket. Turn-up is for heavier fabrics like flannel or tweed, and it puts weight at the hem so the trouser hangs straighter. Overlock is the casual finish for chinos. Blind hem is for suit trousers where the stitching has to be invisible from outside. Choosing the wrong finish makes the trouser look off at the ankle even if the length is perfect. And on jeans — where the original hem is chain-stitched, not lock-stitched — we re-hem on a chain-stitch machine so the rope-like look survives. A hem that loses the chain-stitch look is instantly recognisable as an alteration.

Fourteen years has taught me that clients come in with the wrong question. "Can you shorten these?" is almost never the right question. The right question is "can you make these fit?" — and fitting a trouser means working on the seat, the rise and the V-joint, in that order, before you ever touch the hem. When we work in that order, the hem is a five-minute job. When we work in the wrong order — hem first, everything else later — we end up re-doing the hem because the trouser needs to sit differently after the seat is fixed. Fit the trouser from the waist down. Leave the hem for last. That's the whole craft, in one sentence.

Will altering my trousers damage the fabric?
No, as long as the alteration is done with the fabric in mind. Make your trousers last between alterations

Eight habits that keep trousers out of the workshop

Trousers take more wear than any other garment in a Nairobi wardrobe. You wear them every day, they rub against the seat of a chair for eight hours a day, they take the friction of walking, and they're the first garment that shows age at the knee, the crotch and the hem. A well-cared-for pair of trousers lasts five to eight years — and most of what wears them out early is preventable. Here are the eight habits we teach every client when they collect altered trousers.

1. Hang trousers from the hem, not the waistband

A trouser hanger that grips the waistband is the standard solution, but on heavy trousers — wool, flannel, denim — the weight of the trouser pulls the waistband out of shape over time. On those, use a hanger that grips at the hem and lets the trouser hang upside down — the weight pulls the crease out, not the waistband. On lighter trousers, a standard waistband-clip hanger is fine, but hang from the inside of the waistband where the curtain protects the fabric, not from the outside where the clip can leave marks. A wooden trouser hanger with a felt lining is the best option and costs about the price of a coffee.

2. Brush the seat and knee after every wear

The seat and the knee are the two friction points on any trouser. A soft clothes brush, used on the seat and the knee after every wear, keeps dust and dirt from working into the weave. This matters most on wool suiting — embedded dust dulls the colour over a year. On denim and cotton chinos, brushing keeps the fabric from developing a shine at the seat where you sit. Two minutes per trouser, after every wear. The clients with the longest-lasting trousers are the ones who do this.

3. Don't sit on rough surfaces — the seat wears through

The seat of a trouser wears through from the friction of sitting. Wooden chairs, rough concrete ledges, and the woven plastic seats of a matatu are all abrasive to wool and cotton. If you can help it, avoid sitting directly on these surfaces for long periods — especially if the trouser is wool. On denim, the same rule applies but denim is more forgiving. If you must sit on a rough surface regularly, keep a spare pair of trousers for those occasions and save your good trousers for gentler environments. It sounds precious but it's the difference between replacing trousers every two years and every six.

4. Air trousers between wears — don't wash them every time

Trousers do not need to be washed after every wear. Wool trousers in particular should be dry cleaned once every five to eight wears at most. Denim should be washed as rarely as you can stand — every ten wears or more. Cotton chinos can go three to four wears between washes. Between washes, hang the trousers on a hanger in a well-ventilated spot for a few hours and let them air out. The fabric breathes, odours evaporate, and the trouser settles back into shape. This one habit roughly doubles the life of a wool trouser.

5. Dry clean wool trousers sparingly — not after every wear

Dry cleaning is hard on wool. Every dry clean strips a little more of the natural lanolin out of the fibre, and after four or five cleans the wool starts to feel drier and to develop a nap. On suit trousers worn regularly, one dry clean per season is usually enough — spot clean obvious marks between. On dress trousers worn less often, one dry clean a year is plenty. Store the trouser hanging in a breathable garment bag between wears, with a cedar block or lavender sachet in the pocket to keep moths away.

6. Steam the crease, don't press it flat

The front crease on a dress trouser is a design feature, but pressing it flat every time you wear the trouser flattens the wool and eventually creates a permanent line. Steam the crease instead — with a steamer or an iron held just above the fabric — so the wool relaxes and the crease falls back into place on its own. On wool-blend and pure wool trousers, this alone can extend the life of the crease for years. On cotton chinos, the crease can be pressed but should still be done through a muslin cloth at medium heat, never directly hot.

7. Loosen the waistband before hanging

When trousers are hung with the waistband still tight — especially if they have a hook-bar closure that's under tension — the waistband fabric is stressed permanently. Over months, the waistband goes out of shape and the hook-bar pulls through the fabric. Always loosen the waistband and the belt before hanging the trouser. Same rule applies to the fly zip: open the zip before hanging, so the fly doesn't press a crease into the fabric. Small habit, big difference over a year.

8. Fix small issues before they grow

A loose hem stitch is a KSh 1,000 fix today. Left for three months, the hem fabric frays and the hem has to be re-cut at KSh 1,500+. A hook-bar starting to loosen is a KSh 300 fix today. Left alone, the hook-bar tears through the waistband and the band has to be replaced at KSh 1,800+. A small hole at the back pocket corner is a KSh 800 re-set today. Left alone, the hole grows and the back pocket has to be removed and re-cut at KSh 1,500+. The clients who spend the least on trouser maintenance are the ones who message us the first time they notice something small. Small fixes early always cost less, and they preserve more of the original trouser.

Follow those eight habits and your trousers will hold their shape, their colour and their crease for years. And when they do eventually need work — the hem re-set, the seat taken in, the V-joint re-cut — we're one WhatsApp message away.
